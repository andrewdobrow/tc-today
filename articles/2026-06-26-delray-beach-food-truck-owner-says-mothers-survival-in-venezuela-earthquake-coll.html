Delray Beach food truck owner says mother survived Venezuela earthquake building collapse

Josney Peroza, owner of Ukelele Foods, a Venezuelan food truck in Delray Beach, describes his business as more than just a place to eat. "This is a cultural Venezuelan experience that we share with the community. We call it Venezuela without a passport," he said. That connection to his homeland became deeply personal when two earthquakes measuring 7.2 and 7.5 magnitudes struck Venezuela seconds apart. Peroza first learned of the disaster through news alerts, but the reality hit hard when he saw video footage of the building where his mother lived. Hours later, his brother confirmed she had been inside when the structure collapsed around 3 a.m.

Communication in Venezuela became nearly impossible as power failed and connections were cut. Peroza spent anxious hours not knowing if his mother was alive, relying on prayer to maintain peace. After about eight hours, the call finally came confirming she had survived. His relatives and neighbors had worked with their bare hands to free her from the rubble. "The Venezuelan culture is well known because of the service for the people, and especially in this kind of situation," Peroza said. His mother suffered a broken hip requiring surgery and lost her home in the collapse in the coastal state of La Guaira.

The disaster revealed a wider humanitarian crisis affecting the entire region. Peroza described a population struggling without power, water, or shelter. Officials confirm nearly 200 people are dead and at least 1,000 others are injured. Most buildings are made of concrete and metal, making rescue efforts extremely difficult with limited tools. "The country doesn't have any structure for this kind of situation," Peroza said. "We need international help immediately." The United States announced it would send rescue crews to assist. Peroza emphasized the urgency of the situation, noting that survivors face unclear prospects for food, work, and shelter in the coming days.

Despite the disaster affecting his family, Peroza believes he can help more effectively from Florida than by traveling to Venezuela. "We don't have any plans to go down there. I think we are more helpful from here," he explained, noting that his mother and other survivors will need housing and resources that require commercial activity and employment opportunities. He plans to continue operating his food truck while raising awareness about Venezuela's needs. Peroza called his mother's rescue "absolutely a miracle" and said it proves more lives can still be saved with international response. "There are families in Venezuela waiting for rescue, medical attention, information, and hope," he said. "Right now, Venezuela needs urgent humanitarian help."
