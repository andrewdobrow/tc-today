Delray Beach food truck owner calls mother's Venezuela earthquake survival a miracle

Josney Peroza, owner of Ukelele Foods Venezuelan food truck in Delray Beach, learned of a devastating disaster when news alerts reported two powerful earthquakes striking his home country seconds apart. The quakes measured 7.2 and 7.5 magnitudes and struck the coastal state of La Guaira. When initial images arrived, Peroza saw a video showing the building where his mother lived had collapsed. Hours later, his brother confirmed she had been inside when it fell. For the next eight hours, Peroza could not reach his mother as communication throughout Venezuela shut down due to widespread power outages and severed connections. Around 3 a.m., the family finally received a call confirming his mother was alive but trapped under the rubble. Relatives and neighbors worked with their bare hands to free her from the debris.

Despite her rescue, Peroza's mother faced serious injuries and loss. She sustained a broken hip requiring surgery and lost her home entirely in the collapse. Peroza described the broader humanitarian catastrophe unfolding across the region, where officials confirmed nearly 200 deaths and at least 1,000 injured. The country lacked any infrastructure to handle a disaster of this magnitude, he said. Most buildings in Venezuela are constructed from concrete and metal, making rescue efforts extremely difficult without proper equipment. Residents had been forced to use prayer and their bare hands as their main tools for helping people still trapped in the wreckage.

Peroza emphasized the urgent need for immediate international assistance. He noted that Venezuela had no power, no water, and no shelter for displaced survivors. He described widespread uncertainty about food, housing, and employment in the coming days. The United States announced it would send rescue crews to help. Peroza said he believed he could better assist his mother and his country by remaining in Florida rather than traveling to the disaster zone. From there, he could work toward rebuilding her life and support the broader relief efforts while continuing to operate his food truck.

Peroza described his business as more than a place to eat, calling it "a cultural Venezuelan experience" and "Venezuela without a passport." He planned to continue serving customers while sharing his country's story and helping survivors. He viewed his mother's survival as proof that more lives could still be saved with rapid international response. For those still waiting for word from loved ones in Venezuela, Peroza offered a message of faith and resilience, emphasizing the importance of trusting in the Lord during the crisis.
