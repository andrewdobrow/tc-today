St. Lucie man with pancreatic cancer approved for experimental treatment

Russell Reed spent 62 days waiting to learn whether he would be approved to receive daraxonrasib, an experimental drug his oncologist believed could help after traditional treatments were no longer effective. The 64-year-old St. Lucie man said chemotherapy was no longer going to help him at this point. Daraxonrasib, also known as RMC-6236, is an investigational once-daily oral pill developed by Revolution Medicines. The drug targets mutated KRAS proteins, which drive more than 90% of pancreatic cancers. While the treatment is not yet FDA-approved, early clinical trial results have generated optimism among some patients and physicians.

According to Reed's family, his oncologist submitted a request through the FDA's Expanded Access Program, which can allow patients with serious or life-threatening illnesses to receive treatments outside of clinical trials when other options are limited. While waiting for an answer, Reed and his wife Marnie focused on making the most of their time together. For Marnie, the fight was never just about accessing a drug. It was about more family trips, more memories and more moments together. She said the idea of sitting and waiting for the phone to ring was not okay with her, and she did not want to miss a minute with her husband.

Reed said research and clinical trial results gave him hope that daraxonrasib could help slow the progression of his disease. He said there is a lot of anecdotal evidence and a lot of evidence from the clinical trials that show that this is the miracle drug for pancreatic cancer. It is not a cure, but it will give him time. Marnie emphasized the importance of being present during this difficult period, saying that her husband is still here and she does not want to miss anything by not paying attention.

Just two days after sharing their story with WPTV, Reed learned he had been approved to receive the treatment. The drug is now in his hands, and his family says he is already feeling better and optimistic about what comes next. While daraxonrasib is not considered a cure for pancreatic cancer, the Reeds say the approval represents something they have been fighting for all along: more time together. The past two months have been filled with uncertainty for Reed, but the approval has brought new hope to him and his family as they continue their journey.
