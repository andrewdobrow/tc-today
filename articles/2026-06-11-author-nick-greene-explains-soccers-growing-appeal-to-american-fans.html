Author explains soccer's growing appeal to American fans in new book

Nick Greene, author of "How to Watch Soccer Like a Genius," believes Americans are embracing soccer far more than many people recognize. During a recent interview with WPTV's Jon Shainman, Greene shared his passion for the sport and discussed his book's unique approach to understanding the game. The book examines soccer through the perspectives of agronomists, architects, computer scientists, and other experts, offering readers fresh insights into why the sport captivates billions of fans worldwide. Greene emphasized soccer's fundamental appeal, saying "you have this game that is the simplest game, you just need a ball and you don't even need grass."

Several key moments in the 1990s transformed America's relationship with soccer. The 1994 World Cup, which took place in the United States, marked a turning point for the sport domestically. The launch of Major League Soccer that same decade provided American fans with a professional league to follow regularly. The 1999 U.S. Women's World Cup victory further energized public interest and demonstrated the sport's potential to capture American attention. These three milestones collectively contributed to soccer's sustained growth in the country over the past several decades.

The sport has undergone a dramatic evolution since its early days. Greene explained that soccer's transformation reflects a shift from individual-focused play to sophisticated team strategies. The first modern players, who emerged in England during the 1860s and 1870s, were aristocratic gentlemen who approached the game differently than today's professionals. One early player famously characterized the sport as something played "purely for my own pleasure," highlighting how individualistic the game once was. Modern soccer, by contrast, emphasizes complex tactical coordination and collective team objectives.

Looking toward the future, Greene contemplates soccer's next chapter in America. He questions whether the sport will maintain its current trajectory of steady growth or whether it will ascend to become a truly dominant spectacle in American culture, reaching what he calls "the stratosphere." Greene's expert-driven approach in his book aims to deepen readers' appreciation for soccer's tactical nuances and strategic elements. As the sport continues gaining momentum across the country, understanding these complexities becomes increasingly valuable for American audiences seeking to engage more fully with the game.
