Treasure Coast teens join national fentanyl summit in Orlando as DEA administrator addresses drug crisis

Teens from anti-drug coalitions across the country joined grieving parents and federal law enforcement at the Fentanyl Free America Summit in Orlando this week. The summit drew students who traveled from around the country to learn about the resurgence of fentanyl so they can return home with a message about one of the country's deadliest drug threats. Hannah Kahn, 18, attended the summit alongside other teen advocates. Jasmine Figueroa, 19, said she has a relative who is addicted to methamphetamine and that seeing how that has destroyed his life encouraged her to prevent that from happening to anybody else.

The students are members of their community anti-drug coalitions who came with questions and answers about how fentanyl spreads and why young people may not recognize the danger. Kahn said people are not seeking out fentanyl necessarily because it is just laced within other drugs. The teens had pointed advice for adults on how to talk to young people about drugs. Figueroa said it is not a taboo topic and adults should tell them what cocaine is because they are going to get educated one way or another. She said adults might as well be the first ones to educate young people and tell them the truth about how things are.

This year's summit welcomed a powerful new partner, the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration. DEA Administrator Terrance Cole spent time with victim families and said the poison is being manufactured and distributed out of Mexico by the Mexican cartels. Cole said the president has designated Mexican cartels as foreign terrorists, which opens different avenues for the DEA to target them. For parents who have lost children, the summit is deeply personal. Mari Rodriguez's daughter, Imam, died of fentanyl poisoning in 2023. Rodriguez said her daughter made the mistake of buying a Percocet pill that had fentanyl in it and it took her life. Rodriguez said they are killing our children, our youth, and our future. She added that the U.S. should have been working on this since 2009, when the first fentanyl was found on our streets, because these monsters are not stopping.

For the teens at the summit, prevention comes down to open conversation and making Narcan as common as any household product. Kahn said a lot of people have that stereotype that Narcan is only for responders and EMS but people have to think about it as a household product, just like Benadryl. While deaths from the synthetic opioid are down, tracking how widespread fentanyl has become is nearly impossible in part because of Narcan, an over-the-counter nasal spray that is increasing survival rates.
