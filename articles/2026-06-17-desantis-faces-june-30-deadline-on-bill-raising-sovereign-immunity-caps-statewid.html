DeSantis vetoes sovereign immunity bill that raised caps on government negligence payouts

Governor Ron DeSantis rejected legislation that would have increased damage caps for people harmed by government negligence for the first time in 16 years. The bill passed with strong bipartisan support in March before DeSantis vetoed it, saying that raising the caps would encourage more frivolous lawsuits. "When you raise the cap, you are going to generate more unmeritorious lawsuits," he said. The measure would have increased sovereign immunity caps from $200,000 per person and $300,000 per incident to $350,000 per person and $500,000 per incident, effective October 1.

HB 145, sponsored by Sarasota Republican Rep. Fiona McFarland with a Senate companion bill from Lake Mary Republican Sen. Jason Brodeur, faced fierce opposition from more than 240 lobbyists representing counties, cities and their agencies, including public hospitals. These government entities worried about budget shortfalls under a proposed property tax amendment. The bill would have allowed local governments to voluntarily settle claims exceeding the new caps without requiring legislative approval. The original proposal included higher caps of $500,000 per person and $1 million per incident, which were reduced in a last-minute compromise. Sovereign immunity is a centuries-old legal standard that shields state and local governments from settling lawsuits without their consent. Currently, victims seeking compensation above the caps must obtain approval through special claims bills passed by the Legislature.

McFarland has argued that the current system creates unequal protection under the law. She contends that harmed individuals face an "incredibly arduous and difficult process" with vastly different outcomes depending on whether the responsible party is a public or private entity. "If you or I or one of our loved ones is hit by a truck, we have to pay attention to what's on the side of that truck," she said. "Depending on whether there's a corporate logo or a government seal, we have different access to monetary wholeness." State lawmakers consider roughly a dozen claims bills annually benefiting people injured through government carelessness or wrongly incarcerated exonerees. This year, just over one-third of 14 such measures passed both chambers, including cases involving a man paralyzed in a police van, a motorist injured in a crash with a state tractor, and a young girl hurt due to Department of Children and Families negligence.

DeSantis defended his veto by noting the current system works because some claims bills clear the Legislature yearly. "I signed a number of claims bills this year," he said. "People have gotten millions of dollars with that." However, the Florida Justice Association criticized the veto as a gift to insurance companies and a disservice to Florida families. The group noted that under the current system, victims often wait years or over a decade for compensation. McFarland and Brodeur, both seeking reelection, have not indicated whether they will refile the legislation for the 2027 session.
