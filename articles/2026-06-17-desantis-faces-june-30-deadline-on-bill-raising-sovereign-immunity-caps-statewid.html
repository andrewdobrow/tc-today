DeSantis vetoes sovereign immunity bill that would have raised liability caps for government negligence

Governor Ron DeSantis vetoed legislation that would have increased damage caps in lawsuits against government entities for the first time in 16 years. The measure passed the Legislature with overwhelming bipartisan support in March but faced the governor's veto this week. The bill would have raised caps on damages payable in court settlements for people harmed by government negligence from $200,000 per person and $300,000 per incident to $350,000 per person and $500,000 per incident. The current caps were last set in 2010.

DeSantis said raising the caps would generate more unmeritorious lawsuits and undermine efforts to discourage frivolous claims. "When you raise the cap, you are going to generate more unmeritorious lawsuits," he said. The bill, sponsored by Sarasota Republican Representative Fiona McFarland with a companion measure from Lake Mary Republican Senator Jason Brodeur, drew opposition from more than 240 registered lobbyists representing counties, cities, and local agencies facing budget concerns. The measure would have allowed local governments to voluntarily settle claims exceeding the caps without seeking legislative approval. It was scheduled to take effect October 1 if signed.

Under Florida's sovereign immunity statutes, people harmed by government negligence must seek legislative passage of special claims bills to receive compensation above the damage caps. Lawmakers consider roughly a dozen such claims bills annually with varying success rates. This year, just over one-third of 14 claims bills passed both chambers, including cases involving a man paralyzed in a police van, a motorist injured in a crash with a state tractor, and a young girl harmed due to Department of Children and Families negligence despite prior warning signs. McFarland argued the current system creates unequal protection. "If you or I or one of our loved ones is hit by a truck, we have to pay attention to what's on the side of that truck," she said. "Depending on whether there's a corporate logo or a government seal, we have different access to monetary wholeness."

DeSantis countered that the current system works effectively. "I signed a number of claims bills this year," he said. "People have gotten millions of dollars with that." The Florida Justice Association criticized the veto, calling it a gift to insurance companies and a disservice to families seeking accountability for government negligence. The group noted that victims often wait years or more than a decade for compensation under the current system. It remains unclear whether McFarland or Brodeur will refile the legislation in 2027, when a new governor will hold veto power.
