Immigration Detainees from Alligator Alcatraz Scattered Across U.S. as Legal Battles Continue

The closure of the controversial Alligator Alcatraz detention facility in the Florida Everglades has created a new crisis for immigrants who were not deported before the facility shut down. According to immigration attorney Richard Hujber, the detainees have been transferred to facilities across the country, including Louisiana, Texas, New Mexico, and as far away as Tacoma, Washington. These dispersals have made it significantly harder for detainees and their legal representatives to navigate the immigration system effectively. Hujber explained that "there's different laws in different circuit courts in different parts of the country," meaning that strategies that work in one jurisdiction may not succeed in another.

The geographical separation of detainees from their legal representatives has created serious obstacles in their cases. Many detainees now face impossible choices about whether to continue fighting for residency or asylum status or to give up and return to their home countries voluntarily. With bond becoming unattainable for some due to the complications caused by transfers, detainees are caught in a difficult position. As Hujber put it, many are asking themselves whether they should "continue to fight this case, because if I can't even get a bond, what's the chance I'm going to get a green card or asylum?" The uncertainty has left families anxious about their loved ones' futures.

For some, however, the facility's closure brought relief. Anna Maria Hernandez's cousin was released just before Alligator Alcatraz permanently closed its doors. He is now under an order of supervision, which permits him to work, drive, and live in the United States. Hernandez has become an advocate for other families still facing detention, urging the government to allow others the same opportunity her cousin received. She reflected on the emotional toll of her family's experience, saying "we lived every day wondering if our family is safe." Her cousin's release represents the kind of outcome that other detainees and their families are hoping to achieve.

Despite the closure of Alligator Alcatraz, the fight for many detainees is far from over. The Baker County facility in North Florida remains operational, and uncertainty continues to cloud the futures of former detainees scattered across the country. Governor Ron DeSantis reported that approximately 21,000 people were deported from Alligator Alcatraz during its operation. Hujber emphasized that while families want to continue fighting for their relatives' cases, ultimately the decision rests with the detainees themselves. For many, this means navigating a complicated legal system from thousands of miles away from their support networks, with limited access to consistent legal representation.
