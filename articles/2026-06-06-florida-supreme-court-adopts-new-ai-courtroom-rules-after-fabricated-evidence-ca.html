Florida Supreme Court adopts new AI courtroom rules after rampant misuse

Florida's highest court has adopted new rules requiring attorneys and self-represented litigants to certify that any evidence or case law they present in court actually exists and is accurate. The rules take effect June 15 in response to what legal and artificial intelligence experts describe as a growing crisis of AI misuse in courtrooms. Violators could face sanctions ranging from public reprimand to fines and suspension.

The problem has become serious enough that Florida's Supreme Court felt compelled to act. Federal Judge William Matthewman, chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has worked to address AI misuse in his own courtroom and remains concerned about the trajectory. "Unfortunately, I see it getting worse before it gets better. I think the Florida Supreme Court's order will help in Florida, but I unfortunately have seen over the last couple of years that it is growing and growing and growing," Matthewman said. He cautioned that lawyers and self-represented parties should verify everything they file. "I would caution lawyers and parties representing themselves to make sure everything you file in court is accurate, because if not, you could find yourself in hot water," he said.

A recent case in Polk County illustrates the problem. An attorney representing the Polk County School District filed a motion to dismiss in a school bus camera lawsuit containing fabricated facts and citations to cases that do not exist. The attorney apologized and stated he used ChatGPT as a drafting and research aid but had no excuse for failing to verify the information before filing. The school district said it takes accuracy and integrity seriously and is reviewing whether the attorney will continue receiving contracted work. Paul Figueroa, a Tampa criminal defense attorney who recently began using AI to improve his workflow, acknowledged the technology's limitations. "It can be something as simple as it gives a quote that's directly on point to my case, and you go, all right, this is brilliant, this is what I've been looking for, I could have searched 300 cases and not come up with anything better than this, and then you realize that's not in the case," Figueroa said. When asked if the new rules go far enough, he replied, "I do. I think it goes far enough for what we know now."

The consequences of AI misuse have extended beyond courtroom documents. Earlier this year, a Florida woman was jailed after a fake AI-generated text was used against her, though she was later cleared of all charges. In another case, a St. Petersburg woman created an AI-generated photo of a man she falsely claimed had burglarized and raped her. She later admitted the claim was fabricated, telling police, "I lied about it."
