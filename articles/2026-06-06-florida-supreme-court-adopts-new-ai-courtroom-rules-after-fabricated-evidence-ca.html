Florida Supreme Court adopts new AI rules for courtrooms after rampant misuse

Florida attorneys and self-represented litigants must soon verify that everything they cite in court actually exists and is accurate. The Florida Supreme Court has adopted new rules to combat a growing crisis of artificial intelligence misuse in courtrooms, with the regulations taking effect June 15. The problem of fake evidence, fabricated facts, and bogus case law has become so widespread that the state's highest court felt compelled to take action. Those who violate the rules face consequences ranging from public reprimand to fines and suspension.

Federal Judge William Matthewman, chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has worked to control AI use in courts after witnessing its misuse firsthand in his own courtroom. Matthewman said, "It's really a problem," and expressed concern that the situation will worsen before improving. "Unfortunately, I see it getting worse before it gets better. I think the Florida Supreme Court's order will help in Florida, but I unfortunately have seen over the last couple of years that it is growing and growing and growing," he said. Criminal defense attorney Paul Figueroa in Tampa, who recently began using AI to improve his work, emphasized that the technology is unreliable. "It can be something as simple as it gives a quote that's directly on point to my case, and you go, all right, this is brilliant, this is what I've been looking for, I could have searched 300 cases and not come up with anything better than this, and then you realize that's not in the case," Figueroa explained. When asked whether the new rules are sufficient, Figueroa responded, "I do. I think it goes far enough for what we know now."

A recent case in Polk County illustrates the severity of the problem. An attorney representing the Polk County School District in a lawsuit over school bus cameras was publicly criticized after a judge found fabricated facts and nonexistent case citations in a motion to dismiss. The attorney acknowledged using ChatGPT as a drafting and research tool but stated, "there was no excuse" for his "failure to verify…before filing." The school district said it takes accuracy and integrity in legal proceedings seriously and is evaluating whether to continue using the attorney's services.

Matthewman issued a warning to all legal professionals and self-represented parties. "I would caution lawyers and parties representing themselves to make sure everything you file in court is accurate, because if not, you could find yourself in hot water," he said. The Florida Supreme Court's order represents an important step in addressing AI-related problems in the justice system, though concerns remain about whether it will be enough to stem the tide of technology misuse in courtrooms.
