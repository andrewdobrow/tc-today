Florida Supreme Court adopts new AI courtroom rules after fabricated evidence cases

The Florida Supreme Court has adopted new rules to address the misuse of artificial intelligence in legal proceedings, marking a response to what legal experts describe as a growing crisis. The regulations take effect June 15 and require attorneys and self-represented litigants to certify that any citations and evidence they present in court are accurate and exist. Violations could result in sanctions ranging from public reprimands to fines and suspension from practice.

Federal Judge William Matthewman, chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has worked to combat AI misuse in courts after witnessing its widespread problems firsthand. "It's really a problem," Matthewman said, expressing concern about the trajectory of the issue. "Unfortunately, I see it getting worse before it gets better. I think the Florida Supreme Court's order will help in Florida, but I unfortunately have seen over the last couple of years that it is growing and growing and growing." He urged lawyers and self-represented parties to verify everything before filing, warning they could face serious consequences otherwise.

The rules emerged after several high-profile incidents highlighted the dangers of unverified AI-generated content in courtrooms. In Polk County, an attorney representing the school district in a lawsuit over school bus cameras was criticized after a judge found fabricated facts and nonexistent case citations in a motion to dismiss. The attorney acknowledged using ChatGPT as a drafting and research tool but stated there was no excuse for failing to verify the information before submission. The school district indicated it would review whether the attorney would remain eligible for future contracts. Additionally, a St. Petersburg woman was arrested after using AI to create a false photo of a man she accused of burglary and rape, later admitting the image was fabricated.

Criminal defense attorney Paul Figueroa in Tampa, who recently began using AI to enhance his workflow, acknowledges the technology's limitations. "It can be something as simple as it gives a quote that's directly on point to my case, and you go, all right, this is brilliant, this is what I've been looking for, I could have searched 300 cases and not come up with anything better than this, and then you realize that's not in the case," Figueroa explained. When asked if the new rules sufficiently address the problem, Figueroa believes they do for the current situation. Judge Matthewman's warning remains clear: anyone filing court documents must ensure accuracy or risk serious legal consequences.
