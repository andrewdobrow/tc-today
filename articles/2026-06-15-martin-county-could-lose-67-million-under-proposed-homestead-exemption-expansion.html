Proposed homestead exemption expansion could cost Martin County over $67 million

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could provide tax relief for homeowners, but critics worry the benefit may come at the expense of renters if local governments need to raise rates to compensate for lost revenue. According to analysis from the Florida Policy Institute, Martin County could lose more than $67 million if voters approve the amendment in November. The statewide impact could be even more significant, with local governments across Florida potentially losing nearly $4.8 billion in total revenue.

Mike Readling, executive director of Habitat for Humanity of Martin County, expressed concern that the proposed tax cut would not help the people his organization typically serves. He noted a recent increase in people seeking assistance with rent and bills, with 95 percent being renters. "The housing situation is so dire. It's gotten progressively worse. We have families coming in everyday," Readling said. He believes the exemption would not benefit the demographic his organization works with. Attorney John Tolley explained that local governments facing budget shortfalls would have limited options: reduce services, use reserve funds, or increase millage rates.

Realtor Michael Caputo acknowledged that if millage rates rise, landlords without homestead exemptions could face higher tax bills and potentially pass those costs to renters. However, he noted the amendment would also lower the property tax cap on non-homesteaded properties, including commercial apartment complexes, from 10 percent down to 5 percent. A 2025 Florida Senate analysis found only 24 affordable and available rental units exist for every 100 extremely low-income renters in the state.

Caputo expressed skepticism that municipalities would actually raise millage rates, suggesting the market dynamics might prevent significant rent increases. "If they're paying more for their property, they're going to try and increase that on the tenant and give it a try. If it doesn't happen for them, then they'll have to readjust their pricing," Caputo said. Attorney Tolley added that the lower tax cap on rental properties could potentially lead to reduced rents. Despite these competing perspectives, the core concern remains whether the homestead exemption expansion will ultimately burden renters who do not benefit from the tax break while local governments grapple with reduced revenue.
