Martin County Could Lose $67 Million Under Proposed Homestead Exemption Expansion

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could cost Martin County more than $67 million in lost revenue if voters approve the amendment in November, according to analysis from the Florida Policy Institute. The statewide impact would be even more significant, with local governments across Florida potentially losing nearly $4.8 billion under the measure.

The expansion raises concerns about who will ultimately bear the financial burden. Mike Readling, executive director of Habitat for Humanity of Martin County, says the proposed tax cut would do little for the people his organization serves. He reports seeing an uptick in people seeking help with rent and bills, with 95 percent of those seeking assistance being renters. "The housing situation is so dire," Readling said. "It's gotten progressively worse. We have families coming in everyday." According to a 2025 Florida Senate analysis, there are just 24 affordable and available rental units for every 100 extremely low-income renters in the state. "The demographic that we're working with and Habitat for Humanity tries to help, it's not going to help them at all," Readling said.

Attorney John Tolley explains that local governments facing revenue shortfalls have limited options: cut services, dip into reserves, or raise millage rates. If rates rise, landlords who don't receive the homestead exemption could face higher tax bills and potentially pass those costs on to renters. Realtor Michael Caputo acknowledged this possibility, saying "There could be an effect where we see some people getting priced out of these rentals, which are already pretty high." However, Caputo believes there is a high probability that local municipalities may not raise millage rates at all. He also noted that if landlords do face higher costs, the market may stabilize anyway due to supply and demand dynamics.

The amendment includes another provision that could help offset concerns about rising rents. It would lower the property tax cap on non-homesteaded properties, including commercial apartment complexes, from 10 percent down to 5 percent. Tolley suggested this reduction could potentially lead to lower rents. "The fact that you could have less taxes, you actually might see a trend where rents go down even further than they were before," Tolley said. Caputo added that while landlords may initially try to pass increased costs to tenants, they would have to readjust pricing if demand doesn't support higher rents, since "costs are pretty movable as well."
