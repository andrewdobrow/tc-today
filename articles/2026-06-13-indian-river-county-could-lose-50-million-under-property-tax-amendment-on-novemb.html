Indian River County could lose $50 million under property tax amendment on November ballot

Indian River County leaders gathered at the Heritage Center in Vero Beach on Friday morning to address concerns about a property tax amendment heading to voters this November. The amendment would increase the homestead exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. County officials and community leaders expressed alarm over the potential financial impact, with projections showing the county could lose approximately $50 million in funding by 2028 from its roughly $597 million budget.

Laura Moss, vice chair of the Indian River County Commission, organized the meeting to bring together neighbors and community leaders to discuss the amendment's implications. Moss criticized the state's approach, saying "The state is going to dictate to the local governments how we fund whatever it is we fund. Every county is different. We fund what the community wants." Lance Lunceford, president of the Indian River County Tax Payers Association, called the amendment "an unsound, unplanned piece of public policy" and emphasized that the projected revenue loss represents a substantial portion of the county's annual budget. For a smaller county like Indian River, he noted, such a reduction could prove devastating to essential services.

The potential impact on emergency services is particularly concerning. Christen Brewer, president of Indian River Firefighters, stated that fire rescue could lose between $15 to $20 million from its $70 million budget, since about 79.1 percent of the department's funding comes from ad valorem taxes. The budget shortfall could prevent the development of three projected fire stations. Lunceford emphasized there is no realistic way to absorb such a large loss without cutting into law enforcement and first responder services that the community depends on.

Rather than a genuine tax cut for residents, Lunceford characterized the amendment as a tax shift that would place financial burdens elsewhere. To compensate for lost homestead exemption revenue, the county may need to increase taxes on non-homesteaded and commercial properties or raise impact fees and utility fees. Lunceford warned that increasing taxes on businesses ultimately increases costs for everyone, as higher business taxes translate to higher prices for consumers. The amendment represents what Moss called "a war on local government" by the state, highlighting the tension between Tallahassee's policy decisions and local governments' ability to fund the services their communities require.
