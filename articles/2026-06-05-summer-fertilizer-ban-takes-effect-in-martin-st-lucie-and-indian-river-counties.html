Summer fertilizer ban begins June 1 in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop using fertilizers containing phosphorus or nitrogen on their lawns and landscaping. The restriction will remain in place through September 30, covering a four-month period during the region's rainy season. The ban applies equally to all three Treasure Coast counties and will lift after the summer months end.

The seasonal prohibition aims to address a significant environmental concern. Jordan Skaggs, with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the ban targets nutrient-rich runoff that flows into waterways during heavy rains. This excess nutrients can trigger algae blooms in sensitive ecosystems like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. These blooms create a cascade of ecological problems, as Skaggs noted: "It can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife." Beyond ecological damage, the blooms pose direct threats to public health. According to Skaggs, "At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health."

For residents seeking to maintain their gardens and landscaping during the ban period, experts recommend practical alternatives. Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, suggests composting as an effective substitute. Rather than requiring complex systems, Ricketts noted that "a small trash can" can serve as an accessible composting method. This approach provides nutrients to landscapes without relying on commercial fertilizers that contain restricted nutrients.

Residents should prepare now for the June 1 start date by using any remaining nitrogen or phosphorus fertilizers before the deadline. After September 30, when the ban ends, normal fertilizer applications can resume. The four-month restriction represents a collaborative effort by the three counties to protect the region's waterways and ecosystems during the season when rainfall is heaviest and runoff poses the greatest threat to water quality.
