Summer fertilizer ban takes effect in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties

Starting June 30, residents across Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen on their lawns and landscaping. The restriction remains in place through the rainy season, when nutrient-heavy runoff poses the greatest threat to the region's waterways. Officials implemented the ban to combat algae blooms that thrive when excess nutrients wash into local waterways during heavy rains.

The ban targets a significant environmental problem affecting some of the area's most important ecosystems. Jordan Skaggs, who works with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the restriction aims to protect waterways including the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. Algae blooms can damage these environments in multiple ways. When blooms become dense enough, they block sunlight from reaching plants beneath the water's surface that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. Additionally, certain types of harmful algae blooms can produce toxins that damage human health, harm wildlife populations, and degrade overall ecosystem conditions.

Residents should not interpret the ban as an obstacle to maintaining their landscapes during the restricted months. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as a practical alternative that provides nutrients without contributing to runoff problems. Ricketts noted that composting requires minimal space and expense. Even a small trash can can serve as an effective composting container, making it accessible for most homeowners. By composting yard waste and kitchen scraps, residents can create nutrient-rich material for their gardens and landscaping without relying on commercial fertilizers during the ban period.

The three-county fertilizer restriction reflects a growing commitment to protecting Florida's waterways from nutrient pollution. As the rainy season intensifies, any fertilizer applied to lawns and landscapes risks being washed into nearby storm drains, rivers, and coastal areas where it fuels algae growth. By switching to composting during these months, residents can maintain healthy landscapes while playing an active role in preserving water quality across the region. The ban applies uniformly throughout the three-county area, ensuring consistent protection for the shared waterways that define this part of Florida's environment.
