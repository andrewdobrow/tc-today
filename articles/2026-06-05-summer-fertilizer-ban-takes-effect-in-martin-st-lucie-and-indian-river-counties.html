Fertilizer ban begins June 1 in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop applying any fertilizer containing phosphorus or nitrogen to their lawns and landscaping. The restriction remains in effect through September 30, covering the peak of Florida's rainy season.

According to Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, the ban targets a significant environmental problem. Nutrient-rich fertilizer runoff during heavy rains feeds algae growth in local waterways. The Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River face particular risk from these blooms, which can damage aquatic ecosystems. "It can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife," Skaggs explained. When algae blooms reach certain densities, they can become harmful, releasing toxins that threaten human health, wildlife populations and the overall health of the ecosystem.

The seasonal ban creates challenges for property owners accustomed to regular fertilizer applications. However, local experts offer practical alternatives. Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective substitute during the ban period. Rather than purchasing commercial products, residents can build nutrients into their soil through composting at home. "It can be like a small trash can," Ricketts said. "That is the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape." This simple approach allows homeowners to maintain their gardens and landscaping without contributing to the nutrient pollution problem.

The three-county fertilizer ban reflects growing recognition of how residential lawn care affects water quality across the region. By reducing the amount of nitrogen and phosphorus entering waterways during the wet season, county officials hope to prevent the algal blooms that have increasingly plagued Florida's estuaries and coastal areas. Residents who follow the guidelines during these four months can help protect the local waterways that support fishing, recreation and wildlife while still keeping their properties attractive through composting and other nutrient management strategies.
