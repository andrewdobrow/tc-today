Byron Donalds, David Jolly Lead Florida Governor's Race as Primary Nears

Republican U.S. Rep. Byron Donalds and Democrat David Jolly hold commanding positions in Florida's governor's race with the August 18 primary approaching.

A Tyson Group survey released this week placed Donalds at 48% among Republicans, followed by Lt. Gov. Jay Collins at 9%, businessman James Fishback at 8%, and former Florida House Speaker Paul Renner at 4%.

About 26% of respondents remained undecided. Donalds has raised more than $90 million across his campaign operation and received President Donald Trump's endorsement.

The Republican primary has grown increasingly contentious as Collins, who was appointed lieutenant governor by Gov. Ron DeSantis in August 2025, attempts to consolidate support despite not receiving DeSantis's endorsement. During a Thursday campaign call, Collins positioned himself as the strongest alternative to Donalds and urged Renner to leave the race, saying the primary is between Donalds and himself.

Meanwhile, Fishback faces challenges on multiple fronts. The Front Line, a Republican group aligned with U.S. Sen.

Ted Cruz, announced a $400,000 campaign targeting Fishback over statements the group characterizes as antisemitic. The artificial intelligence-generated ad depicts Fishback as a talking fish making racist comments.

Collins is also challenging whether Fishback meets Florida's seven-year residency requirement for governors. A two-day hearing on the residency question is scheduled to begin July 21 in Tallahassee. The residency challenge adds another complication to Fishback's campaign as he attempts to gain traction in a race where Donalds maintains a significant polling advantage over the rest of the Republican field.

On the Democratic side, Jolly moved this week to address his past business and political relationship with Clearwater financier Leo Govoni. Federal prosecutors allege Govoni and accountant John Witeck stole more than $100 million from a nonprofit managing trust funds for people with disabilities, with the alleged scheme operating from 2009 through 2025. Jolly previously worked as a vice president at Boston Finance Group, a Govoni-controlled company, and Govoni served as a finance co-chairman during Jolly's unsuccessful 2016 U.S.

Senate campaign. Jolly is not a defendant in the case. The connection nonetheless presents a challenge for Jolly as he seeks to maintain his lead in the Democratic primary while managing questions about his past associations with individuals now facing serious federal charges.
