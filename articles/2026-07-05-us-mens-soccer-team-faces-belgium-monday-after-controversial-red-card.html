Supreme Court ruling allows absentee ballots postmarked by Election Day to count if received later

The Supreme Court handed election administrators across the country significant clarity Monday, ruling 5-4 that states may count absentee ballots postmarked by Election Day but received afterward. The ruling preserves grace period laws that election officials in several states depend on to process ballots. The case, Republican National Committee v. Wetzel, centered on a Mississippi law allowing ballots received up to five business days after Election Day to count, provided they bear an Election Day postmark.

A panel of three Trump-appointed Fifth Circuit judges had struck the law down in 2024, reading federal election statutes to require ballots to be received, not merely cast, by Election Day. The Supreme Court reversed that ruling. Justice Amy Coney Barrett, a Trump appointee, wrote the majority opinion and was joined by Chief Justice Roberts and the three liberal justices. Barrett wrote that federal law establishes when voters must make their choice, not when officials must have their ballots in hand. The election-day statutes say nothing about ballot receipt, she wrote, and the court cannot add to the words Congress chose. States retain authority to set their own receipt deadlines, meaning grace period laws in roughly 30 states covering military and overseas voters are also protected.
