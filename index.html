A West Palm Beach company is betting Floridians will pay $1,250 for a guaranteed seat on a chartered flight out of the state when a hurricane threatens. Priority Evac founder Jason Murgio said the membership-based service was born from personal experience watching people struggle to leave ahead of storms. "Priority Evac really came out of me personally watching people I know try to leave ahead of a storm," Murgio said. The plan offers members a seat on a chartered Airbus departing from the three major South Florida airports and three others on the West Coast, with all flights heading to Atlanta. A single membership covers evacuations for two storms per season.

Murgio said operations are led by a retired Air Force colonel who ran chief of operations duties at NORAD and NORTHCOM and has personally directed Florida hurricane evacuations. The service is designed to get members out before highways jam and airfares spike, two problems that routinely plague Florida evacuations. "The whole idea is the plan is already in place and before everyone else starts scrambling, you know that you have a route out," Murgio said.

The concept appears to be unlike anything travel experts have seen before. Clint Henderson, managing editor of news at The Points Guy travel website, said the company's credentials are promising, but questions remain until the service is tested by an actual storm. "That's why I'm extra cautious on something like this: they say they do have the planes and they do have some very serious people running this company, they say they have the planes prebooked, but we won't know till the rubber meets the road in an actual hurricane," Henderson said.

For families, the $1,250 per person cost can add up quickly. Murgio said there is already interest from people looking to join the hurricane evacuation service. The company is positioning itself as a solution to the chaos that often accompanies hurricane evacuations in Florida, when both road travel and commercial airline options become difficult or prohibitively expensive.

JetBlue is canceling its flight route between Vero Beach Regional Airport and New York's JFK Airport, with the last flights operating on Sept. 9. The airline cited low demand as the reason for ending the route, which launched last December to excitement from Treasure Coast residents hoping for convenient travel options to the Northeast. Brandon Dambeck, interim airport director, said JetBlue was not seeing the ridership numbers it had hoped for on the JFK route. He explained that the decision reflects standard airline industry practices, noting that air carriers adjust their routes based on demand.

Not everyone agrees the route had sufficient time to establish itself. Charlie Ahern, who regularly uses JetBlue flights at Vero Beach Regional Airport, believes the route would have grown over time. "I think if they hung in there, it would have worked. I think it would have built up pretty good," Ahern said. He emphasized the convenience of flying out of Vero Beach, saying "It's easy. It's close." The Boston route from Vero Beach continues to perform well according to Dambeck, who said it is seeing great demand. Grece Ezzi flew into Vero Beach on JetBlue from Boston on Friday for the first time.

Regarding the possibility of the JFK route returning in the future, Dambeck said JetBlue will continue to evaluate the market. "They'll evaluate continuously. So just because it's gone now doesn't mean that it never will come back," Dambeck said. He noted that air carriers regularly fluctuate their routes based on demand, characterizing such adjustments as normal business operations in the airline industry.

All affected customers will be notified directly by JetBlue and will receive full refunds. JetBlue has not announced whether it plans to add any replacement routes from Vero Beach or if it will adjust frequency on the remaining Boston service. 6:48 AM ET

Teens from anti-drug coalitions across the country joined grieving parents and federal law enforcement at the Fentanyl Free America Summit in Orlando this week, united by a shared mission to stop fentanyl before it kills more Americans. The summit drew students who traveled from around the country to learn about the resurgence of fentanyl so they can return home with a message about one of the country's deadliest drug threats. Hannah Kahn, 18, explained that a relative addicted to methamphetamine encouraged her to prevent similar destruction in others' lives. Jasmine Figueroa, 19, told adults that drug education should not be a taboo topic, saying young people will get educated one way or another and parents should be the first to tell them the truth about drugs.

This year's summit welcomed the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration as a partner. DEA Administrator Terrance Cole spent time with victim families and said the poison is being manufactured and distributed out of Mexico by the Mexican cartels. Cole said the president has designated the Mexican cartels as foreign terrorists, which opens different avenues for the DEA to target them. The students, who are members of their community anti-drug coalitions, said fentanyl spreads because it is often laced in other drugs and young people may not recognize the danger. Kahn said people are not seeking out fentanyl necessarily, but it is just within other drugs.

For parents who have lost children, the summit is deeply personal. Mari Rodriguez's daughter, Imam, died of fentanyl poisoning in 2023 after she made the mistake of buying a Percocet pill that had fentanyl in it. Rodriguez said every day she has to live with what she didn't know and questions why she gave her some space. She said the fight against fentanyl in the U.S. should have started in 2009, when the first fentanyl was found on streets, because the people behind it are not stopping. A DEA chemist recently told reporters that one sample turned out to be 16 percent fentanyl, which is high.

While deaths from the synthetic opioid are down, tracking how widespread fentanyl has become is nearly impossible in part because of Narcan, an over-the-counter nasal spray that is increasing survival rates. For the teens at the summit, prevention comes down to open conversation and making Narcan as common as any household product. Kahn said a lot of people have the stereotype that Narcan is only for responders and EMS, but people have to think about it as a household product, just like Benadryl.

2026-07-17

Demolition is underway at Murray Middle School in Martin County, marking the beginning of a major reconstruction effort following tornado damage from Hurricane Milton. The demolition phase alone will cost $3.3 million, though the total project cost has not yet been finalized. Officials plan to fund the reconstruction through a combination of local capital improvement funds, sales tax revenue, and Public Education Capital Outlay. Students displaced from damaged classrooms are continuing their education in portable units while the construction work proceeds.

The project encompasses more than just repairing storm damage. According to Kirstie Gernaine, coordinator of public information and community relations for the Martin County School District, Murray Middle School was already scheduled for significant construction work before the hurricane struck. The planned improvements included a new administration area, a new media center, and a brand new art lab. When Hurricane Milton's tornadoes destroyed buildings 18, 10, and nine, the district incorporated reconstruction of those structures into the existing improvement plans. The comprehensive project will also include a complete overhaul of the school's parking lot.

Safety has been a priority as construction proceeds on the active campus. The school district has installed barriers around the work zone to prevent students from entering construction areas and to maintain a secure learning environment. These safety measures are designed to allow normal school operations to continue while extensive construction work takes place. District officials have emphasized that parents can be assured their children will remain safe during the construction period, with precautions in place to separate students from the work areas.

The Martin County School District aims to complete all renovations by December 2027. The extended timeline reflects the scope of work required to both repair the tornado damage and complete the previously scheduled facility improvements. By combining the storm recovery effort with already planned upgrades, the district is working to turn the challenges created by Hurricane Milton into an opportunity for comprehensive campus modernization. The rebuilt and renovated Murray Middle School is expected to serve students with improved facilities for years to come, transforming destruction into a chance for significant enhancement of the educational environment.
