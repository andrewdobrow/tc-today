Yesterday, 4:55 PM ET

The Martin County School District plans to cut district staff as part of a reorganization for the upcoming school year, saving nearly $4 million from next year's budget. Superintendent Michael Maine told WPTV that the plan includes eliminating 16 administrative and district-level positions, which includes three assistant principal positions and removing 42 unfilled instructional vacancies. Maine said 10 of those vacancies were held in reserve, contingent on increasing enrollment. The reorganization eliminates positions that Maine felt are no longer needed at the district office, allowing the district to absorb a majority of the reduction. The changes are expected to save about $3.9 million from the district's projected budget next school year.

Maine said the cuts reflect how the district routinely manages staffing each year, looking at projected student enrollment and allocating resources accordingly. The Martin County School District currently enrolls 16,000 students. Maine said the goal is to protect classroom resources while streamlining operations at the district level. He emphasized that the district can become more efficient at the district office with taxpayer dollars to be able to do more with less at the district level versus at individual school sites. Maine described his approach to the reorganization as lean and mean.

Not everyone is fully on board with the scope of the cuts. Abigal, whose son will be a fourth grader at Jensen Beach Elementary this fall, said she values the small class sizes and close teacher relationships the district currently offers. She noted that teachers have one-to-one interactions with the kids and do a lot of different kinds of learning. She said she hopes the district is careful about how deep the cuts go.

Abigal expressed concern about maintaining adequate resources for students, saying that children need the administrators, teachers, and all the resources they can get. Maine stated that for a district of Martin County's size, the $3.9 million in savings represents a significant amount of money. He maintained that this approach to staffing represents the normal way the district operates, adjusting positions based on projected student numbers.

Yesterday, 10:04 PM ET

The United States men's soccer team saw its World Cup hopes end at home Monday night after losing 4-1 to Belgium at Lumen Field in Seattle. Charles De Ketelaere scored twice and assisted on another goal as Belgium exposed American defensive weaknesses to advance to the quarterfinals, where they will face 2010 champion Spain on Friday at Inglewood, California, for a semifinal berth against France or Morocco. The loss marked the second time in 12 years that Belgium knocked the United States out in the round of 16, as the Red Devils extended their unbeaten streak to 18 games while seeking their first World Cup title.

Malik Tillman tied the score 1-1 in the 31st minute with his second free kick goal of the tournament after Brandon Mechele knocked down Folarin Balogun about 25 yards from goal. Tillman's kick deflected off Hans Vanaken's head to the left of goalkeeper Thibaut Courtois. However, the Americans conceded just 61 seconds after the ensuing kickoff, prompting coach Mauricio Pochettino to show his frustration by kicking a rack in front of the bench, sending four water bottles flying. American star Christian Pulisic injured his right foot when he hit a boot of Belgium captain Youri Tielemans on a 52nd-minute shot attempt and was replaced seven minutes later.

De Ketelaere opened the scoring in the eighth minute, marking the first time the United States conceded first in this year's World Cup. Timothy Castagne hooked a centering pass around Chris Richards, and De Ketelaere split Tim Ream and Antonee Robinson to redirect the ball into an open net with his right foot. De Ketelaere scored again in the 33rd minute when he outjumped Ream and headed the ball past goalkeeper Matt Freese. Vanaken added Belgium's third goal in the 57th minute after Freese lost control of the ball in front of his net, and second-half substitute Romelu Lukaku scored the final goal in the third minute of stoppage time after Belgium stripped the ball from Richards.

The Americans played with star forward Balogun, whose one-game red card suspension was controversially lifted by FIFA. After winning three games in a World Cup for the first time in this expanded 48-nation tournament, the United States failed to reach the quarterfinals for the first time since 2002 in front of a crowd of 66,925. All six CONCACAF nations have been eliminated from the tournament, with co-hosts United States, Mexico and Canada all falling in the round of 16.

Yesterday, 5:01 PM ET

Ron DeSantis has likely wrapped his final regular bill-signing season, closing one of his last major opportunities to shape Florida law from the governor's desk. With the new fiscal year underway and DeSantis term-limited from seeking another consecutive term, the regular lawmaking window for his administration is essentially closed, barring a future special session, emergency response or other extraordinary action. Florida's Constitution bars a governor who has served more than six years across two consecutive terms from being elected to the succeeding term.

A review of regular-session action shows the scale of DeSantis' eight years in office: 2,037 regular-session bills signed since 2019, along with 66 regular-session vetoes. His busiest year came in 2023, when 341 regular-session bills became law. His lightest was his first year, 2019, with 189. Those laws reshaped major parts of Florida life. Under DeSantis, Florida expanded school choice, with the governor signing HB 1 in 2023 to eliminate income restrictions and open scholarship programs more broadly to students across the state. He also signed new abortion restrictions, including Florida's six-week ban, after first approving a 15-week restriction earlier in his tenure. The list includes permitless carry, expanded immigration enforcement, COVID-era limits on vaccine and mask mandates, new election laws, restrictions on diversity, equity and inclusion programs, and major property insurance changes. DeSantis also signed 2026 legislation giving Florida new authority tied to terrorist organization designations and additional limits on local DEI programs, continuing a broader conservative policy push in his final years in office. That agenda became central to the national brand DeSantis promoted as the Florida blueprint, a fast-moving conservative policy model powered by Republican control of the Legislature and the governor's office.

DeSantis also used his veto power to block dozens of bills. In 2022, he vetoed a net-metering bill that critics said would have hurt rooftop solar customer incentives. That same year, DeSantis vetoed an alimony overhaul, citing concerns about retroactivity. In 2024, he vetoed hemp restrictions, arguing the bill would impose heavy regulatory burdens on small businesses. In 2026, he vetoed a sovereign immunity bill that would have increased lawsuit limits against state and local government entities.

Over eight years, DeSantis vetoed roughly $10.6 billion in spending, using Florida's line-item veto power to cut local projects, trim legislative priorities and reinforce his image as a fiscal conservative. In June, DeSantis signed a $117.6 billion budget for the 2026-27 fiscal year after nearly $810 million in line-item vetoes, with his office calling it the fourth straight year of declining state spending. Democrats and local officials have often criticized those cuts, arguing they hit community projects, infrastructure needs and social services. DeSantis and his allies have defended them as necessary discipline in a fast-growing state budget.

9:55 AM ET

Russell Reed, a 64-year-old St. Lucie County man battling pancreatic cancer, has been approved to receive daraxonrasib, an experimental drug his family hopes will give him more time. Reed spent 62 days waiting to learn whether he would be approved to receive the treatment after traditional treatments were no longer effective.

Daraxonrasib, also known as RMC-6236, is an investigational once-daily oral pill developed by Revolution Medicines. The drug targets mutated KRAS proteins, which drive more than 90% of pancreatic cancers. While the treatment is not yet FDA-approved, early clinical trial results have generated optimism among some patients and physicians. According to Reed's family, his oncologist submitted a request through the FDA's Expanded Access Program, which can allow patients with serious or life-threatening illnesses to receive treatments outside of clinical trials when other options are limited. "Chemotherapy isn't going to help me at this point," Reed said.

While waiting for an answer, Reed and his wife Marnie focused on making the most of their time together. For Marnie, the fight was never just about accessing a drug. It was about more family trips, more memories and more moments together. "The idea of sitting here and waiting for the phone to ring is not ok with me," Marnie Reed said. "You're still here, and if you don't pay attention, you're going to miss it. I don't want to miss a minute with him." Reed said research and clinical trial results gave him hope that daraxonrasib could help slow the progression of his disease. "There's a lot of anecdotal evidence and a lot of evidence from the clinical trials that show that this is the miracle drug for pancreatic cancer," Reed said. "It is not a cure, but it will give me time."

Reed received approval just two days after sharing his story with WPTV. The drug is now in his hands, and his family says he is already feeling better and optimistic about what comes next. While daraxonrasib is not considered a cure for pancreatic cancer, the Reeds say the approval represents something they have been fighting for all along: more time together.

Yesterday, 5:24 PM ET

A Vero Beach homeowner says rising insurance costs may force her to sell her home within months. Kelly Hagen said her annual homeowners insurance premium has climbed to $9,000, adding $750 per month on top of her mortgage payment for her 1,500-square-foot, three-bedroom, two-bathroom home built in 1994. The premium jumped from $6,000 to $8,000 and then to $9,000 in successive years, leaving her struggling to keep up with the relentless increases.

Hagen's insurance company requires impact windows, an expensive upgrade she has struggled to afford while working two jobs. She works full time and also drives for Instacart during nights and weekends to try to make ends meet. Her situation worsened when she was turned away for a $10,000 My Safe Florida Home grant after it took too long to find a contractor, a roadblock many Florida homeowners have faced. When asked how much longer she could manage the increases, Hagen said probably just a couple more months before she would have to try to sell her house. She said leaving would be devastating.

State leaders are encouraging homeowners to shop around, pointing to new insurers entering the market with competitive rates. Stand Insurance, a new company, is offering to help homeowners connect with affordable contractors using AI technology to study homes and then works to pair homeowners with vendors and contractors to make repairs. CEO Dan Preston said helping homeowners be safer reduces claim payouts and lowers premiums for customers. He explained that if you can actually help someone be safer and have their home be safer, it benefits everyone involved because the company does not have to pay out in claims and customers pay less in premiums.

Experts advise homeowners in similar situations to explore workable changes to their policy and shop around for lower premiums with other insurance companies. For Hagen, the window to find a solution is closing fast as the financial pressure has left her with few options if costs continue to climb. Her story reflects a crisis facing many Florida homeowners who are being priced out of their homes by soaring insurance costs and expensive mitigation requirements they cannot afford to meet.
