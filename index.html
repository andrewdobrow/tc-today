Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties will need to stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen on their lawns and landscaping. The restriction will remain in place through the rainy season and aims to protect local waterways from nutrient-laden runoff that fuels algae blooms.

Jordan Skaggs, who works with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the ban is designed to reduce the amount of nutrients washing into waterways during heavy rains. The Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River are among the areas most vulnerable to algae blooms triggered by excess nutrients. These blooms create several problems for aquatic ecosystems. According to Skaggs, "It can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife." When algae blooms reach high concentrations, they can become dangerous. "At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health."

For those concerned about maintaining their landscapes during the ban, there is a practical alternative. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting. Ricketts said, "It can be like a small trash can. That is the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape." Composting allows residents to continue caring for their gardens and landscaping without relying on commercial fertilizers that could contribute to water quality problems.

The fertilizer restrictions apply throughout all three counties to both lawns and landscaping. Once the rainy season concludes, residents will be permitted to resume using fertilizers containing phosphorus and nitrogen. The temporary measure represents a community effort to protect the region's sensitive waterways during the months when heavy rainfall increases the risk of nutrient runoff entering local rivers and lagoons.

Yesterday, 5:55 PM ET

A proposed federal law banning shark feeding off Florida's coast has sparked pushback from the state's diving industry, which argues the measure unfairly targets small businesses and nonprofits while ignoring fishing practices. The bill, H.R. 3831, known as the Safe Florida Seas Act, passed the U.S. House and would prohibit shark feeding in federal waters within 200 miles of Florida, though it includes an exception for commercial harvesting. Supporters describe it as a necessary safety measure to reduce encounters between humans and sharks.

Dive operators say the legislation singles out their work, which often includes education, conservation, and structured shark encounters. Nathan Campbell, owner of Emerald Charters, noted that Congress has not requested operators present their data or explain their methods. The industry maintains a key difference between their practices and commercial fishing: operators conduct underwater baiting during dives rather than surface chumming, which they argue is less likely to condition sharks to approach boats. Commercial fishing operations, they contend, can disperse hundreds of gallons of chum at the water's surface, a practice the bill does not address.

The proposal references Hawaii's 2006 ban on baited shark diving in federal waters as a model. However, Florida operators and some researchers point to what they consider a cautionary outcome there. Dr. Matt Ajemian, an associate research professor at Florida Atlantic University, states that shark diving in Florida maintains a near perfect safety record. Since Hawaii implemented its ban, industry members note that shark incidents have increased and fatalities have occurred. According to the Western Pacific Regional Fishery Management Council, at least one in four fishing trips now results in a shark biting off caught fish.

Campbell has sent a letter to Congress requesting that senators delay action, conduct additional research, and include all stakeholders, both tourism operators and fishing interests, in developing regulations. He emphasized that operators welcome accountability but want an opportunity to present science-based evidence before rules eliminate their businesses. The diving industry argues that shark tourism supports local economies and promotes ocean conservation awareness while serving both recreational and therapeutic purposes for customers. Campbell said operators are not afraid of sharks or being held responsible, but rather want the chance to prove their practices are safe and beneficial before facing elimination.

Starting June 30, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties will need to stop applying fertilizers containing phosphorus or nitrogen to their lawns and landscaping. The restriction aims to protect the region's waterways during the rainy season, when nutrient-laden runoff can trigger harmful algae blooms.

Jordan Skaggs, with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the ban targets a specific environmental problem. When fertilizer washes into waterways during heavy rains, it enriches the water with nutrients that fuel algae growth. These blooms can damage sensitive ecosystems like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River by blocking sunlight from reaching beneficial aquatic plants that fish and wildlife depend on for food and habitat. In some cases, dense algae blooms produce toxins that threaten both human and animal health while degrading overall ecosystem conditions.

The ban will remain in effect to protect water quality during Florida's wet season, but residents still have ways to maintain their gardens and yards. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as a practical alternative. According to Ricketts, composting works much like a small trash can and provides an effective method to add nutrients back into landscaping without relying on commercial fertilizers. This approach allows homeowners to recycle organic waste while nourishing their plants naturally.

The restriction represents a significant step toward reducing nutrient pollution in local waterways. By preventing phosphorus and nitrogen-based fertilizers from entering the ecosystem during peak runoff periods, the counties hope to reduce algae bloom frequency and severity. Residents should plan ahead to transition their lawn care practices before the June 30 deadline and explore composting options that can sustain their landscaping throughout the ban period.

Fertilizer ban takes effect June 30 in Martin County to protect St. Lucie River

Starting June 30, residents in Martin, Indian River, and St. Lucie counties will no longer be able to use fertilizers containing phosphorus or nitrogen. The ban applies to all lawn and landscaping fertilizer use and will remain in effect through the rainy season. The restriction is designed to combat a significant environmental problem that affects the region's waterways during...

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Starting June 30, residents in Martin, Indian River, and St. Lucie counties will no longer be able to use fertilizers containing phosphorus or nitrogen. The ban applies to all lawn and landscaping fertilizer use and will remain in effect through the rainy season. The restriction is designed to combat a significant environmental problem that affects the region's waterways during months when heavy rainfall increases nutrient runoff.

Jordan Skaggs, with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the ban serves an important purpose for protecting local water systems. Nutrient-rich runoff from fertilizers can trigger algae blooms in sensitive areas like the Indian River lagoon and St. Lucie River. These blooms create multiple problems for the ecosystem. They block sunlight from reaching beneficial aquatic plants that fish and wildlife depend on for food and habitat. In more severe cases, harmful algae blooms can accumulate to densities where they produce toxins that threaten human health, harm wildlife, and damage the overall health of the ecosystem.

For residents concerned about maintaining their gardens and landscapes during the ban, Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective alternative. Ricketts described composting as a simple and practical solution that doesn't require complicated equipment or techniques. According to Ricketts, composting can work "like a small trash can" and represents "the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape." This approach allows residents to continue caring for their outdoor spaces while avoiding contributions to nutrient pollution in local waterways.

The three-county fertilizer ban reflects growing concerns about water quality in Florida's coastal regions. By reducing nutrient inputs during the period when rain increases runoff into rivers and lagoons, the ban aims to prevent the conditions that allow algae blooms to develop. Residents who rely on fertilizer for lawn maintenance have several weeks before the June 30 deadline to transition to alternative methods like composting or to use remaining fertilizer supplies responsibly.

The threat stretches from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake, where a breach could turn a thin strip of land into an open inlet. Among sites at risk is the Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old archaeological site already half lost to erosion, along with wildlife refuges including Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park. Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society said there are over 33 threatened and endangered species in those areas.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said the town continues to fight erosion through constant maintenance. In April 2025, the town completed a major beach renourishment project, dumping enough sand to fill nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Field said the town is reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over, noting that about $3 million in town taxpayer dollars goes toward beach renourishment every year. Field warned that if an island breach occurs, repairs could require massive state and federal investment, and if funding for beach renourishment goes away, Florida's coastline will change very quickly.
