A Jensen Beach teenager will represent Team USA at the Sailing World Championship in France this weekend, following years of competitive sailing that started when she was four years old. Harper Aberman first discovered the sport at a summer camp in Pennsylvania that her grandparents enrolled her in. "They then decided to throw my cousin and me into summer camp, where they then did a four-week course, and my cousin hated it, and I fell in love with it, and I ended up going for eight weeks," Aberman said.

When her family relocated to Jensen Beach, Aberman joined the green fleet program at the US Sailing Center of Martin County, where her competitive sailing career truly began to flourish. "That is where the sailing adventure took off for me," Aberman said. Aberman describes herself as an adrenaline junkie and says she is drawn to the sport's most challenging conditions. "When it's windy, I've always struggled to stay upright and stay out of the water," she said. Despite her modest self-assessment, Aberman has won countless tournaments, placed in several national competitions, and established herself as one of the biggest names in the youth sailing circuit.

Aberman learned she would represent Team USA during Spring Break when she received the life-changing email. "It was during Spring Break, I got the email. I started screaming, my parents started screaming, and we were like, 'This is not real. We're going to Europe this summer," Aberman said. Racing begins Monday at the venue in France. Aberman explained the training demands the location has required. "We plan on taking the waters Monday for racing, and it's just been a lot of recovery and a lot of time and effort out on the water, but it's definitely going to be worth it. Yesterday, 10:19 PM ET

Residents in an unincorporated Palm Beach County neighborhood are grappling with unexpected retroactive sewer bills from the city of Delray Beach, with some bills exceeding $4,000. Homeowners along Franwood Drive received notices demanding payment for years of unpaid sewer fees following a city audit completed in April 2026. The bills cover up to three years of charges at $90 per month, plus new monthly sewer fees going forward. Candace Etzler received a notice for $3,139, while her neighbor Ruth Stephens faces a bill totaling $4,494.92. At least a dozen homeowners in the area received similar notices.

The root of the issue traces back to 1998, when development in the area disrupted the residents' septic systems. State law required them to connect to the nearest municipal sewer system as a result. However, residents say they attempted to pay for sewer service in the past but were told they did not need to do so. The city's recent audit triggered the retroactive billing, catching residents off guard. "We were in total shock because it feels like we're being punished," Stephens said. Etzler added her frustration about the calculation method, saying "How can you have this random number of 36 months and at the price of $90 a month."

City Commissioner Juli Casale defended the billing decision in a statement, explaining that the city must determine costs incurred and recover applicable fees from those receiving service. "This is about ensuring that public utility costs are paid by those receiving the service rather than being shifted to the taxpayers," Casale said. Despite this rationale, residents question both how the back charges were calculated and the fairness of being billed retroactively without warning. Some homeowners have already paid the fees or enrolled in payment plans out of fear their service could be disconnected.

County Commissioner Marci Woodward has begun investigating the matter, stating she is working with the county attorney to examine the legality of the retroactive charges and the ongoing monthly payments. Residents acknowledge they understand the need to pay monthly sewer fees but argue the thousands in back charges are unreasonable given the circumstances. The situation reflects growing tension between municipal cost recovery and resident concerns about fairness and transparency in utility billing practices.

Martin County to open manufactured home repair funding portal in August

The legislation addresses a critical housing crisis uncovered through an investigation revealing that nearly 700,000 Florida manufactured homes, about two-thirds of the state'...

Yesterday, 6:54 PM ET

Senate Bill 594 became law on July 1, making $167 million in state affordable housing funding available to help mobile and manufactured home owners pay for emergency repairs and storm-hardening retrofits. The legislation addresses a critical housing crisis uncovered through an investigation revealing that nearly 700,000 Florida manufactured homes, about two-thirds of the state's total, were built before current federal safety standards and cannot withstand modern storms. Many residents living in these older homes, particularly seniors, lack the financial resources to make necessary safety improvements that can cost $10,000 to $15,000 or more.

The push for this law came after an EF-3 tornado destroyed 138 homes at Spanish Lakes Country Club in Fort Pierce, killing six residents. That disaster sparked an investigation that exposed the vulnerability of hundreds of thousands of older manufactured homes statewide. Homeowners across Florida began requesting assistance, and lawmakers responded by expanding access to state SHIP funding for manufactured home lot rent, emergency repairs, and storm-hardening retrofits.

Martin County is preparing to implement the program with an online portal opening in August. According to Julie Stellatos with Martin County Human Services, approximately 75 percent of the county's annual SHIP funding, typically between $1 million and $1.5 million, can now assist eligible mobile and manufactured homeowners. Interested residents can complete an online application or contact the office to request a mailed version. Stellatos emphasizes that funding operates on a first-come, first-served basis, so applicants should prepare their paperwork quickly. She notes that the law will help residents the county previously could not serve.

Other counties in the region are at different stages of implementation. Okeechobee County is updating its local housing assistance plan to comply with the new law and plans to offer lot rent, rehabilitation, and emergency repair assistance once the program launches. St. Lucie County is awaiting final state guidance before announcing its application process. Indian River County will present the legislative changes to its board on August 18, with a September 30 deadline to submit its plan to the state. Stellatos explains that while counties follow a shared calendar from the Florida Housing Coalition, timelines can vary. She recommends that interested homeowners visit the Florida Housing Finance Corporation website to check their county's available funding, verify income eligibility, identify contacts, and prepare for when applications open.

Sea turtle nesting season in Florida typically runs from March through October. Conservation groups continue encouraging beachgoers to turn off beachfront lighting at night, properly dispose of trash and avoid disturbing nesting turtles or hatchlings. 10:15 AM ET

A new Florida law mandating heart screenings for high school student athletes went into effect July 1, fulfilling a commitment born from tragedy in Port St. Joe. The Second Chance Act requires all high school student athletes to receive a medical evaluation that includes an electrocardiogram, commonly known as an EKG, before participating in school-sanctioned sports. The legislation, HB 1135, was sponsored by Rep. Jason Shoaf in the House and carried by Sen. Corey Simon in the Senate during the 2025 Legislative Session. Gov. Ron DeSantis signed the measure last summer. The law honors Chance Gainer, a standout athlete at Port St. Joe High School who collapsed and died from sudden cardiac arrest during a rivalry football game.

Sudden cardiac arrest represents the leading cause of death among student athletes, accounting for approximately 75 percent of all student athlete deaths. According to research, roughly 1 in every 300 students may have an undiagnosed heart condition with the potential to be life-threatening. For 80 percent of these students, cardiac arrest serves as the first symptom. Implementing EKG screenings allows medical professionals to identify these conditions before they become fatal. Chance Gainer's parents, Equila and JJ, channeled their grief into advocating for the law's passage. "Today marks more than just the start of a new law, it marks a promise kept," Shoaf said, emphasizing that the legislation represents "a promise to every parent in Florida that we will do our best to protect their children."

PanCare of Florida has partnered with Who We Play For to provide free sports physicals and heart screenings to student athletes throughout the region. Evan Ernst, Founder and CEO of Who We Play For, stressed the importance of the measure. "The Second Chance Act represents the state-wide commitment we need to make these heart screenings accessible to every student athlete in our state," Ernst said. He added that the organization operates under the principle that no family should experience the tragedies the Gainer family endured, particularly when such outcomes could be prevented through proper precautionary measures.

As fall practice seasons approach, families should prioritize scheduling their child's required sports physical immediately. Information regarding free EKG screening events is available through the partnering organizations. The law's implementation ensures that student athletes across Florida will have access to potentially lifesaving cardiac evaluations before they compete in school-sanctioned sports.
