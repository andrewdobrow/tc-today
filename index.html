St. Lucie County implements 45-day anchoring limits in Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor

Yesterday, 5:58 PM ET

St. Lucie County established new anchoring restrictions effective June 1 to address growing problems with abandoned vessels in local waterways. The county created two anchor limitation areas in the Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor, both located near the South Causeway Bridge. Under the new rules, boat owners cannot anchor a vessel for more than 45 consecutive days within any six-month period in these designated areas. Those who wish to continue anchoring must leave all anchor limitation areas for at least 24 hours before returning.

County officials report that abandoned vessels have become an increasing concern in these waterways. Last year, the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission collected approximately two dozen derelict vessels from the area. According to Erick Gill with St. Lucie County, the new restrictions will improve navigation and safety. "We need to make sure that highway for vessels is clear and free for other vessels to come and go," Gill said. By limiting how long vessels can remain anchored, the county aims to prevent waterways from becoming congested with abandoned or long-term anchored boats.

Environmental protection is another key motivation for these restrictions. When abandoned vessels remain in the water for extended periods, they can pose significant environmental risks. James Beams with St. Lucie County Port, Inlet and Beaches previously explained the concerns in December, noting that diesel fuel, oil, and waste from holding tanks can contaminate the water. Gill emphasized that the anchoring limits will help prevent these pollutants from entering the lagoon ecosystem.

The Indian River Lagoon supports remarkable biodiversity, with over 4,000 species of animals, plants, and birds calling it home. Gill stressed the importance of protecting this delicate environment through the new regulations. Boat owners and residents interested in learning more about the anchor limitation areas can find a detailed site map on the St. Lucie County website. These new restrictions represent the county's commitment to maintaining navigable waterways while protecting the environmental health of one of Florida's most important coastal ecosystems.

Senator Darryl Rouson has accepted a position as Presidential Scholar and Professor of Trial Litigation at New College of Florida in Sarasota, putting to rest months of speculation about his political future. The announcement came Tuesday and confirmed that Rouson will not seek election to the Florida House after term limits force him from his current District 16 Senate seat. Representative Michele Rayner is vacating the House seat to run for Rouson's Senate position, and Rouson acknowledged the encouragement he received from community members to continue his service by pursuing that office. "After much prayer, thought, and reflection, I have decided that I will not seek election to the Florida House of Representatives and will leave the Legislature when my term in the Senate ends this year," Rouson said.

Rouson brings extensive legal and public service experience to his new role. He is a trial attorney and former prosecutor who earned his law degree from the University of Florida and holds the distinction of being the first Black prosecutor in Pinellas County. Since 2008, he has represented the Tampa Bay area in the Legislature, first serving in the House before moving to the Senate in 2016. He previously served as President of the St. Petersburg NAACP and currently works as an attorney with Emerson Straw PL in St. Pete, a trial law firm that represents victims of negligence. When he leaves the Legislature this year, his public service career will have lasted approximately 18 years.

At New College, Rouson will teach courses in trial advocacy and leadership in public life. The institution's program has prepared students for careers in law, government, and public service through independent scholarship, intensive writing, and close faculty mentorship. Rouson emphasized the connection between legal training and broader intellectual development. "Great lawyers are not simply trained in the law. They are trained to analyze complex questions, engage competing ideas, and communicate with clarity and conviction," he said. "Those are qualities New College has cultivated for generations, and I am honored to help carry that tradition forward."

New College President Richard Corcoran, who appointed Rouson to the state's Constitution Revision Commission in 2017 when Corcoran served as Speaker of the House, praised the addition to the faculty. "His experience, judgment, and commitment to public service will be a tremendous asset to our students and our institution," Corcoran said, calling Rouson's career in the courtroom and Legislature "an extraordinary opportunity to learn from one of Florida's most respected legal and public leaders." New College has not announced when Rouson will formally begin his teaching duties.

The situation was further complicated by the fact that two of the children are nonverbal and attend a specialized school for their needs. Despite this, Rolle could not identify the name of the school the children attended, raising additional concerns about the level of care and attention provided.

Following the investigation, authorities removed all five children from Rolle's care and transferred them to the Department of Children and Families. Rolle was released on bond following her arrest. Governor DeSantis signs bills to reshape Hillsborough County government, put school superintendent choice to voters

Governor Ron DeSantis signed two bills Monday that will allow Hillsborough County voters to decide whether to expand the County Commission and switch to an elec.

1:29 PM ET

Governor Ron DeSantis signed two bills Monday that will reshape Hillsborough County government and put the school superintendent selection process before voters. The measures, sponsored by Apollo Beach Republican Rep. Michael Owen, will appear on the November 2026 ballot. HB 4029 asks voters whether to expand the Hillsborough County Commission from seven members to nine and eliminate countywide seats in favor of single-member districts. The board would later grow to 11 members if the county's population reaches at least 2.5 million residents. The bill would also require a majority plus one vote of the full Commission for key actions including appointing or removing the County Administrator, County Attorney and County Internal Auditor, and proposing charter amendments. It would revise the composition and appointment process for the county's Charter Review Board and alter rules for citizen-led charter amendments.

HB 4027 asks Hillsborough voters whether to replace the district's appointed Superintendent with an elected one. If approved, the Hillsborough County Public Schools Superintendent would be chosen in a partisan countywide election beginning in 2028 and serve a four-year term. Supporters argued the current structure dilutes local representation in a county with more than 1.5 million residents and makes countywide races more expensive and harder for candidates. Owen said he envisions the change giving residents an opportunity to elect representatives closer to home. The Superintendent proposal drew sharp debate, with supporters arguing voters should have a direct say over the leader of one of the state's largest school districts, while opponents warned the change could politicize the role and pull focus from the district's educational mission.

Stuart police responded to Atlantic Tire at 705 Southeast Monterey Road on Tuesday morning after a vehicle fell off a lift and fatally injured a 24-year-old technician. Martin County Fire Rescue's extrication team arrived just after 6:30 a.m. and found the technician pinned between the vehicle and the wall inside the mechanic bay. He was pronounced dead at the scene. Stuart police say OSHA is involved and the incident is being investigated as a workplace incident.

Palm Beach County braces for largest department budget cuts in history

Palm Beach County is preparing for what leaders describe as potentially the largest cuts to county departments ever, as proposed changes to property taxes threaten to eliminate nearly two-thirds of the county's budget.

A few minutes ago

Palm Beach County is preparing for potentially the largest cuts to county departments in history, as proposed property tax changes threaten to eliminate nearly two-thirds of the county's budget. County Administrator Joe Abruzzo said a reduction in property taxes would save residents money but could cost the county more than 65% of its budget. If the property tax reduction passes, the county's budget for 30 departments could drop from $609 million, losing just shy of $400 million over the next two years, according to the county's property appraiser. The fire department alone could lose just shy of $200 million.

The cuts would also impact parks, libraries, roads, and other county services. Abruzzo said county leaders are confident they can operate the government with these cuts without disrupting services. "We are making arguably the largest cut potentially in history to the PBC departments and operating funds but I can tell you we feel comfortable running the government with these cuts," he said. "We don't believe it will throw any types of services out of whack and we will be in my estimation extremely fiscally responsible." A significant part of the budget discussion has focused on property taxes compared to homeowners insurance, which has grown significantly in recent years. Final property value estimates are expected in July, with the full budget to be decided in September.

Mom Mobile brings prenatal care to Okeechobee County women

A 38-foot mobile clinic is now offering maternity care to pregnant women in Okeechobee County every Tuesday at the public library on West 16th Street.

10:30 AM ET

Okeechobee County lacks a hospital that delivers babies, forcing many pregnant women to travel to Fort Pierce or Highlands County for prenatal care. The Mom Mobile program, which originated with the Martin County Healthy Start Coalition, now brings maternity services directly to expecting mothers in Okeechobee. The 38-foot mobile clinic is stationed at the public library on West 16th Street every Tuesday.

Andrea Medellin with the Okeechobee Healthy Start Coalition said the need was immediate. "In Okeechobee we have zero delivery hospitals. The closest delivery hospital is Fort Pierce or Highlands County, depending on which side of the community you live on," Medellin said. During the first three days of operation, fifteen women were seen, and appointment requests continue to arrive. Each visit is designed to last an hour to provide quality care to high-risk mothers. Twenty-year-old Evelyn Allen, a pregnant Okeechobee resident, struggled to find affordable resources before Mom Mobile arrived. "I couldn't find any resources and the resources I did find you have to come out of pocket for it, or they would expect you to do more and you can't," Allen said.
