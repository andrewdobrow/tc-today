Fort Pierce coral reef protections rolled back as activists rally to save Oculina Bank

The Oculina Bank is found nowhere...

Yesterday, 1:28 AM ET

A federally appointed council voted to roll back longstanding protections for the Oculina Bank, a rare deep-water coral reef located just off the coast of Fort Pierce. Dozens of community members gathered at the Blake Library in Martin County to rally for saving the reef after the vote, which would reopen parts of the reef to shrimp trawling.

The Oculina Bank is found nowhere else on Earth and thrives hundreds of feet below the surface in deep, dark waters, unlike most coral that needs shallow sunlight to survive. The reef is home to more than 2,000 marine species. Dr. Grant Gilmore, a scientist who has dived the rare reef, warned that removing the habitat and food source provided by the coral would devastate fish populations. Mark Perry, Executive Director of the Florida Oceanographic Society, and Gilmore urged the public to help save the reef during the gathering.

Two proposals would reopen parts of the reef to shrimp trawling, even though scientists say trawling destroyed as much as 90% of the reef before federal protections were put in place decades ago. The proposals followed a Trump executive order issued in April 2025 that tasked eight regional councils nationwide to find ways to reinvigorate fishing industries. Bob Zales, executive director of the Southeastern Fishing Association and a generational shrimper, believes reopening shrimping access is essential for the struggling rock shrimp industry, which he says is teetering on the brink of extinction.

Council data shows the rock shrimp industry has plummeted from over 700 trips per year in the mid-1990s to under 100 since 2010. The number of active rock shrimpers dropped from 144 in 1996 to less than 30, with just 12 active boats in 2018. Zales attributes the decline to high costs, competition from imported shrimp, and closures of previously open fishing areas. Scientists warned that reopening the reef could irreparably damage what little remains, noting that the corals grow very slowly and have been there for a long time.

More than 100 paddlers will launch from Bimini, Bahamas, at midnight on June 13 and cross 80 miles of open Atlantic Ocean to Lake Worth Beach in the annual Crossing for Cystic Fibrosis. The endurance event, expected to take 12 to 16 hours, raises awareness and funds for families living with cystic fibrosis through the Pipers Angels Foundation.

Event founder Travis Suit established the foundation in honor of his daughter, Piper, who was diagnosed with cystic fibrosis at age 4. Suit, an avid paddler, was inspired to create the crossing after learning about the natural respiratory benefits of ocean salt air. The foundation provides direct support for cystic fibrosis patients and families, connecting them with resources and community.

The crossing is part of a four-day schedule beginning June 10 with arrivals and a poolside social at Bimini Cove. On Thursday, participants will take part in a 4Ocean beach cleanup and a community buffet dinner. Friday brings a Paddle Out Rose Ceremony to honor cystic fibrosis warriors, followed by rest and safety checks before launch night. All paddlers must wear a U.S. Coast Guard-approved personal flotation device, waterproof whistle, and reflective gear during dark hours, and GPS trackers provided by the event must be worn and active at all times.

Paddlers must raise at least $2,000 for the cause, while crew or virtual participants have a $500 minimum. Final contributions are due July 31. Spectators are invited to Lake Worth Beach on June 13 to cheer paddlers during a daylong beach celebration marking their arrival. More information, registration, and donation details are available at crossingforcysticfibrosis.com and pipersangels.org.
