2026-07-15

Martin County faces a $2.1 billion transportation funding shortfall over the next two decades, with nine road widening projects left without financial backing, according to a PowerPoint obtained through a public records request. The Martin County Metropolitan Planning Organization estimates the county will need approximately $2.8 billion for transportation improvements over the next 20 years, but only $701 million is currently available. This means the county can fund just about one quarter of its identified transportation needs.

Beth Beltran, administrator for Martin County's Metropolitan Planning Organization, said the cost analysis was emailed to county and Stuart officials last August. Of the $701 million currently available, all but $10 million comes from state and federal funding sources. The significant funding gap has forced difficult decisions about which transportation projects can move forward and which must remain on hold.

Among the most pressing unfunded needs are nine roads the county identified as requiring widening as traffic grows. The total cost to widen those roads is approximately $213 million, money that is not currently available, leaving all nine projects out of the county's draft Cost Feasible Plan. Those roads include SW Martin Downs Boulevard, SR-714/SW Martin Highway, SW 96th Street, SW Bridge Road, CR-714/SW Martin Highway, SW Allapattah Road/CR-609, SE Green River Parkway, SW Indiantown Avenue, and SE Bridge Road/CR-708.

State Road 710 is among the county's highest priorities due to safety concerns. Beltran said fatal crashes as well as serious injury crashes were just enormous on that corridor, with improvements estimated at about $300 million. The substantial price tag for State Road 710 alone represents nearly half of the total funding shortfall and highlights the challenge facing county officials as they balance safety needs against available resources. The funding gap affects not just road widening projects but a range of transportation improvements needed across the county over the next two decades.

Palm Beach County commissioners deny private Christian school proposal in Jupiter Farms

Yesterday, 6:35 PM ET

Palm Beach County commissioners voted Tuesday to deny a controversial proposal to build a large private Christian school in Jupiter Farms after hours of emotional public debate over traffic, growth and the future of the community's rural character. The proposal from Calvary Church would have brought a K-12 private Christian school to a vacant site on Indiantown Road, with plans for nearly 1,000 students. Palm Beach County Mayor Sara Baxter was the only commissioner to vote in favor of the project, citing the need for private Christian education in the area.

For more than three hours, residents lined up to speak, with traffic emerging as one of the most contentious topics of the night. Opponents warned the project was simply too large for Jupiter Farms and would fundamentally change the character of the community. One speaker told commissioners that preserving the rural residential and equestrian character of Jupiter is the objective of county plans, noting the scale of the proposed school is larger than the proposed Home Depot and has more customers in it. Supporters pushed back, with one resident saying fewer buses would result and envisioning grandchildren riding bikes to school, adding it would be much safer for the kids.

Commissioners spent significant time questioning details presented by county staff and project representatives. One issue centered on concerns from the zoning board that development would cover roughly 80 percent of the property. Project officials disputed that interpretation, explaining that required buffer landscaping between the school and nearby homes was being counted as development, along with a planned lake on the site. Another major point of disagreement involved whether there is actually a need for another school in the area. Calvary representatives argued nearby schools are already at capacity, but commissioners noted many schools highlighted in the presentation were public schools while the proposed project would be a private school with tuition costs and different enrollment requirements that would not benefit public school students. Commissioner Joel Flores pointed to where public support was coming from during the hearing, saying only about 25 percent of those who spoke in favor of the project were from Jupiter, while many supporters came from outside the area, including Port St. Lucie and Boca Raton.

The county's Zoning Commission Board voted unanimously last month to recommend denial of the proposal. Calvary Church officials say they have not decided what comes next. Church leaders say it remains unclear whether they will rework the project and try again or move forward with selling the property instead. The county denied the application without prejudice, which means the school project could be brought back in a different form, but Calvary officials would have to go through the same administrative process.

The St. Lucie County Sheriff's Office announced Tuesday that a second person has been arrested in the fatal shooting of Fort Pierce art teacher Andre Leonard Barnes, 66. Dale A. Mincey, 41, Barnes' nephew, was formally charged with first-degree murder Saturday in connection with Barnes' death. Mincey remains in custody at the St. Lucie County Jail and is being held without bond. Deputies said the fatal shooting was premeditated.

The fatal shooting happened July 7 at a residence on the 2700 block of Essex Drive. Deputies responded to the home at around 3:30 p.m. after receiving reports of a person with a gunshot wound. When deputies arrived, they learned a man had shot another man with a rifle. Barnes was taken to a local hospital, where he died. Karen Moye was also at the home when the shooting occurred. She heard a loud bang while she was in the bathroom and came out to find Mincey and called police, an arrest affidavit said. Moye, who lived in the home with Mincey and Barnes, told detectives immediately after the shooting that the pair did not have a good relationship and that Mincey told her he was expressing fear in the days leading up to the shooting, the arrest affidavit said. Moye told detectives Mincey said Barnes was trying to kill him and Mincey did not want to be left alone at the house.

Sheriff Richard Del Toro said Tuesday during a press conference that detectives uncovered information indicating that Moye, the victim's sister and mother of the suspect, knowingly provided false information to investigators and withheld pertinent information related to the homicide investigation. She has been charged with false report to law enforcement and the commission of a capital felony. During questioning, Mincey told detectives he could not recall what happened the day of the shooting and that he didn't have any issues with Barnes and did not know how Barnes died, an arrest affidavit said. However, he admitted to owning two firearms. In a follow-up interview with Moye on Monday, she gave a detective conflicting accounts of what happened on the day of the shooting, according to an arrest affidavit. She told detectives she was in fear of Barnes and that something might happen between Mincey and Barnes. When asked why she didn't notify police, she said she thought both of them would get in trouble.

Barnes worked as an art teacher for the St. Lucie County School District since 2017, serving at West Gate K-8 and Samuel S. Gaines Academy. The district confirmed his passing and extended condolences to his loved ones. Barnes leaves behind three adult children. Former student Caila Coleman, who said Barnes was her 7th-grade teacher in Tallahassee, remembered him as a pillar of the community and father figure. Deputies said the fatal shooting remains an active investigation and are encouraging anyone with information to contact the St. Lucie County Sheriff's Office or Treasure Coast Crime Stoppers.

Yesterday, 4:27 PM ET

A 28-year-old man died Tuesday after he was struck by a semi trailer truck while fleeing from U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents on State Road 16 near a gas station in Florida. The man was one of four people who fled on foot during an encounter with ICE and Homeland Security Investigations agents in the parking lot of a local gas station and convenience store. Florida Highway Patrol officials confirmed they were not involved in the incident but have opened an investigation into the death. According to the state highway patrol, the man ran across State Road 16 and into the path of the tractor trailer. He was struck in the right lane and sustained fatal injuries on scene.

Details about the victim, including his name, nationality and immigration status, were not immediately available. ICE and the Department of Homeland Security have not yet commented on the incident. The state highway patrol said in a statement to Scripps News that during the encounter, four occupants of a vehicle fled on foot, with one running across the highway and into the path of the oncoming truck.

The death comes as ICE is under increasing scrutiny for multiple fatal encounters nationwide. Joan Sebastian Guerrero, a 26-year-old Colombian man living in Maine, was fatally shot by ICE agents Monday during a traffic stop. Senator Angus King said Guerrero was not the target of an immigration operation. Days earlier, Lorenzo Salgado Araujo was fatally wounded during a traffic stop involving ICE in Houston. Like the shooting in Maine, Salgado Araujo was not the person ICE was trying to find.

The Trump administration ordered Immigration and Customs Enforcement agents to pause most traffic stops on Tuesday following the fatal incidents. Florida Highway Patrol officials have confirmed their investigation into the State Road 16 death remains ongoing. The incident marks the latest in a series of deadly encounters involving ICE agents that have drawn attention to the agency's enforcement tactics. The pause on traffic stops comes as federal immigration enforcement operations continue under increased public and political scrutiny.

Registration is now open for Port St. Lucie City University, a free nine-session program designed to help residents better understand how their local government operates.

Classes begin Wednesday, Aug. 12, and will be held from 5:30 to 8:30 p.m. in the Council Chambers at Port St. Lucie City Hall, located at 121 SW Port St. Lucie Blvd.

Participants will learn about City departments, municipal services and the decision-making processes that shape the community. The program also gives residents opportunities to meet City staff, ask questions and gain a closer look at the work involved in operating one of Florida's largest cities.

Residents who complete the course will receive a certificate of completion and may also choose to become a City Ambassador, helping share information about City programs and services throughout the community.

City University is intended to encourage civic engagement while strengthening connections between residents and local government. Since the program began in 2016, more than 600 residents have participated.

Enrollment is limited to 160 participants.

Residents who are unable to attend the in-person classes can also access City U on Demand, which offers a more flexible way to learn about Port St. Lucie government and municipal operations.

Jupiter Inlet Lighthouse offers 165 years of history with 105-step climb to panoramic views

2026-07-15

The Jupiter Inlet Lighthouse, a red brick tower constructed in 1860 at the base of the Jupiter Inlet on Florida's Atlantic coast, has operated continuously for 165 years as both an active navigational aid and a public attraction. The historic structure has guided mariners since before the Civil War while welcoming visitors who make the 105-step climb to its observation gallery. The spiral staircase winds through the interior of the brick tower, following the same narrow path that lighthouse keepers climbed night after night to maintain the powerful Fresnel lens that projected light across the water to passing ships. This climb provides visitors with a tangible connection to the daily work of the keepers who ensured the beacon remained operational throughout more than a century and a half of continuous service.

The observation gallery at the top delivers panoramic views stretching across multiple waterways and the ocean. The Atlantic Ocean extends to the east, while below the tower, the turquoise waters of the Jupiter Inlet converge with the Loxahatchee and Indian rivers. Visitors at this elevated vantage point can watch boats glide through the channel as sea breezes sweep across the observation area. The commanding perspective reveals why this location proved ideal for a lighthouse, offering clear sightlines across the coastal landscape and waterways that allowed keepers to monitor maritime traffic and project the beacon across miles of ocean.

The museum grounds preserve more than just the tower itself. Restored historic buildings chronicle the lighthouse's journey from its construction before the Civil War through its years as an active Coast Guard station. These structures provide information about the lighthouse's role in regional maritime history and offer details about the daily lives of the keepers who operated the station. The site maintains the supporting buildings that allowed the lighthouse station to function across generations.

The preservation efforts tell the complete story of this working navigational aid that has marked the Florida coastline for mariners throughout its 165 years of operation. Modern visitors follow the same path that lighthouse keepers took throughout history to maintain operations, retracing the steps of the early keepers as they ascend the narrow spiral staircase. The combination of the functioning lighthouse, restored keeper buildings, and access to the observation gallery allows visitors to experience both the historical significance and the practical purpose of this enduring coastal landmark.
