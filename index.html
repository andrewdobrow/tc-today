Port St. Lucie's 34987 zip code has earned recognition from RentCafe as one of the fastest-growing in the nation, with Southern Grove leading the charge. The neighborhood, which was virtually nonexistent a decade ago, has transformed into a thriving community. Housing inventory in the area has increased by 177 percent, representing one of the largest growth spurts in Florida. The neighborhood has also attracted residents who are highly educated and maintain strong homeownership rates.

The explosive growth of Southern Grove stems partly from its deliberate design as one of four planned neighborhoods created to draw people to the area and encourage them to remain. The strategy appears to be working, as major employers have made Southern Grove a hub for job opportunities. FedEx and Amazon have both established operations there, with Amazon demonstrating its confidence in the area by planning to close its current facility later this year and construct a larger replacement.

According to Alexandra Both, a writer for RentCafe, Florida's emergence as a hotbed for new neighborhoods reflects several competitive advantages. The state ranks second only to Texas for up-and-coming communities, largely due to its favorable tax environment and housing costs that remain lower than major markets like New York and California. Beyond these factors, sustained investment in infrastructure has spurred construction activity throughout the state's growing suburbs.

Other Florida communities have also caught the attention of RentCafe. Ponte Vedra Beach claimed the top position on the organization's "America's 50 Newest Neighborhoods" list, appealing to families, retirees, and young professionals alike. Both Daytona Beach and the St. Augustine area made the top 50, receiving praise for their excellent schools and robust housing demand. This recognition underscores Florida's broader appeal as a destination for growth and development, with Southern Grove standing out as a particularly striking example of how strategic planning and economic investment can transform a region.

5:58 PM ET

St. Lucie County rolled out new anchoring restrictions on June 1 designed to address the growing problem of abandoned vessels in local waterways. The regulations apply to two anchor limitation areas: the Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor near the South Causeway Bridge. Under these rules, boaters cannot anchor a vessel for more than 45 consecutive days within any six-month period without leaving all designated anchoring limitation areas for at least 24 hours.

County officials have noticed an uptick in derelict vessels cluttering these important waterways. Last year alone, the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission collected approximately two dozen abandoned vessels from the area. According to Erick Gill with St. Lucie County, the new restrictions serve a practical purpose by improving navigation. "We need to make sure that highway for vessels is clear and free for other vessels to come and go," Gill explained. By limiting how long boats can remain anchored in one spot, the county aims to prevent vessels from becoming permanent fixtures that obstruct traffic and access.

Beyond navigation concerns, abandoned vessels pose serious environmental threats to the lagoon ecosystem. James Beams with St. Lucie County Port, Inlet and Beaches highlighted these dangers in an earlier interview, noting that long-abandoned vessels create multiple pollution risks. "There's a lot of environmental concern with the diesel and oil tanks and then holding tanks," Beams said. When these vessels deteriorate or leak, they release harmful contaminants into the water. Gill emphasized that the new anchoring limits will help prevent these pollutants from contaminating the waterway.

The Indian River Lagoon is an ecologically vital area supporting over 4,000 species of animals, plants, and birds. Protecting this biodiversity is central to the county's motivation for implementing the restrictions. "We want to make sure we're protecting it," Gill said. Boaters who need detailed information about the anchor limitation areas can access a site map through St. Lucie County. The new rules represent the county's effort to balance recreational boating access with environmental preservation and waterway management.

Under these new rules, boaters cannot anchor a vessel for more than 45 consecutive days within any six-month period in these designated areas. Anyone who wants to anchor longer must leave all anchoring limitation areas for at least 24 hours before returning.

The county has been observing an increase in abandoned vessels in these waterways, which prompted the new policy. According to Erick Gill with St. Lucie County, the Florida Wildlife Commission collected around two dozen derelict vessels last year. The anchoring restrictions aim to keep these waterways more navigable for active boating traffic. "We need to make sure that highway for vessels is clear and free for other vessels to come and go," Gill said. Environmental protection is another major concern driving the new regulations. James Beams with St. Lucie County Port, Inlet and Beaches emphasized this threat, noting the environmental consequences of prolonged vessel abandonment. Gill stressed the importance of preventing these pollutants from contaminating the lagoon, which serves as home to an estimated 4,000 species of animals, plants, and birds. "We want to make sure we're protecting it," Gill said.

The anchoring limitation areas represent the county's effort to balance boating access with environmental stewardship and waterway maintenance. By preventing long-term anchoring without breaks, officials believe they can reduce the number of abandoned vessels while protecting the lagoon's ecosystem and ensuring clear navigation for legitimate boating activities. Eridjon Dacruz of West Palm Beach is traveling to Miami this month to watch Cape Verde compete in the World Cup for the first time in the island nation's history. Dacruz grew up in Cape Verde, an archipelago of 10 islands located off the African coast, before moving to the United States. The island nation has a population of only half a million people and gained independence from Portugal in the 1970s. As one of the smallest countries ever to qualify for the World Cup, Cape Verde's achievement has generated tremendous excitement among its citizens, including Dacruz's family members who still live there.

Cape Verdeans are celebrating their national team, known as the Tubarão Azul or Blue Sharks, reaching the World Cup field. Dacruz expresses amazement at the accomplishment, saying he did not expect it to happen in his lifetime given the country's small size. He recalls searching for Cape Verde players in FIFA video games years ago and finding only about two available. The qualification has been the dominant topic of conversation among people on the islands, reflecting how significant this moment is for the nation. Dacruz will attend Cape Verde's match against Uruguay in Miami, a gift from his girlfriend presented during their anniversary dinner in a small box with a handwritten note that read "I'm going to the World Cup."

Cape Verde opens the tournament against Spain, one of the tournament favorites, before facing Uruguay. Dacruz remains optimistic about his team's chances, saying that if they win a couple of games, they can advance far in the competition. He is currently waiting for the newest Cape Verde national jersey to arrive so he can wear it to the game this month. Making the World Cup will provide the nation's soccer federation with more than 10 million dollars to help develop the next generation of players and strengthen the country's soccer program.

During hurricane season, Cape Verde receives significant attention in weather coverage as tropical systems frequently form near the islands before tracking west across the Atlantic toward the United States. According to meteorologist Sami Squires, tropical waves form along the African coast in the region and eventually track across the Atlantic, sometimes heading toward the U.S. These weather patterns make Cape Verde a familiar reference point for people monitoring hurricane season activity.

A 6.1 magnitude earthquake centered off the west-northwest coast of Cuba sent tremors across Florida on Tuesday, triggering evacuations in Miami and being felt as far north as Jacksonville. The quake interrupted daily activities at multiple buildings throughout the region, prompting immediate safety responses from local authorities.

Students and staff at Fusion Academy in Miami were among the first to evacuate when the earthquake struck. The evacuation alarm differed noticeably from routine fire drills, with an urgent message declaring this was not a standard drill. Maria Torrado, a student at the academy, noted that people on the upper floors experienced the shaking more intensely than those on lower levels. The heightened urgency of the alarm startled many students who were accustomed to regular fire safety exercises.

Reactions to the earthquake varied across different locations. Students at the Swatch Watchmaking School were evacuated but did not initially understand what was happening. Jack Hodapp reported that people in the break room felt the quaking sensation, but those in other areas had no idea why they were being asked to leave the building. Some students initially thought their coworkers were pranking them. Juan Cardona, who was also evacuated, initially suspected criminal activity was unfolding before learning from conversations with others that an earthquake centered near Cuba had caused the commotion.

The Miami-Dade Sheriff's Office responded by establishing a command center and blocking off streets near affected buildings as a precautionary measure. After buildings were inspected for safety, evacuated individuals were allowed to return inside. The National Weather Service assessed the situation and confirmed there was no tsunami risk resulting from the earthquake. No injuries were reported in Florida from the seismic event. The swift response from local authorities and the rapid return to normal operations meant that despite the disruption and initial confusion, the situation was resolved without significant consequences for the affected communities.

West Palm Beach man to cheer Cape Verde at World Cup in Miami

Eridjon Dacruz of West Palm Beach is headed to Miami this month to cheer on Cape Verde, one of the smallest countries ever to qualify for the World Cup.

1:43 PM ET

Eridjon Dacruz, a West Palm Beach resident who grew up in Cape Verde, will attend his country's World Cup game against Uruguay in Miami this month. Cape Verde, an archipelago of 10 islands off the African coast, gained independence from Portugal in the 1970s and has a population of half a million people. The nation's soccer team, known as the Tubarão Azul or Blue Sharks, qualified for the World Cup for the first time ever, making Cape Verdeans ecstatic about their achievement. Dacruz received his ticket as an anniversary gift from his girlfriend, who presented him with a handwritten note during their anniversary dinner saying "I'm going to the World Cup."

Cape Verde opens the tournament against Spain, one of the tournament favorites, before facing Uruguay. Dacruz expressed confidence in his team's ability to advance, saying "All we have to do is win a couple of games and we'll get far." The World Cup qualification will provide Cape Verde's soccer federation with more than $10 million to help develop the next generation of players. During hurricane season, the islands are frequently referenced in weather coverage, as tropical systems often form near Cape Verde before tracking west across the Atlantic toward the U.S.
