Summer Fertilizer Ban Takes Effect in Martin, St. Lucie and Indian River Counties

A seasonal fertilizer ban is now in effect across Martin, St. Lucie and Indian River counties. The ban restricts the application of fertilizers containing nitrogen and phosphorus during the summer rainy season. The restriction aims to prevent excess nutrients from washing into local waterways during heavy summer rains. Fertilizer runoff contributes to algae blooms and water qu...

5:39 PM ET

A seasonal fertilizer ban is now in effect across Martin, St. Lucie and Indian River counties. The ban restricts the application of fertilizers containing nitrogen and phosphorus during the summer rainy season.

The restriction aims to prevent excess nutrients from washing into local waterways during heavy summer rains. Fertilizer runoff contributes to algae blooms and water quality problems in the Indian River Lagoon and other coastal waters.

The ban typically runs from June 1 through September 30 in the three counties. Residents and landscaping companies cannot apply fertilizers with nitrogen or phosphorus to lawns and landscapes during this period. Violators may face fines.

Local governments enacted the seasonal bans to protect water quality in the lagoon system. The restrictions align with state best management practices for fertilizer use in coastal communities.

The Indian River Lagoon has faced persistent environmental challenges from nutrient pollution. During the summer months, heavy rainfall increases the likelihood that fertilizer applied to residential and commercial properties will run off into nearby waterways. Once in the lagoon system, excess nitrogen and phosphorus promote the growth of harmful algae blooms that can damage the ecosystem.

These blooms deplete oxygen in the water, creating dead zones where marine life cannot survive. The algae can also produce toxins that affect both wildlife and human health. The problem has become increasingly severe in recent years, prompting local officials to take action.

The fertilizer ban represents one of several strategies communities are using to improve water quality. By preventing nutrient application during the wettest months, the ban reduces the amount of pollution entering the lagoon system through stormwater runoff.

Residents who need information about the ban should contact their local government offices. Many communities provide guidance on alternative lawn care practices that comply with the restriction. Some properties may qualify for exemptions under certain circumstances, though residents should verify specific rules in their county.

Landscaping companies operating in the three counties have been notified of the requirements. Contractors must adjust their service schedules to avoid applying restricted fertilizers during the ban period. Companies that violate the restrictions face potential fines and other penalties.

The ban applies to all nitrogen and phosphorus-containing products, including many common lawn fertilizers. Residents should check product labels to determine if their fertilizers fall under the restriction. Some fertilizer types that do not contain these nutrients may still be applied during the summer months.

Local officials emphasize that the ban is temporary and seasonal. Once the rainy season ends on September 30, residents and businesses can resume normal fertilizer applications. The cycle repeats each year to coincide with the months when rainfall is heaviest and runoff risk is greatest.

2:38 PM ET

Florida has implemented emergency rules to prevent the entry of screwworm, a parasitic fly recently detected in Texas that poses a threat to livestock and wildlife across the state. The Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services activated the emergency protocols in response to the discovery of the invasive pest in neighboring Texas, marking a significant agricultural concern for Florida's livestock industry.

Screwworm flies lay eggs in open wounds of warm-blooded animals, and the hatched larvae feed on living tissue. The parasite can infest cattle, horses, sheep, goats, pigs, and wildlife, causing severe damage and potentially fatal infections if left untreated. The pest was eradicated from the United States in 1966 through a coordinated effort, but periodic detections in border states require immediate action to prevent reestablishment. Florida's agricultural economy, which includes significant cattle ranching operations and a diverse livestock sector, faces substantial risk if the pest gains a foothold in the state.

The emergency rules allow state agriculture officials to restrict the movement of livestock and animals from affected areas and implement enhanced inspection protocols at entry points. Florida maintains strict biosecurity measures to protect its agricultural industries, which contribute billions of dollars annually to the state economy. The department can now require health certificates and movement permits for animals entering Florida from regions where screwworm has been detected.

State agriculture officials are coordinating with federal authorities and Texas counterparts to monitor the situation and prevent the pest's spread. Florida livestock owners are being advised to regularly inspect their animals for wounds and signs of infestation. The emergency rules remain in effect until the threat subsides and normal biosecurity protocols can resume. Florida's quick response reflects the serious economic and animal welfare implications of a screwworm outbreak, with state officials emphasizing vigilance among farmers and ranchers throughout Florida.
