Those blooms could disrupt waterways like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. S...

Jun 5, 5:36 PM ET

Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties will ban any fertilizer containing phosphorus or nitrogen starting June 30. Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County said the ban is to help reduce nutrient rich run off during the rainy season, which can cause algae blooms.

Those blooms could disrupt waterways like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. Skaggs said algae blooms can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife. At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health.

Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, said a good alternative during the ban is using composting for gardens and landscaping. Ricketts said composting can be done in a small trash can, which is the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape.

The fertilizer ban runs through the rainy season to prevent nutrient runoff from reaching local rivers and lagoons during the period when Florida typically receives the most rainfall.

Janet Milici, co-owner of Natural Flow Impact Windows and Doors in Stuart, has spent nearly two years and almost $40,000 trying to obtain a permit for a simple storage building at her business property. She first applied in 2024 to build a 30-by-40-foot structure in the back of her property to store materials used by her company, not as part of any business expansion.

Throughout the application process, Milici says she has been required to complete multiple studies and reviews including engineering assessments, environmental evaluations, traffic analyses, and even pay for turtle relocation. "People try to cut corners and do things without permitting, but we are trying to do everything right," Milici said. Despite understanding that permitting requirements exist for good reasons, she believes the process has become unreasonably complicated for a straightforward storage project. The permit has recently been approved, but the city continues to charge additional fees as the project moves forward.

The delays have created serious problems for Milici as hurricane season began. With materials still stored outdoors while waiting for the project to be completed, she faces potential damage and significant financial losses. "The thing that bothers me is that hurricane season is here, and if I can't put my materials in a safe place, then it's going to cost me—it's going to put me out of business if you really want to know," she said. She stressed that she is simply trying to improve her property and the service she provides to customers, but feels blocked at every stage.

Milici brought her concerns to public attention after attending a Let's Hear It community meeting in Jensen Beach, deciding the issue deserved broader attention among area residents. "It's been a process, and I thought that this should be brought to everyone's attention who lives in this area," she said. WPTV has requested information from the City of Stuart's Planning and Zoning department about the standard process for obtaining storage building permits within city limits but has not yet received a response.

Jupiter Island faces critical erosion threatening to split island at Peck Lake

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report warns Jupiter Island's entire 11.5-mile shoreline is critically eroded, with the ocean potentially breaking through at Peck Lake and splitting the island in half.

Jun 5, 4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report warns that Jupiter Island's 11.5-mile shoreline has eroded so severely that ocean breakthrough at Peck Lake could split the island in half. The erosion threat extends from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake, where a breach would transform a thin strip of land into an open inlet. This scenario has occurred before. In the early 1960s, a storm breached Peck Lake until the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers filled it back in with sand. According to the report, the Joseph Reed Mound, an archaeological site between 3,000 and 4,000 years old, has already lost half its area to erosion. Wildlife refuges including Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park are also threatened. Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society noted that over 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit these areas and that future breaches could destabilize ecosystems dependent on a careful balance between fresh and saltwater environments.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field acknowledged that sea level rise is factual, noting that rates have accelerated over the last 15 years. In April 2025, Jupiter Island completed a major beach renourishment project that deposited enough sand to fill nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. The town spends approximately 3 million dollars in taxpayer money annually on beach renourishment. Field emphasized that current solutions are temporary fixes, stating "We are just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over." He warned that if funding disappears, "the coastline of Florida is going to change very quickly, and not in a way that anybody's particularly happy with."

Florida childhood poverty drops by 200,000 since Prosperity Initiative launch

Florida has reduced the number of children living in poverty by more than 200,000 since launching the Florida Prosperity Initiative, though 16.5% of the state's children still live in poverty.

4:15 AM ET

More than 200,000 fewer Florida children are living in poverty since the launch of the Florida Prosperity Initiative, according to the State of Childhood Poverty in Florida report. However, 16.5% of Florida children still live in poverty, with 711,576 children affected statewide. Over half of these children are concentrated in just 152 of Florida's 983 ZIP codes, representing 15% of the state's total ZIP codes. Hillsborough County demonstrated the largest single-year reduction in childhood poverty in Florida, with 3,971 fewer children living in poverty in 2024 than in 2023. In the Tampa Bay region, disparities remain stark. In Hillsborough County's 33612 ZIP code, 3,912 children live in poverty, the highest concentration in the area, while only 37 children live in poverty in ZIP code 33606.

The Florida Prosperity Initiative focuses on 10 Root Causes of Poverty, including access to meals, behavioral and mental health services, stable housing, and workforce training. Mark Wilson, President and CEO of the Florida Chamber of Commerce and Foundation, said Florida aims to become the 10th-largest global economy by 2030 and cut childhood poverty in half, as outlined in the Florida 2030 Blueprint. The Florida Chamber Foundation is hosting the 2026 Florida Prosperity Economic Opportunity Solution Summit on June 9 in Tampa to examine actionable, data-driven strategies that expand economic opportunities at the ZIP code level.

Florida sees one in five children still living in poverty despite recent progress

Florida's childhood poverty rate stands at 16.5%, with disparities concentrated in specific ZIP codes across the state, according to a new analysis from the Florida Prosperity Initiative.

4:15 AM ET

Florida still has one in five children living in poverty, with 16.5% of the state's 711,576 children affected, according to the Florida Prosperity Initiative's State of Childhood Poverty in Florida report. More than half of these children are concentrated in just 152 of Florida's 983 ZIP codes, with dramatic disparities visible in the Tampa Bay region. In Hillsborough County's 33612 ZIP code, 3,912 children live in poverty, the highest concentration in the area, while nearby ZIP code 33606 has only 37 children in poverty. Despite these challenges, Hillsborough County demonstrated that progress is possible, recording the largest reduction in childhood poverty in Florida over the past year, with 3,971 fewer children living in poverty in 2024 than in 2023.

The report identifies 10 Root Causes of Poverty affecting Florida families, including access to meals, stable housing, behavioral and mental health services, and healthcare. These issues are interconnected: children struggling with hunger are more likely to face academic challenges, families without stable housing often experience employment and education disruptions, and limited healthcare access creates long-term financial instability. Mark Wilson, President and CEO of the Florida Chamber of Commerce and Foundation, emphasized that reducing childhood poverty is essential to Florida's goal of becoming the 10th-largest global economy by 2030 and strengthening the state's future workforce.
