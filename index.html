Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties have a ban on any fertilizer containing phosphorus or nitrogen. Jordan Skaggs, with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, said the ban is to help reduce nutrient rich run off during the rainy season, which can cause algae blooms. Those blooms could disrupt waterways like the Indian River lagoon and St. Lucie River.

"It can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife," said Skaggs. "At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health." Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, said a good alternative during the ban is using composting for your garden and landscaping. "It can be like a small trash can," said Ricketts. "That is the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape."

Orion Jean Baptiste, a 9-year-old Port St. Lucie resident, has started a junior chess club to introduce other young people to a game he has loved since age 5. Baptiste learned to play chess by spending time with family members and developed a passion for the game that he now wants to share with his community. The Atlantis Chess Club meets every Friday at the Morningside Library in Port St. Lucie and welcomes players of all skill levels.

The club has already attracted more than 36 junior members in a short time. Diana Cowan Kempton, the club's program director, believes chess can help shape the next generation of young people. According to Kempton, the game helps develop leaders and community members who are more focused, patient, strategic and thoughtful when making decisions. Baptiste himself sees value in promoting board games during an era when many young people are drawn to technology and screens. "I'm hoping that more people have respect for board games," Baptiste said. "Everybody's more focused on like tech stuff."

Families in the community have embraced the club for its positive atmosphere and educational benefits. Thomas Furlong brought his two children to the club because he saw it as an ideal opportunity for his daughter to learn the game and his son to improve his skills. Furlong also appreciated the friendly competition it created between his children. His daughter Francesca Furlong, who recently joined the club, enjoys the social aspect of playing with other children. "Playing with other kids" is what she likes most about being a member, she said.

The Atlantis Chess Club's success in Port St. Lucie has led to expansion plans. The club will soon launch two additional programs at the Fenn Center in Fort Pierce, extending Baptiste's mission to more young people in the area. Through his initiative, Baptiste is demonstrating how one person's passion for a game can build community connections and inspire others to develop skills like patience, strategic thinking and focus. His simple goal of helping kids learn about chess has grown into something much larger.

Gov. Ron DeSantis announced appointments and reappointments to the St. Johns River State College District Board of Trustees this month. Alexandria Hill and Kevin Payne were newly appointed to the board, while Richard Komando, Willard "Judd" Sapp Jr. and Tammy "Chereese" Stewart were reappointed. The Florida Senate must confirm these appointments before they become official.

St. Johns River State College operates three campuses across Orange Park, Palatka and St. Augustine, serving approximately 8,000 students combined. Hill brings legal expertise to the board as a partner in the Driver, McAfee, Hawthorne Dibenow law firm. She is active in the Clay County community and serves on the Clay County Development Authority. Hill earned a bachelor's degree in finance and marketing from Stetson University and a law degree from the University of Florida. Payne works as senior manager of contracting for Imagine 360 health insurance company and serves as president of the Jacksonville Association of Health Underwriters. He holds a bachelor's degree in accounting from the University of Texas and an MBA from Oklahoma City University.

Komando brings extensive legal experience to the board as a partner in the Bradley, Garrison Komando law firm on the First Coast. He previously served as circuit director and statewide general counsel for the Fourth Judicial Circuit Guardian ad Litem Office for Northeast Florida and worked as an assistant state attorney in that circuit. Komando holds a bachelor's degree in criminal justice and an MBA from Florida State University, along with a law degree from Loyola University. Sapp is a Navy veteran who previously served on the St. Johns County Day School Board of Trustees. He earned a bachelor's degree from Florida State University.

Stewart currently serves as assistant county manager for the Clay County Commission and was previously elected to that position. She is involved in multiple local organizations, including the Clay County Development Authority, Clay County Cattlemen Association, Clay County Farm Bureau and the Florida Planning and Zoning Association. Stewart earned a bachelor's degree from Texas A&M University and a master's degree in educational leadership and administration from the University of North Florida. These board members will help guide the college's operations and strategic direction across its three-county service area.

State leaders are weighing whether to ban fireworks for the Fourth of July's 250th anniversary celebration as Florida faces drought conditions more severe than 1998, the last time a statewide ban was enacted.

Jun 5, 2:17 PM ET

Florida's Fourth of July celebrations could look very different this year as state leaders weigh whether to ban fireworks due to severe drought conditions. The Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services says this year's drought is even more severe than 1998, the last time a statewide fireworks ban was put in place. The entire state of Florida is at least abnormally dry, with areas including Palm Beach County and the Treasure Coast experiencing moderate to extreme drought conditions.

Florida Agriculture Commissioner Wilton Simpson issued an early warning about the possibility of a ban in May. "If we're still in drought conditions come Fourth of July, absolutely. We're talking about obviously, potentially hundreds of thousands of people going out doing fireworks. You could put the entire state on fire in evening all over the state all at one time," Simpson said. Simpson acknowledged the significance of this year's 250th anniversary celebration but urged residents to consider alternatives. At Sky King Fireworks in West Palm Beach, owner Bill Murphy reported increased customer activity ahead of the holiday. Murphy said the store monitors drought conditions closely but remains cautiously optimistic about rain in the forecast. The Department of Agriculture said a fireworks ban would be a last resort.

Palm Beach County rental assistance program overwhelmed with 8,000 calls per month

Palm Beach County's Emergency Rental Assistance Program receives about 600 applications in the first couple of hours they're open each month, with nearly 8,000 calls for housing assistance monthly.

Yesterday, 6:03 PM ET

Palm Beach County's Community Services Department has nearly $2 million in combined grant funding for its Emergency Rental Assistance Program, designed to help residents who can't pay rent. But each month, demand overwhelms available resources. "We're doing what we can to prioritize and serve those who are most needy but the demand it far outweighs the resources that are available," said program director Dr. James Green. According to the Department of Housing and Urban Development, the average rent for a one-bedroom in Palm Beach County is $2,020 a month. Green said the recent surge in applications stems partly from rising costs of living, including housing, gas, and food.

Applications open at the beginning of each month but fill rapidly. "There are nearly 8,000 calls for housing assistance per month based on our data combined with 211. We currently have about 800 applications in the queue for rental assistance right now. We typically receive about 600 applications the first couple of hours they're open," Green explained. At the time of reporting, the county's assistance portal was marked CLOSED due to high demand. Green urges anyone in need to call 1-833-CSD-WILL to check for availability. For those unable to access the county's program, 211 is available 24/7 to connect residents with nonprofits and other assistance programs in the area.

Jupiter Island officials warn erosion could split island in half at Peck Lake

A new state report warns that nearly the entire coastline of Jupiter Island is critically eroded, with the ocean potentially breaking through at Peck Lake and splitting the island in half.

Jun 5, 4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report warns that Jupiter Island's 11.5-mile shoreline has eroded so severely that the ocean could break through at Peck Lake, potentially splitting the island in half. The threat stretches from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake, where a breach could turn a thin strip of land into an open inlet. A similar breach occurred during a storm in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers filled it back in with sand. Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society said a future breach could upend ecosystems that depend on a delicate balance of fresh and saltwater. The consequences extend beyond the shoreline to conservation lands and historical sites. At risk is the Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old archaeological site already half lost to erosion, along with wildlife refuges including Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park. Over 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit these areas, and erosion is threatening mangrove swamps critical for habitat and coastal protection.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said the town fights erosion through constant beach renourishment. In April 2025, the town completed a major project dumping enough sand to fill nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Field said oceanfront homes are not in immediate danger because of this work, but characterized the approach as temporary: "We are just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over." The town spends approximately $3 million annually in taxpayer funds on beach renourishment. Field warned that without continued state and federal funding, "We would have cataclysmic erosion."

Jun 3, 1:03 PM ET

More than 100 paddlers will cross 80 miles of the open Atlantic Ocean this June to raise awareness and funds for families living with cystic fibrosis in the annual Crossing for Cystic Fibrosis. According to event founder Travis Suit, participants will launch from Bimini, Bahamas, at midnight on June 13, arriving at Lake Worth Beach after 12 to 16 hours of paddling. The endurance trek benefits the Piper's Angels Foundation, which Suit established in honor of his daughter, Piper, diagnosed with cystic fibrosis at age 4.

The crossing is part of a four-day schedule beginning June 10, with arrivals and a poolside social at Bimini Cove. On Thursday, participants take part in a 4Ocean beach cleanup and a community buffet dinner. Friday brings a Paddle Out Rose Ceremony to honor cystic fibrosis warriors, followed by rest and safety checks before launch night. All paddlers are required to wear a U.S. Coast Guard-approved personal flotation device, waterproof whistle, and reflective gear during dark hours. GPS trackers provided by the event must be worn and active at all times, and mandatory safety meetings will be held. Paddlers must raise at least $2,000 for the cause, while crew or virtual participants have a $500 minimum. Final contributions are due July 31. Spectators are invited to Lake Worth Beach on June 13 to cheer paddlers during a daylong beach celebration marking their arrival.
