Florida Supreme Court adopts new AI courtroom rules after fabricated evidence cases

12:04 PM ET

The Florida Supreme Court has adopted new rules to address the misuse of artificial intelligence in legal proceedings, marking a response to what legal experts describe as a growing crisis. The regulations take effect June 15 and require attorneys and self-represented litigants to certify that any citations and evidence they present in court are accurate and exist. Violations could result in sanctions ranging from public reprimands to fines and suspension from practice.

Federal Judge William Matthewman, chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has worked to combat AI misuse in courts after witnessing its widespread problems firsthand. "It's really a problem," Matthewman said, expressing concern about the trajectory of the issue. "Unfortunately, I see it getting worse before it gets better. I think the Florida Supreme Court's order will help in Florida, but I unfortunately have seen over the last couple of years that it is growing and growing and growing." He urged lawyers and self-represented parties to verify everything before filing, warning they could face serious consequences otherwise.

The rules emerged after several high-profile incidents highlighted the dangers of unverified AI-generated content in courtrooms. In Polk County, an attorney representing the school district in a lawsuit over school bus cameras was criticized after a judge found fabricated facts and nonexistent case citations in a motion to dismiss. The attorney acknowledged using ChatGPT as a drafting and research tool but stated there was no excuse for failing to verify the information before submission. The school district indicated it would review whether the attorney would remain eligible for future contracts. Additionally, a St. Petersburg woman was arrested after using AI to create a false photo of a man she accused of burglary and rape, later admitting the image was fabricated.

Criminal defense attorney Paul Figueroa in Tampa, who recently began using AI to enhance his workflow, acknowledges the technology's limitations. "It can be something as simple as it gives a quote that's directly on point to my case, and you go, all right, this is brilliant, this is what I've been looking for, I could have searched 300 cases and not come up with anything better than this, and then you realize that's not in the case," Figueroa explained. When asked if the new rules sufficiently address the problem, Figueroa believes they do for the current situation. Judge Matthewman's warning remains clear: anyone filing court documents must ensure accuracy or risk serious legal consequences.

5:36 PM ET

Starting Monday, residents in Indian River, Martin, and St. Lucie counties will be prohibited from using any fertilizer that contains phosphorus or nitrogen. The restriction applies to both residential lawns and landscaping projects throughout the three-county area.

According to Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, the ban aims to decrease nutrient-rich runoff during the rainy season. Excessive nutrients in stormwater runoff can trigger algae blooms in local waterways. The Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River are particularly vulnerable to these blooms, which can damage the ecological health of these important water systems. Algae blooms block sunlight from reaching beneficial aquatic plants that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. In more severe cases, harmful algae blooms produce toxins that pose risks to human health, wildlife, and the overall ecosystem.

Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as a practical alternative during the ban period. Rather than relying on chemical fertilizers, residents can use compost to naturally enhance their gardens and landscaping. Ricketts suggests that a small trash can is an accessible and effective method for composting kitchen and yard waste, making it easy for homeowners to add nutrients to their landscape without breaking the law.

The fertilizer ban represents a concerted effort by the three counties to protect local waterways from nutrient pollution during the months when heavy rainfall increases the risk of runoff entering rivers, lagoons, and coastal areas. Residents who use their outdoor spaces for gardening and landscaping should prepare now by transitioning to composting methods before the ban takes effect.

Excess nutrients in these waterways can trigger algae blooms that harm aquatic ecosystems. The Indian River lagoon and St. Lucie River are particularly vulnerable to these blooms. According to Skaggs, algae can block out plants that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. For those looking to maintain their gardens and landscaping during the ban period, composting offers a practical solution. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective alternative for adding nutrients to soil. He noted that composting does not require expensive equipment or extensive space. According to Ricketts, residents can use a small trash can as a composting container, making it one of the easiest and most practical methods available for enriching their landscape with natural nutrients.

The fertilizer restrictions reflect growing concerns about water quality in Florida's coastal areas. By limiting the application of nitrogen and phosphorus-based products during peak rainfall months, the three counties hope to reduce the nutrient load entering their waterways and prevent the environmental damage associated with algae blooms. Residents should plan ahead before June 1 to adjust their lawn care and landscaping practices accordingly.

Florida state leaders are examining whether to implement a statewide ban on fireworks for this year's Fourth of July celebration as drought conditions have reached levels not seen since 1998, when the state last prohibited fireworks across the entire state. The Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services has determined that current drought conditions are even more severe than those that prompted the 1998 ban. Every part of Florida is experiencing at least abnormally dry conditions, with Palm Beach County and the Treasure Coast facing moderate to extreme drought situations.

Florida Agriculture Commissioner Wilton Simpson warned about the potential ban earlier in the year, recognizing both the importance of celebrating the nation's 250th anniversary and the serious fire risks posed by widespread fireworks use. Despite the drought concerns, Sky King Fireworks in West Palm Beach has experienced increased customer activity as people prepare for the holiday. Bill Murphy, who works at the store, said the business closely monitors drought conditions to determine whether it is safe for fireworks use. Murphy expressed cautious optimism about the situation, pointing to weather forecasts that predict significant rainfall. "We always keep a close eye on the drought index to make sure the ground is saturated enough, but the reality is there's plenty of rain in the forecast," Murphy said.

The Department of Agriculture has indicated that a statewide fireworks ban would be a last resort measure, though the severity of current drought conditions has prompted serious deliberation about whether such action will become necessary. State officials continue to monitor conditions as the Fourth of July approaches to determine whether celebrating the nation's 250th anniversary will require residents to find alternatives to traditional fireworks displays.

4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report reveals that nearly Jupiter Island's entire coastline faces critical erosion, endangering infrastructure, ecosystems, wildlife and archaeological sites. The assessment found the island's 11.5-mile shoreline has deteriorated so severely that ocean water could break through at Peck Lake, potentially dividing the barrier island in two. The threat extends from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake, where a breakthrough would transform a narrow land strip into an open inlet. This scenario has historical precedent. A storm breached Peck Lake in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers restored it with sand fill. Mark Perry from the Florida Oceanographic Society warns that another breach would destabilize ecosystems requiring a precise balance between fresh and saltwater to function. He also notes the consequences reach far beyond the shoreline itself.

The stakes include significant natural and cultural resources. The Joseph Reed Mound, an archaeological site dating back 3,000 to 4,000 years, has already lost half its mass to erosion. Wildlife refuges at risk include Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park. Perry points out that over 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit these areas. A separate state assessment indicates erosion threatens mangrove swamps essential for habitat and coastal defense, while elevated water levels could displace entire species across the island. Perry emphasizes that sea levels have risen since the 1950s and have accelerated over the last 15 years, requiring collaborative approaches to address the problem.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field confirmed the town battles erosion through continuous maintenance efforts. In April 2025, the community completed a substantial beach renourishment initiative, adding sand equivalent to filling nearly 400 Olympic-sized pools. While oceanfront homes face no immediate danger, Field stressed this security depends entirely on ongoing maintenance work. He characterized the current approach as repeatedly applying a temporary solution. About $3 million in local taxpayer funding supports beach renourishment annually. Field cautioned that a potential breach would demand significant state and federal investment for repairs. He indicated the recent FDEP assessment strengthens Jupiter Island's position to secure state and federal funding for erosion mitigation efforts.

Field emphasized that sustained funding remains uncertain. He warned that discontinuing beach renourishment support would trigger rapid and widespread coastal changes across Florida that residents would likely regret. The island's future depends on maintaining this investment in protective measures.

Florida dive operators fight federal shark feeding ban, warn of tourism and conservation impacts

A federal bill to ban shark feeding in waters within 200 miles of Florida has cleared the U.S. House, drawing sharp criticism from dive operators who say it unfairly targets their conservation-driven businesses.

5:55 PM ET

The Safe Florida Seas Act, H.R. 3831, would prohibit shark feeding in federal waters within 200 miles of Florida, with an exception for harvesting. Supporters call it a common-sense safety measure to reduce human-shark interactions. However, a coalition of dive operators contends the bill unfairly targets their work, often veteran-owned small businesses and nonprofits focused on education, conservation and responsible shark encounters, while leaving fishing practices untouched. Nathan Campbell, owner of Emerald Charters, said no one from Congress has asked operators to present data or explain their practices. Operators say they avoid surface chumming by taking bait underwater during dives, which they argue is less likely to condition sharks to approach boats looking for food, unlike some commercial harvesting operations that release hundreds of gallons of chum at the surface.

The bill cites Hawaii's 2006 ban on baited shark diving in federal waters as precedent. However, Florida operators and some researchers say the Hawaii experience tells a different story. Dr. Matt Ajemian, an associate research professor at Florida Atlantic University, says shark diving in Florida has a near perfect safety record for customers. Industry members note that since Hawaii's ban, shark incidents have increased, fatalities have been reported and, according to the Western Pacific Regional Fishery Management Council, at least one in four fishing trips ends with a shark biting off part of the catch. Campbell has sent a letter to Congress urging senators to delay action, gather research and involve all stakeholders in shaping any new rules.
