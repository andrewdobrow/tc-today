9:28 AM ET

Governor Ron DeSantis has signed the newest version of the Live Local Act, legislation that has now been revised three times since its original approval three years ago. Senate bill SB 962, sponsored by Green Cove Springs Republican Sen. Jennifer Bradley, and House bill HB 837, sponsored by Coral Gables Republican Rep., both passed the Florida House and Senate this month. The updated law continues efforts to address Florida's housing shortage by expanding affordable residential options while limiting local government control over housing development.

The revised legislation requires cities and counties to permit apartment buildings and mixed-use housing projects in areas currently zoned for commercial or industrial purposes, provided that at least 40% of the units are designated as affordable housing for a 30-year period. This requirement can also apply to land owned by local governments, school districts, and large religious institutions. The law restricts how much local governments can control the height and design of these developments, making it more difficult for cities and counties to prevent projects through zoning restrictions. The measure will expand the types of dwelling units allowed, reduce local limitations on development, and protect funding sources for home purchases. Agricultural land and farm operations remain excluded from these provisions.

The original Live Local Act faced criticism from organizations like Florida TaxWatch for not going far enough to help middle-income renters. This group identified a gap in the housing market for households earning too much to qualify for affordable housing subsidies but too little to afford market-rate rents in their communities. In August, TaxWatch published a report recommending that legislators revisit and strengthen the measure. The state responded by enacting these revisions, which address some of the shortcomings identified in the watchdog group's analysis.

The updated Live Local Act represents a significant shift in Florida's approach to its housing crisis. By reducing local control over development design and height while requiring acceptance of affordable housing projects in commercial and industrial zones, the state has taken a stronger position in removing construction barriers. The mandate that 40% of units remain affordable for three decades establishes a long-term commitment to preserving housing accessible to lower-income Floridians. The original measure from 2023 aimed to increase affordable and workforce housing construction through funding mechanisms, tax incentives, and zoning changes. These latest updates build on that foundation with more aggressive state-level intervention in local land-use decisions.

Florida resilience expert says disaster preparedness is state's best insurance policy

On National Insurance Awareness Day, a Florida disaster resilience leader argues that strengthening homes and investing in mitigation creates a safer and more insurable Florida.

7:30 AM ET

Leslie Chapman-Henderson, president and CEO of the Federal Alliance for Safe Homes, said National Insurance Awareness Day on June 28 offers an opportunity to discuss resilience as a critical insurance component. Drawing on 12 years in the insurance industry responding to catastrophes and 28 years advancing disaster resilience, Chapman-Henderson distinguishes between insurance and resilience: insurance helps people recover after disasters, while resilience helps them withstand disasters in the first place.

Florida has faced significant insurance challenges for decades due to frequent hurricanes, inflation, litigation costs and continued population growth in vulnerable areas. Following Hurricane Andrew in 1992, Florida strengthened its building codes, and today the state's high-wind standards rank among the nation's strongest. The state created My Safe Florida Home, the nation's first wind-mitigation grant program, which has invested more than $500 million to help thousands of homeowners strengthen their properties. Chapman-Henderson noted that homes built or upgraded to modern standards perform better during disasters and communities prioritizing preparedness recover faster. Researchers at Florida International University and the University of Florida, working with insurance industry engineers, continue developing innovations to protect lives and property while supporting a healthier insurance marketplace.

Peggy Adams Animal Rescue League urges South Florida pet owners to prepare hurricane plans

With hurricane season in full swing, Peggy Adams Animal Rescue League is urging South Florida residents to create a disaster plan for their pets before the next storm hits.

Jun 24, 1:34 PM ET

Dr. Alyssa Comroe, CEO of Peggy Adams Animal Rescue League, told WPTV that the safest place for pets during a hurricane is alongside their owners. The shelter's expert team recommends pet owners ensure their animals wear collars with current identification and have updated microchips registered with correct contact information. Owners should also introduce pets to a designated safe room ahead of time, such as a walk-in closet or downstairs bathroom, and conduct practice runs so the hurricane is not their first time there.

Pet owners should prepare an emergency kit containing a three-day supply of food and water in airtight containers, bowls, a sturdy carrier, bedding, towels, medications for two weeks, vaccination records, first-aid supplies, litter and box for cats, leashes, harnesses, cool packs, wipes, waste bags, and multiple forms of visible ID. The shelter recommends keeping a printed list of local pet-friendly shelters and hotels in an accessible place and maintaining recent photos of pets, ideally with their owner, to help with identification if separated. Florida's hurricane season runs from June through November, overlapping with extreme summer heat. Proper storm preparation reduces risk during evacuations, while heat safety measures such as water enrichment, shade, and limiting midday walks help protect pets year-round.
