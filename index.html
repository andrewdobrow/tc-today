The change came in response to an investigation that revealed ne...

Yesterday, 6:54 PM ET

Nearly 140 new Florida laws took effect on July 1st, and one of them could significantly help some of the state's most vulnerable homeowners. Senate Bill 594 opens up $167 million in state affordable housing funding to assist mobile and manufactured home owners with emergency repairs and storm-hardening retrofits. The change came in response to an investigation that revealed nearly 700,000 manufactured homes across Florida, about two-thirds of the state's total, were built before current federal safety standards and cannot withstand modern storms.

The push for this funding began after an EF-3 tornado destroyed the Spanish Lakes Country Club Community in Fort Pierce. The disaster obliterated 138 homes and killed six residents. Paula Richards, a Spanish Lakes resident, received repair estimates ranging from $10,000 to $15,000 but could not afford the costs. Many seniors living in older manufactured homes across Florida face the same financial barriers to making their structures safer. Their pleas for assistance eventually reached lawmakers, who responded by passing Senate Bill 594 in spring 2026.

Implementation of the program varies by county. Martin County Human Services reports that an online portal will open in August, with about 75 percent of the county's annual SHIP funding, typically $1 million to $1.5 million, now available to eligible mobile and manufactured homeowners. St. Lucie County is still waiting for final guidance from the state before announcing its application process. Indian River County officials will present the legislative change to their Board on August 18th, with a September 30th deadline to submit their implementation plan to the state. Okeechobee County is updating its local housing assistance plan to comply with the new law and plans to offer lot rent, rehabilitation and emergency repair assistance once the program opens.

People interested in applying should visit the Florida Housing Finance Corporation website to check income eligibility and learn how to apply. Applications can be submitted online or by mail. According to Julie Stellatos with Martin County Human Services, the funding operates on a first-come, first-served basis, making it important for residents to gather their paperwork quickly. While counties generally follow the same timeframe based on a calendar from the Florida Housing Coalition, the specific timeline varies by location. Beginning preparation now by visiting the state website and selecting your county will help you understand what funding is available and who to contact when applications open in your area.

Yesterday, 10:19 PM ET

Homeowners in an unincorporated Palm Beach County neighborhood are facing unexpected bills for years of unpaid sewer fees after the city of Delray Beach completed an audit in April 2026. Several residents along Franwood Drive received notices demanding retroactive payments, with some bills exceeding $4,000. The notices cover up to three years of charges and establish new monthly sewer fees moving forward. Candace Etzler received a bill for $3,139, and she estimates that at least a dozen homeowners in her neighborhood received similar notices. Resident Ruth Stephens was charged $4,494.92 and expressed her concern about the unexpected expense. Both homeowners said they fear their sewer service could be disconnected if they fail to pay the retroactive charges.

The billing stems from events dating back to 1998, when nearby development disrupted the neighborhood's septic systems. Florida state law required residents to connect to the closest municipal sewer system following the disruption. Etzler explained the situation, saying "They shook up our drain fields for our septic and the state law mandates you to hook up to the municipality that's closest to you." According to residents, they previously attempted to pay for sewer service but were told by the city that payment was not required. This confusion appears to have created the gap between when they should have begun paying and when the city actually billed them for the service.

Residents are questioning how the city calculated the retroactive charges. Etzler challenged the methodology, asking "How can you have this random number of 36 months and at the price of $90 a month." Stephens echoed the frustration, stating "I understand having to pay a monthly fee, but my total was $4,494.92, and that's hard for people to just come up with." Some residents have already paid the fees or enrolled in payment plans out of concern about potential disconnection.

City Commissioner Juli Casale defended the billing in a statement, explaining "When a sewer connection is identified, the City's responsibility is to determine what costs were incurred and recover the applicable fees. This is about ensuring that public utility costs are paid by those receiving the service rather than being shifted to the taxpayers." County Commissioner Marci Woodward said she has contacted the county attorney's office to investigate the legality of both the retroactive charges and the monthly payment requirements going forward. The situation highlights a dispute over municipal billing practices and whether residents should bear the full cost of utility services they were unable to pay earlier.

Martin County free clinic sees surge in patients after federal health subsidies expired

Volunteers in Medicine, a free medical clinic serving low-income families in Martin County, is experiencing increased patient demand following the expiration of enhanced financial assistance programs through the Affordable Care Act at the start of the year. The clinic's leadership says the loss of these federal subsidies has left many residents without health insurance and unab...

Yesterday, 6:23 PM ET

Volunteers in Medicine, a free medical clinic serving low-income families in Martin County, is experiencing increased patient demand following the expiration of enhanced financial assistance programs through the Affordable Care Act at the start of the year. The clinic's leadership says the loss of these federal subsidies has left many residents without health insurance and unable to access medical care. Dr. Jordan Bromberg, the clinic's medical director, reports that more than 200,000 people across Florida did not renew their ACA coverage this year, with estimates suggesting upwards of 500 Martin County residents lost their insurance access as a result of the subsidy expiration.

One patient affected by the change is Rokaya McGarry, who could no longer afford health insurance premiums after the enhanced subsidies ended. She initially worried about her health situation, knowing that without insurance, emergency rooms would be her only option for care. McGarry said, "Panic set in because you know that the only way that you're going to be helped as far as health is to go to an Emergency." Her situation became urgent when she fell ill following the loss of her mother, experiencing weakness and bronchitis. "The grief and the everything I had like a bronchitis I felt very weak and in a moment of desperation," McGarry said. She contacted Volunteers in Medicine and received care within a day.

According to Bromberg, the loss of insurance coverage poses serious health risks for people who delay seeking preventive care. "There are a lot of things that get screened for that if they're found early can prevent terrible outcomes," he explained. While only about 20 patients have sought care at the clinic specifically after losing ACA coverage, Bromberg believes many more Martin County residents are in similar situations. He emphasized the clinic's capacity to help, stating, "There are so many more out there that can come and see us. We have the capacity for more."

The clinic provides free medical care without restrictions to individuals earning under $39,990 per year, or under $82,500 for a family of four. McGarry expressed deep gratitude for the care she received, saying, "I can't even express how grateful I am to them because I don't know where I'd be." As the clinic continues to serve patients who lost coverage, its leadership encourages others in similar circumstances to reach out for assistance.

A Jensen Beach teenager will represent Team USA at the Sailing World Championship in France this weekend, marking the culmination of years spent competing in one of sailing's most prestigious youth circuits. Harper Aberman's journey in the sport began at age four when her grandparents enrolled her in a four-week summer camp in Pennsylvania. While her cousin disliked the experience, Aberman embraced sailing immediately. "I fell in love with it, and I ended up going for eight weeks," she said of those early summers.

When Aberman's family relocated to Jensen Beach, she joined the green fleet program at the US Sailing Center of Martin County, where her competitive sailing career truly accelerated. "That is where the sailing adventure took off for me," Aberman recalled. She has since become one of the biggest names in the youth sailing circuit, winning countless tournaments and placing in several national competitions. Aberman describes herself as an adrenaline junkie, drawn to sailing's most demanding conditions. She acknowledges the physical challenges of competitive racing, noting that windy conditions have historically caused her to struggle with staying upright and avoiding the water.

The opportunity to compete for Team USA arrived unexpectedly during Spring Break when Aberman received an email confirming her selection. The moment sparked excitement throughout her household. "I started screaming, my parents started screaming, and we were like, 'This is not real. We're going to Europe this summer,'" she said of learning the news. The family's summer plans transformed instantly from routine to extraordinary with the prospect of traveling to France for the international competition.

In preparation for the championship, Aberman has spent the past several weeks training intensively in Spain. She emphasized the significant commitment required to compete at this level, stating that preparation has involved "a lot of recovery and a lot of time and effort out on the water." Despite the demanding training schedule, Aberman expressed confidence that the effort will prove worthwhile, particularly given the prestige of the venue hosting the championship. Racing is scheduled to begin Monday, setting the stage for Aberman to compete alongside the world's top young sailors in her division.

Yesterday, 5:45 PM ET

After nearly two years of legal battles with Big Tech trade associations, Florida's landmark social media law finally took effect following an 11th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals ruling in November. However, most major technology companies covered under the law are not complying with its requirements. HB 3, which bans children under 14 from creating social media accounts and requires parental consent for those aged 14 and 15, passed the Legislature in March 2024 and was signed by Governor Ron DeSantis that same month. The legislation was a signature priority for then-House Speaker Paul Renner, who emphasized at the bill signing that developing brains are vulnerable to addictive technologies.

Attorney General James Uthmeier has pursued aggressive enforcement since the law survived court challenges. He announced that Meta has complied with HB 3 by suspending hundreds of thousands of underage accounts and confirmed the company would implement compliance measures by early May. However, TikTok and Google, which owns YouTube and YouTube Kids, have remained silent on their compliance plans and effectively ignored the law. Uthmeier warned noncompliant platforms face $50,000-per-violation fines that could accumulate into billions of dollars. In June, he sued TikTok for violating HB 3 by allowing underage accounts to persist and failing to secure proper parental consent for older teenagers, stating that TikTok knowingly deceives parents and exposes children to harmful content.

Google's continued silence is particularly significant given YouTube's massive presence in young people's lives. Research from Pew shows that roughly nine in 10 U.S. teenagers use YouTube, more than any other platform, with approximately three-quarters using it daily. The exposure risks are substantial: 46 percent of parents with children age 11 or younger reported their child encountered inappropriate content on YouTube. Even YouTube Kids, marketed for children as young as 4, has been found serving content addressing gender ideology and systemic racism, according to congressional testimony cited by Fox News. These are topics that DeSantis and Uthmeier have both criticized extensively.

At a press conference, Uthmeier directly addressed tech platforms still resisting compliance. "YouTube and others, you got to wake up. This is real and you can save lives," he said. Whether Google, TikTok, Roblox and other platforms will follow Meta's compliance remains uncertain. If holdouts persist in noncompliance, Uthmeier indicated they will face significant financial consequences and urged them to "open up your checkbook."

The suspect now faces multiple charges stemming from the discovery. These charges include trafficking in methamphetamine, possession charges, and methamphetamine sale charges. Under Florida law, the possession of these substances in amounts exceeding certain thresholds automatically elevates charges to trafficking level, which carries much more severe penalties than simple possession. The 100-plus grams seized in this case clearly met that threshold.

This arrest is part of ongoing drug enforcement efforts by law enforcement in the Fort Pierce area. The St. Lucie County Sheriff's Office continues to pursue investigations aimed at disrupting drug trafficking operations within the community. Yesterday, 5:13 PM ET

Governor Ron DeSantis announced Wednesday that Florida will designate the Council on American-Islamic Relations, the Muslim Brotherhood, Antifa, Tren de Aragua, Sinaloa-related groups, and Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps as terrorist organizations under HB 1471, a new state law that took effect July 1. At a Tampa press conference with FDLE Commissioner Mark Glass and Statewide Prosecutor Brad McVay, DeSantis said the state is preparing its first slate of designations under the law, formally titled "Systems of Law and Terrorist Organizations." The law authorizes Florida's Chief of Domestic Security to recommend designations, which must be approved or rejected by the governor and Florida Cabinet by majority vote before taking effect. Designated organizations can face public-funding restrictions, possible corporate dissolution in certain circumstances, and penalties tied to material support. DeSantis said the designations would affect state and local funding, colleges and universities, and Florida's K-12 school-choice scholarship program.

DeSantis framed the announcement as a continuation of a December executive order targeting CAIR and the Muslim Brotherhood that directed state agencies to deny contracts, funding and other public benefits to those providing support to the groups. That order was quickly challenged in federal court and temporarily blocked in March after CAIR, CAIR Florida and civil-rights groups sued, arguing it violated constitutional protections. DeSantis said the new law gives the state better legal footing. The designations are not final until the Cabinet signs off. Attorneys representing CAIR said they expect another court fight, with Scott McCoy, deputy legal director of the Southern Poverty Law Center, stating the U.S. Constitution is supreme in Florida.

DeSantis vetoes bill to raise sovereign immunity caps, blocking first increase in 16 years

Governor Ron DeSantis vetoed legislation that would have modestly raised the limits on what Floridians can receive in court settlements for government negligence without legislative approval.

Yesterday, 6:15 PM ET

Gov. Ron DeSantis vetoed HB 145 on Wednesday, blocking the first increase in 16 years to Florida's sovereign immunity caps. The measure, sponsored by Sarasota Republican Rep. Fiona McFarland with a companion bill by Lake Mary Republican Sen. Jason Brodeur, passed with overwhelming bipartisan support in March. It would have raised caps from $200,000 per person and $300,000 per incident, last set in 2010, to $350,000 per person and $500,000 per incident. The bill also would have allowed local governments to voluntarily settle claims exceeding those caps without legislative approval. DeSantis said raising the caps would generate more unmeritorious lawsuits and that the current system effectively disincentivizes frivolous litigation.

McFarland argued the existing system creates prohibitive obstacles for people harmed by government negligence and produces vastly different outcomes depending on whether the offender was a public or private entity. More than 240 lobbyists registered to speak about the bill, many representing counties, cities and agencies facing budget shortfalls under a proposed property tax amendment on the November ballot. DeSantis countered that the current system works, noting he signed a number of claims bills this year and people have received millions of dollars. State lawmakers consider roughly a dozen claims bills annually. This year, just over a third of 14 such measures cleared both chambers.

Florida Chamber poll finds 64% of state voters very proud to be American during 250th anniversary

A statewide Florida Chamber of Commerce poll shows most Floridians are proud to be Americans during the 250th anniversary of the signing of the Declaration of Independence, though responses fall along partisan and age lines.

Yesterday, 6:00 PM ET

A statewide poll conducted by the Florida Chamber of Commerce shows 64% of Floridians say they are "very proud" to be American during the 250th Anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. Another 12% say they are "somewhat proud," while 12% indicate they are not proud at all. The survey of 604 likely voters was conducted between May 1 and May 9. Mark Wilson, President and CEO of the Florida Chamber of Commerce, said the poll demonstrates that Floridians take pride in the nation, in Florida's national leadership, and in the entrepreneurial spirit that defines America.

Perceptions of American pride fall sharply along partisan lines. Some 89% of Republicans say they are very proud Americans compared to 34% of Democrats, while 58% of no-party voters say the same. About 25% of Democrats say they're not proud at all of being American, compared to only 2% of Republicans and 12% of no-party voters. Age also significantly influenced responses, with 71% of Floridians over 60 saying they're very proud to be American versus just 42% of those under 40. On American history broadly, 74% of Florida voters say Americans should be proud of the nation's history, with only 12% saying they feel ashamed. Republicans led at 91% on this question compared to 51% of Democrats and 71% of no-party voters.

Benjamin Franklin artifact collection opens July 6 at Historical Society of Palm Beach County

One of the largest collections of Benjamin Franklin artifacts will go on display at the Historical Society of Palm Beach County in downtown West Palm Beach starting July 6, with free admission through August.

Yesterday, 5:28 PM ET

One of the largest collections of Benjamin Franklin artifacts will open July 6 at the Historical Society of Palm Beach County in downtown West Palm Beach. Zachary Jacobson of Palm Beach Gardens owns the collection with his brother, and they are displaying it to celebrate America's 250th birthday. The exhibit, titled Benjamin Franklin: Creating an American Icon, features over 50 books written by, printed by or about Franklin, along with work from his time as a printer. More than 120 pieces are on display in total.

Highlights include a grandfather clock originally from Franklin's Philadelphia home, a plate inscribed "B. Franklin," and original medallions made when Franklin was representing France during the American Revolution. The exhibit also features a plaster model for the Libertas Americana medal designed by Franklin, and the glass armonica, which Franklin called his favorite invention. Jacobson's stepfather started the collection 50 years ago. Jacobson and his brother eventually bought the remains of the collection and continued it for 15 years. The exhibit is free to the public and runs through August at 300 N Dixie Highway.
