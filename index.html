The Palm Beach County Library System is encouraging readers of all ages to participate in its 2026 Summer Reading Challenge, themed "Unearth a Story," running through July 31. According to Community Engagement Manager Lindsey McMullen, the program provides free activities, incentives and grand prizes designed to inspire residents to reconnect with reading. To participate, people can register at any library branch, complete an activity log and return it to receive a prize while supplies last. Those who submit their logs also enter an automatic drawing for one of several grand prizes.

The library has created age-appropriate reward options for different groups. Teens ages 12 to 17 and adults have the option of selecting a fidget spinner, chip clip or notebook. Activity logs can be picked up and returned at any branch location.

Beyond the reading challenge itself, each library branch is hosting special events throughout the summer. These activities range from children's story times and craft sessions to adult workshops and wellness programs. The library is also bringing characters to various locations, including Dewey the Library Owl and Penelope Rex, a dinosaur character created specifically for this summer's challenge.

The summer reading initiative aligns with educational research showing that such programs help prevent the summer slide, the learning loss that students can experience during extended breaks from school, according to the American Library Association. The challenge welcomes all county residents and library cardholders. For more information about this summer's reading program or to learn about the special events happening at your local branch, residents can visit their nearest Palm Beach County Library location or contact the library system directly.

Yesterday, 4:49 PM ET

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could deliver tax savings to homeowners while potentially burdening renters, according to recent analysis and local experts. The Florida Policy Institute estimates Martin County would lose more than $67 million in local government revenue if voters approve the amendment in November. Statewide, the same study projects local governments could lose nearly $4.8 billion.

The concern is particularly acute for renters, who make up the majority of people struggling with housing costs. Mike Readling, executive director of Habitat for Humanity of Martin County, reports that 95% of people seeking assistance with rent and bills are renters who would receive no direct benefit from the homestead exemption expansion. "The housing situation is so dire. It's gotten progressively worse. We have families coming in everyday," Readling said. A 2025 Florida Senate analysis underscores the rental market challenge, finding only 24 affordable and available rental units exist for every 100 extremely low-income renters.

Attorney John Tolley outlined the difficult choices facing local governments confronted with revenue shortfalls. They could reduce services, deplete financial reserves, or increase millage rates. While homesteaded homeowners would still benefit from the tax break even if rates rise, landlords without the exemption would face higher tax bills and might pass those costs to renters. Realtor Michael Caputo acknowledged this risk but offered a counterargument. "It shouldn't affect them whatsoever," Caputo said of homesteaded homeowners, though he noted landlords "are just going to have a flow through effect, you know, if they're paying more for their property, they're going to try and increase that on the tenant."

The amendment includes a second provision that could influence rental costs. It would lower the property tax cap on non-homesteaded properties, including commercial apartment complexes, from 10% to 5%. Tolley suggested this reduction might actually decrease rents. Caputo added that municipalities might not raise millage rates at all, and market forces could prevent significant rent increases. "If it doesn't happen for them, then they'll have to readjust their pricing," Caputo said, noting that rental costs are movable as supply and demand fluctuate. Despite these potential offsetting factors, the immediate question remains whether local governments will cut services, use reserves, or raise rates to fill the projected revenue gap.

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could reduce property taxes for homeowners, but the financial impact on renters and local services remains a significant concern. The statewide loss could reach nearly $4.8 billion across all local governments.

The potential revenue loss raises questions about how Martin County and other municipalities will maintain current service levels. The situation could create particular hardship for renters, who would not benefit from the homestead exemption but could see costs rise if landlords pass along higher property taxes. At Habitat for Humanity of Martin County, Executive Director Mike Readling witnesses the housing crisis firsthand. Readling emphasizes that the proposed tax cut offers no relief for the population his organization serves. According to realtor Michael Caputo, homeowners with homestead exemptions would remain protected from millage rate increases, but landlords who lack this exemption could face significant tax bill increases and might raise rents to compensate.

However, some observers see potential benefits in the amendment. The proposal would lower the property tax cap on non-homesteaded properties, including apartment complexes, from 10 percent to 5 percent. Tolley suggests this reduction could pressure landlords to keep rents stable or even lower them to remain competitive. Caputo believes municipalities may avoid raising millage rates altogether, and he argues that market forces could keep rental costs in check even if landlords' expenses increase. He points out that tenants can relocate if rents become unaffordable, forcing landlords to adjust pricing to maintain occupancy rates. Despite these counterarguments, the shortage of affordable housing and the vulnerability of low-income renters suggest the amendment's impacts could be substantial for the community's most economically fragile residents.

Florida university faculty donations tilt heavily Democratic, raising viewpoint diversity concerns

Faculty and staff at Florida's five largest public universities donated nearly nine of every 10 dollars to Democratic candidates and committees during the 2024 federal election cycle, according to an analysis of Federal Election Commission data. The study examined contributions from Florida International University, Florida State University, the University of Central Florida, t...

Yesterday, 8:57 PM ET

Faculty and staff at Florida's five largest public universities donated nearly nine of every 10 dollars to Democratic candidates and committees during the 2024 federal election cycle, according to an analysis of Federal Election Commission data. The study examined contributions from Florida International University, Florida State University, the University of Central Florida, the University of Florida and the University of South Florida.

The Democratic share of individual donations ranged from 71% at FIU to 89% at UCF. At every school, more than 80% of donors who clearly favored one party directed their contributions to Democrats. By dollar amount, faculty and staff donated to Democratic committees at more than twice the rate of Florida donors generally and directed money to Democrats at roughly four-and-a-half times the rate of their fellow Floridians. These patterns did not resemble the surrounding state.

Ryan Owens, director of the Institute for Governance and Civics at FSU, said the concern is not any individual professor but the overall campus climate. When a campus tilts heavily in one direction, the range of ideas it can test and challenge narrows, often without anyone intending it. A recent Yale committee report on trust in higher education concluded that echo chambers do not produce the best teaching, research or scholarship. The analysis has limits. It studied only the 2024 election cycle and only donations exceeding $200, excluding faculty members who contribute less or do not contribute politically.

Owens said universities can broaden hiring searches to reach qualified scholars whose viewpoints are underrepresented on campus. Departments that have drifted toward intellectual uniformity can become more intentional about recruiting a wider range of perspectives. Institutions can also invest in meaningful civil discourse programs so that students regularly encounter strong arguments from multiple sides and learn to engage with them thoughtfully. Such efforts could complement the civic literacy requirements already expected of Florida students.

St. Lucie County has been identified as one of Florida's real estate markets facing elevated risk factors. The county joins other Florida communities that are experiencing concerns about their housing and property markets, according to recent analysis of regional real estate conditions.

While specific details about the risk factors were not provided in available reporting, the designation reflects broader challenges affecting the county's real estate sector. Communities across Florida have faced various pressures that impact property values and market stability, and St. Lucie County appears to be among those experiencing these concerns more acutely than some other areas of the state.

The identification of St. Lucie County as a higher-risk market comes as the community continues to address other local challenges. The settlement resulted from service failures during the pandemic, when residents experienced significant trash collection issues. City officials chose the tax roll distribution method to provide funds directly to properties through an established system.

The waste service problems that led to the settlement had troubled residents for years. During the pandemic, many property owners faced overflowing trash and missed pickups. The city and Waste Pro filed lawsuits against each other in 2021, with the company citing labor shortages while the city argued the company had breached its service contract. The two parties reached a settlement agreement, with Waste Pro paying 24 million dollars in compensation. The city council's decision to distribute funds via the tax roll allows the city to apply credits to property accounts systematically. Council members also voted against using settlement money to reimburse the city for emergency solid waste expenses incurred during the service failures. City officials noted that staff members had worked extensively to collect data and develop distribution scenarios to present recommendations to the council, with the goal of remedying the issues and frustrations residents experienced during that difficult period.

The pantry sits just behind the Hobe Sound Public Library, steps away from Koester's residence. Koester observed that once fuel and grocery prices climbed, the pantry's contents disappeared much faster than she could replenish them. "When gas prices went up and food prices went up it was just emptying about faster than I can replace it," she explained. After Koester reached out for help on Facebook, neighbors stepped up significantly to keep the pantry stocked. The contributions have been consistent and generous, ensuring the shelves remain filled for those who need them. One person even commented anonymously that they had driven from Jupiter specifically to visit the pantry and that access to its contents would help them survive the week. These messages of gratitude and stories of real impact fuel Koester's commitment to maintaining the resource and making sure it remains accessible to everyone in need.

Koester credits the tight-knit nature of Hobe Sound for the pantry's success. "I knew the second I posted on that group that Hobe Sound was going to show up for us because that's what we do, we are a tight knit community," she said. She reflects on how community support has evolved over time. "The saying it takes a village is so true and the villages look a lot different than they used to, but people still show up for each other and people want to help," Koester said. The Smithsonian Marine Ecosystems Exhibit operates in partnership with the Smithsonian Marine Station, aiming to raise public awareness about threats facing oceans while inspiring conservation. The facility is located at 420 Seaway Drive in Fort Pierce and is open to the public year-round. More information can be found at sms.si.edu and on their Smithsonian Marine Station social media channels.

Following police advice, she did not pay and instead filed a dispute with SunPass. A second invoice arrived regardless, and though SunPass eventually closed the dispute, Keswick-Paine could not get confirmation the matter was resolved.

Personal injury attorney Doug Burnetti said the problem is far more common than many realize. Security consultant and retired police officer Gene Petrino said the best way to protect yourself from temp tag theft is to keep thorough records of any police reports and toll disputes. Petrino also recommended destroying temporary tags yourself by cutting them into pieces or surrendering them to the tag agency with your information eliminated. A new law in Florida that took effect last year increased penalties for fraudulent use of temporary tags and made the tags easier for the state to track.
