Fertilizer ban begins Monday in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Starting June 30, residents across Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties will need to stop applying fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen. The ban extends through the rainy season as a measure to minimize nutrient-rich runoff that flows into local waterways. Officials say this runoff can trigger harmful algae blooms in environmentally sensitive areas including the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River.

Jordan Skaggs, who works with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained the purpose behind the restriction. The ban is designed to reduce nutrient pollution during months when heavy rainfall increases the likelihood of fertilizer washing into nearby water systems. According to Skaggs, algae blooms stemming from excess nutrients can have serious consequences. "It can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife," he said. Beyond blocking sunlight, these blooms become problematic at higher concentrations. "At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health."

For residents looking for ways to maintain their gardens and landscaping during the ban period, composting offers a practical solution. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, advocates for this approach. Ricketts noted that composting does not require complicated equipment or significant expense. "It can be like a small trash can," Ricketts said. "That is the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape." By composting organic materials, homeowners can create their own nutrient source while simultaneously reducing chemical runoff during the critical rainy season.

The fertilizer restrictions apply uniformly across all three counties on the Treasure Coast, ensuring a coordinated approach to protecting regional water quality. Residents who need more information about permitted alternatives or have specific questions about the ban should contact the University of Florida's St. Lucie County Extension office. Officials encourage people to plan ahead and prepare alternative landscaping methods before the June 30 start date to avoid any disruptions to their yard maintenance routines.

Florida shark diving operators fight federal feeding ban they say could devastate Treasure Coast tourism

A proposed federal ban on shark feeding off Florida's coast has triggered intense pushback from the state's shark diving industry, which argues the measure will harm conservation-focused tourism businesses without meaningfully improving public safety. Yesterday, 5:55 PM ET

A proposed federal ban on shark feeding off Florida's coast has triggered intense pushback from the state's shark diving industry, which argues the measure will harm conservation-focused tourism businesses without meaningfully improving public safety. Known as the Safe Florida Seas Act, H.R. 3831 has passed the U.S. House and would prohibit shark feeding in federal waters within 200 miles of Florida, though it includes an exception for harvesting operations. Proponents of the bill contend it represents a practical safety step to reduce encounters between humans and sharks.

Dive operators say the legislation unfairly singles out their sector, which consists largely of small businesses and nonprofits centered on education and responsible wildlife encounters, while ignoring practices used in commercial fishing. Nathan Campbell, owner of Emerald Charters, points out that Congress has not sought input from the diving industry about their operations or data. The operators emphasize that their methods differ significantly from fishing practices. They deploy bait underwater during dives rather than chumming at the surface, a technique they say is less likely to train sharks to seek food near boats. Commercial fishing operations, by contrast, can discharge hundreds of gallons of chum at the surface. Campbell stressed that the industry welcomes accountability and simply wants the opportunity to present evidence and explain their practices.

The bill references Hawaii's 2006 ban on baited shark diving in federal waters as a model. However, Florida operators and some researchers argue Hawaii's experience actually serves as a cautionary tale. Dr. Matt Ajemian, an associate research professor at Florida Atlantic University, notes that shark diving in Florida maintains a "near perfect safety record" for customers. Since Hawaii implemented its ban, industry members point out that shark incidents have increased and fatalities have occurred. According to the Western Pacific Regional Fishery Management Council, at least one in four fishing trips now result in sharks biting off portions of the catch.

The diving industry emphasizes that shark tourism generates both economic and conservation benefits for coastal communities. These operations provide therapeutic and recreational experiences while raising public awareness about ocean conservation. Campbell has already sent a letter to Congress urging senators to delay action, commission research, and include all stakeholders, including both tourism operators and fisheries, in developing any new regulations. Campbell stated the industry is "not scared of accountability" and simply wants the chance to prove their position using science-backed evidence rather than having regulations based on what he calls "knee-jerk reactions and fear."

Florida residents will vote this November on whether to increase the homestead exemption, effectively reducing property taxes for homeowners across the state. The proposal requires 60% voter approval to pass, the same threshold that caused a similar measure to fail in 2018. As the election approaches, West Palm Beach residents are expressing sharply divided opinions about whether the tax cut is a sound policy decision.

Supporters of the measure argue it benefits people who permanently reside in Florida as homeowners. Brian Edington, a local resident, said "I think it's a really good idea because it saves the people that really live in Florida, the homeowners." Edington expressed comfort with local governments spending less money and suggested that some services could be reduced without harming quality of life. He pointed to trash collection in his neighborhood as an example, noting that twice-weekly pickup could be scaled back to once weekly without negative effects. Another resident named Stan acknowledged potential concerns about budget cuts but suggested that modest reductions might be acceptable if they provide relief to residents.

Critics worry that reducing government funding could have serious consequences for essential services. Rosabella Olivares expressed concern about what lower revenues might mean for municipalities. "There's going to be less money ... because everything is expensive now," she said. The exact amount cities and counties would lose under the plan remains unclear, and officials have not determined where budget cuts would fall if voters approve the measure. Some residents questioned whether a services-based tax system might be more fair, suggesting people should only pay for services they directly use rather than supporting all government functions through property taxes.

Renters were particularly skeptical about the proposal's benefits. While homeowners generally supported paying lower taxes, renters noted they would likely not gain from the tax cuts since they do not own property. One downtown West Palm Beach renter predicted that landlords would pass along any tax savings to tenants through higher rents rather than sharing the benefits. "Pretty sure they're going to raise the rent on everybody, all the renters," the resident said. This concern highlights how the measure would affect different groups of Floridians differently, with homeowners potentially benefiting while renters and others who do not own property might see little advantage.

Summer fertilizer ban begins June 1 in Martin County

Indian River and St. Lucie counties have adopted the same ban during this four-month period. Anyone using fertilizer products during these months needs to check ingredient labels carefully and avoid...

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Starting June 1, Martin County residents must stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen on their lawns and landscaping. The restriction lasts through September 30. Indian River and St. Lucie counties have adopted the same ban during this four-month period. Anyone using fertilizer products during these months needs to check ingredient labels carefully and avoid any products listing these two nutrients.

The ban exists to address a significant environmental concern. During Florida's rainy season, nutrient-rich fertilizer runoff flows into local waterways and can trigger algae blooms. These blooms damage ecosystems like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. Jordan Skaggs, with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that algae can shade out the beneficial plants that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. When harmful algae blooms reach certain densities, they can release toxins that threaten human health, harm wildlife, and damage the overall health of the ecosystem.

For residents concerned about maintaining their gardens and landscaping during the ban, composting offers a practical alternative. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective way to add nutrients to landscapes without breaking the rules. According to Ricketts, setting up a composting system can be simple and affordable. A small trash can works as an easy and practical option for those looking to maintain their landscape during the restriction period.

The seasonal fertilizer restriction applies uniformly across all three Treasure Coast counties and remains in effect through the end of September. Residents who regularly fertilize their lawns should plan ahead and either use up their current fertilizer supplies before June 1 or switch to alternative methods like composting for the summer months. Understanding the ingredients in any fertilizer products is essential, as any product containing phosphorus or nitrogen violates the ban, regardless of the product type or brand.

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin, and St. Lucie counties will need to stop applying fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen. The restriction continues through September 30, covering the region's rainy season when nutrient runoff poses the greatest environmental risk.

The ban exists to address a serious water quality problem. These blooms damage important waterways including the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River, creating ecological disruptions throughout the region.

The consequences of unchecked algae growth extend beyond cloudy water. According to Skaggs, the blooms "can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife." More concerning, certain types of algae blooms can become toxic at high concentrations. "At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health," Skaggs noted. These toxins create risks for people who use the waterways and for the animals that depend on them for survival.

For those maintaining gardens and landscapes during the ban period, composting offers a practical solution. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommended composting as an effective alternative. According to Ricketts, a compost system functions simply and can be as basic as "a small trash can." A state report warns that nearly the entire 11.5-mile coastline of Jupiter Island is critically eroded, with the ocean potentially breaking through at Peck Lake and splitting the island in half.

Yesterday, 4:42 PM ET

A new state report warns that nearly the entire coastline of Jupiter Island is critically eroded, threatening roads, buildings, drainage systems, natural habitats, wildlife and ancient sites. The 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report found Jupiter Island's 11.5-mile shoreline has eroded so severely the ocean could break through at Peck Lake, potentially splitting the island in half. A storm breached Peck Lake in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers filled it back in with sand. Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society said a future breach could upend ecosystems that depend on a delicate balance of fresh and saltwater to survive.

Perry said coastal marshes and conservation lands, including historical and prehistorical sites, are at risk. According to the report, the Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old archaeological site, is already half lost to erosion. Wildlife refuges like Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park are also threatened. Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said the town continues to fight erosion through constant maintenance. In April 2025, the town completed a major beach renourishment project. Field said the town is reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and will continue to do that as long as there is money to do it. About $3 million in town taxpayer dollars goes toward beach renourishment every year.
