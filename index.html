A Russian national connected to a Delray Beach durable medical equipment company accused of Medicare fraud allegedly continued billing the federal health program through a new business after the original company lost its payments. Nika Machutadze was charged with conspiracy to commit money laundering in January 2026 and arrested in Texas the following month. He remains in jail while federal prosecutors in West Palm Beach move forward with the case. Business records for both Sunshine Senior Solutions and Centurion Superior Medical list Machutadze as an owner.

Mike and Connie Parrett were among dozens of Medicare recipients who reported fraudulent billing after Sunshine Senior Solutions charged Medicare for medical equipment Connie said she never needed or received. "I hadn't even been to a doctor for anything," Connie said about the charges. More than a year later, the Parretts received a new Medicare statement at their Ohio home, this time in Mike's name, from Centurion Superior Medical, a company based in Austin, Texas. "But it's the same kind of thing. It's billing catheters, and actually there were six different bills," Mike said. Federal investigators have connected Centurion to the same operators behind Sunshine Senior Solutions.

A criminal complaint alleges Centurion billed Medicare in 2025 after federal authorities suspended payments to Sunshine Senior Solutions in late 2024. Two of the six charges from Centurion on Mike Parrett's statement were denied, though all charges occurred during 2025. Experts warn that victims of one fraud scheme often become targets for additional schemes. "As we've seen in this case, the criminals work with lists of identities and oftentimes they will just share the identities with other fraudsters," said Dr. David Maimon, a cyberfraud expert who studied Sunshine Senior Solutions.

Maimon recommends that potential victims freeze their credit and consider purchasing identity protection plans. He also pointed to the Justice Department's new National Fraud Enforcement Division, which investigates Medicare fraud cases. "This is all suggesting my opinion that we definitely take the issue of fraud more seriously," Maimon said. The repeated targeting of the same individuals demonstrates the ongoing challenge Medicare fraud poses to vulnerable seniors and the importance of government oversight in protecting beneficiaries.

Treasure Coast Square Mall has served the community for decades, but a redevelopment discussion initiated in 2023 has prompted questions about the facility's future direction. Martin County commissioned a study that year to examine potential changes to the mall, including the addition of apartments, an amphitheater, and green space designed in an outdoor format. County officials emphasized that the study was a planning exercise rather than a commitment to specific changes.

Community shoppers hold varied perspectives on the mall's current state and potential improvements. Liz Permar described the mall as "always been a fun place to go to," while Kimberly Grucza acknowledged it as acceptable but not her preferred destination. Despite their different views, both shoppers expressed support for updates to the facility. Permar said an update would be positive, and Grucza indicated that a facelift would benefit the mall. However, not all residents embrace every redevelopment concept under consideration. Grucza expressed concern about the proposed apartments, noting that the area already has numerous residential options.

Business owners operating inside the mall are closely monitoring the redevelopment discussions due to potential impacts on their operations. Jessica Henry, owner of Plantitude and Flame, expressed hope that any changes would maintain an open concept format for stores. Ron Gustafson, owner of MVP Sport Cards, reported that his store has performed well at its current location, stating the mall store model "has been tremendously successful." However, Gustafson also identified maintenance concerns, including water leaking into his store. He believes the mall requires attention and investment but could recover with proper effort and management, saying it is "in neglect" but "could turn around" with the right approach.

The path forward for Treasure Coast Square remains uncertain. Both Martin County Administrator Don Donaldson and Treasure Coast Square itself issued statements affirming that the 2023 study was only a planning exercise and does not necessarily indicate upcoming changes. Whether this study will eventually lead to actual redevelopment depends on decisions yet to be made. The mall's significance as a long-standing community institution means any substantial modifications would likely affect both the shoppers who frequent it and the business owners who depend on it for their livelihoods.

The Cornell Art Museum at Old School Square in Delray Beach is showcasing "Persistence of Matter," a new summer exhibition dedicated to artists who transform discarded materials into compelling artwork. The show reflects the museum's commitment to blending artistic expression with positive community engagement, according to Marusca Gatto, Cultural Arts Director for the Delray Beach Downtown Development Authority. Guest curators Mavis Benson and Pamela Lambie selected artists creating sculptures, installations, and mixed media works entirely from reclaimed objects.

The exhibition highlights several notable artists, including Autumn Kioti, whose creations draw inspiration from both natural and urban environments. Another featured artist, Mike Silverman, presents his "Self-Reflection" series, which transforms discarded computer hard drive platters and printed circuit boards into reflective sculptures. Silverman explained that his pieces are designed to help viewers reconsider their relationship with technology and waste, inviting them to see themselves reflected in materials typically destined for landfills.

As environmental concerns grow across communities, this exhibition serves as a visual conversation about sustainability and responsible consumption. "Persistence of Matter" demonstrates how artistic innovation can highlight the ongoing life cycles of discarded materials while promoting environmental awareness through creative beauty. The show encourages visitors to think differently about waste and its potential for transformation into meaningful art.

The exhibition runs through September 10, 2026, and includes several programming opportunities for deeper engagement. Visitors can attend artist talks, participate in salon discussions, and watch film screenings throughout the exhibition period. The museum maintains regular hours: Wednesday noon to 5 p.m., Thursday and Friday noon to 7 p.m., Saturday 10 a.m. to 5 p.m., and Sunday noon to 5 p.m. For more information about upcoming events and programs, visitors can call 561-654-2220.

The Agriculture, Rural Development, Food and Drug Administration, and Related Agencies Appropriations Act passed the House on a narrow 213-210 vote, with Florida's congressional delegation divided along party lines. Republican supporters highlighted the bill's benefits for the state's agricultural sector. Rep. Scott Franklin, a Lakeland Republican, said the legislation strengthens agricultural research and animal and plant health programs while addressing threats like bird flu and invasive pests. He emphasized that agriculture remains central to Central Florida's economy and way of life, noting that farmers face challenges from rising costs, foreign competition, and severe weather.

The bill allocates $72 million for citrus health response, with $11 million specifically targeting citrus greening disease. Rep. Mario Diaz-Balart, a Hialeah Republican and Vice Chair of the House Appropriations Committee, praised the legislation for ensuring the safety of food and medical products while reinforcing consumer protections. He also highlighted provisions that strengthen national security by safeguarding against foreign agricultural interests and prioritizing research to keep the domestic agricultural sector competitive. Rep. Vern Buchanan, a Longboat Key Republican, secured $2 million to combat animal fighting, calling it a brutal practice linked to criminal activity.

However, every Florida Democrat voted against the legislation, citing concerns about food assistance programs. Rep. Frederica Wilson, a Miami-Dade Democrat, said the bill fails to fully fund the Women, Infants, and Children program and threatens access to the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program. She warned that these cuts harm pregnant women and babies while weakening support for vulnerable Americans. Wilson also objected to provisions she described as targeting LGBTQ individuals and families. Rep. Greg Steube, a Sarasota Republican, also voted against the budget, objecting to funding for organizations including Save the Children and CARE, which he said promote initiatives he opposes, including LGBTQ advocacy and abortion access.

Rep. Daniel Webster, a Clermont Republican who supported the bill, secured $1.5 million for the City of Webster's Rural Water and Waste Disposal Program and championed language supporting the Rural Electrification and Telecommunications Loan Program. He characterized the legislation as supporting farmers and ranchers while promoting food safety and reducing reliance on foreign competitors like China.

St. Lucie County implements 45-day anchoring limits in Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor

St.

Yesterday, 5:58 PM ET

St. Lucie County implemented new restrictions for boaters anchoring in county waterways on June 1. The county established two anchor limitation areas in the Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor near the South Causeway Bridge. Under the new rules, a person may not anchor a vessel for more than 45 consecutive days in any six-month period in any anchoring limitation area without leaving all anchoring limitation areas for at least 24 hours.

Erick Gill with St. Lucie County said the county has been seeing more abandoned vessels in these waterways. Last year, FWC collected around two dozen derelict vessels. Gill said the new restrictions will help make the waterways more navigable and ensure that the highway for vessels remains clear and free for other vessels to come and go. James Beams with St.Lucie County Port, Inlet and Beaches said abandoned vessels sitting for too long could impact water quality due to concerns with diesel and oil tanks and holding tanks. Gill said the restrictions will help avoid those pollutants from getting into the water. Over 4,000 species of animals, plants and birds live in the lagoon, Gill said, and the county wants to make sure it is protecting it.

Governor DeSantis signs bills sending Hillsborough County government reforms to November ballot

Governor Ron DeSantis signed two bills Monday that will let Hillsborough County voters decide in November whether to expand the County Commission and elect thei.

4:47 PM ET

Governor Ron DeSantis signed two bills Monday that will send major Hillsborough County government reforms to the November 2026 ballot. The measures, sponsored by Apollo Beach Republican Rep. Michael Owen, ask voters whether to reshape county government and how the local school district chooses its superintendent. HB 4029 proposes expanding the Hillsborough County Commission from seven members to nine and eliminating countywide seats in favor of single-member districts. The board would later grow to 11 members if the county's population reaches at least 2.5 million residents. The proposal would also require a "majority plus one" vote of the full Commission for certain key actions, including appointing or removing the County Administrator, County Attorney and County Internal Auditor, and proposing charter amendments. It would revise the composition and appointment process for the county's Charter Review Board and alter rules for citizen-led charter amendments.

HB 4027 asks voters whether to replace the district's appointed superintendent with an elected one. If approved, the Hillsborough County Public Schools Superintendent would be chosen in a partisan countywide election beginning in 2028 and serve a four-year term. Supporters of both measures argue the current structure dilutes local representation in a county with more than 1.5 million residents and makes countywide races more expensive. The Superintendent proposal drew sharp debate, with supporters saying voters should have direct say over the leader of one of the state's largest school districts, while opponents warned it could politicize the role and pull focus from the district's educational mission.

County Administrator Joe Abruzzo announced during a budget meeting that if the property tax reduction passes, the county's budget for 30 departments could drop from $609 million to approximately $210 million, a loss of more than 65% of current funding. The Palm Beach County Fire Department alone stands to lose just under $200 million in funding under the proposed tax structure. Despite the magnitude of the proposed reductions, Abruzzo expressed confidence in the county's ability to continue operations, saying the cuts would not throw any types of services out of whack and would be extremely fiscally responsible.

A major concern centers on the proposed $250,000 homestead exemption, which may not represent the final figure. Abruzzo warned commissioners the exemption could potentially increase to $500,000, further deepening the budget shortfall. County leaders are examining millage rates and identifying cost-saving opportunities, with particular attention on the Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office budget, which has increased more than 216% over the past 20 years. To help offset the shortfall, commissioners agreed to implement beach parking fees as a revenue-generating measure. Commissioners will set the maximum millage rate in July at 4.5, with final property value estimates and the complete budget scheduled for approval in September.
