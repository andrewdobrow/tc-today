Yesterday, 6:02 PM ET

Gov. Ron DeSantis signed new legislation this week that restricts how cities, counties and special districts can increase property tax revenue. The laws require local governments to stay closer to the rollback rate, which is set to generate roughly the same property tax revenue as the previous year, and mandate larger voter approval margins to exceed that rate. Florida Chief Financial Officer Blaise Ingoglia emphasized that "we need government at all levels to start thinking about spending cuts," signaling the state's commitment to limiting revenue growth.

Those who support the new restrictions characterize them as essential protections for taxpayers and argue that local governments have allowed spending to grow too rapidly as property values increase. However, local officials express concern that the new constraints, when combined with a proposed constitutional amendment on the November ballot, could create significant shortfalls in city and county budgets. The amendment, designated as Amendment 3, would expand homestead exemptions on non-school property taxes, increasing the exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and then to $250,000 in 2028. Proponents argue this change would provide substantial relief to homeowners, while opponents worry that the tax savings some residents receive could result in service reductions, increased fees, or property tax increases in other areas.

The property tax debate has intensified with a new outside political campaign launching efforts to convince voters to reject the amendment. This campaign adds another significant element to what observers expect will be one of Florida's most closely watched and contentious ballot measures this autumn. The combination of the recently signed restrictions and the proposed amendment represents a major shift in how local governments throughout Florida, including those on the Treasure Coast, will be able to fund essential services moving forward.

These changes reflect a broader conservative push to limit government spending and property tax increases at the local level. As communities across the state prepare for November's election, the debate over how to balance taxpayer relief with adequate public funding remains a central issue. Local governments must now navigate stricter revenue requirements while anticipating additional limitations if voters approve Amendment 3.

The closure of the Alligator Alcatraz detention facility in the Florida Everglades has left detainees dispersed to immigration centers nationwide, creating significant obstacles for those fighting their cases. According to immigration attorney Richard Hujber, individuals have been transferred to facilities in Louisiana, Texas, New Mexico, and Washington. "I had a client moved all the way to Tacoma, Washington, like it can't get farther than that," Hujber said. The geographic separation adds another layer of difficulty for families already struggling with the immigration process.

The scattering of detainees across different regions complicates legal representation and bond hearings. Hujber explained that immigration law varies significantly depending on location. "There's different laws in different circuit courts in different parts of the country," he said, noting that some courts have rejected bond requests that might have succeeded elsewhere. This inconsistency in rulings leaves detainees uncertain about their prospects for release. For many, the inability to secure bond raises larger questions about whether continuing to fight for residency or asylum status is worthwhile, with some considering self-deportation as their only option.

The facility's closure marks the end of nearly a year of operation. Governor Ron DeSantis reported that approximately 21,000 people were deported from the facility during its time in operation. However, not all detainees faced deportation. Anna Maria Hernandez's cousin was released just before the facility shut down and now lives under an order of supervision, which permits him to work, drive, and reside in the United States. Hernandez expressed her relief at her cousin's release while acknowledging the emotional toll the detention had taken on their family. "We lived every day wondering if our family is safe," she said, urging others not to endure similar suffering.

The future remains uncertain for many former detainees and their families. Hujber emphasized that while families want to continue fighting their cases, the burden ultimately falls on the detainees themselves to pursue their legal options. The Baker County facility in North Florida continues operating, but for those transferred from Alligator Alcatraz, the path forward is unclear. The closure has left hundreds of individuals separated from family support systems and navigating an immigration system with inconsistent standards, raising questions about fairness and access to justice across different regions of the country.

Anne Bendel of West Boca Raton has encountered multiple obstacles in her attempt to receive a state grant designed to help homeowners strengthen their homes against storms. The persistent delays have tested her patience as she navigates the My Safe Florida Home program, which typically awards grants up to $10,000 to eligible homeowners.

Bendel first reached out to WPTV in May regarding her application, which was being delayed due to missing paperwork from her insurance company. After resolving that initial issue, representatives with My Safe Florida Home informed her in May that her application status was complete. However, just one week later, the program requested additional documentation, specifically a permit of completion for a large impact window that Bendel had installed. She promptly provided the requested document, but new questions then arose about dates on her paperwork. Her application has since been sent back for further review, leaving the status of her grant funding uncertain. She paid $8,000 out of pocket for the home improvements and is hoping to recover some of those costs through the grant.

The My Safe Florida Home program has been instrumental in assisting thousands of homeowners throughout Florida by providing financial support for storm-resistant upgrades and helping them qualify for insurance discounts. However, Bendel's experience has undermined her confidence in the program's reliability. She described the process as particularly challenging because she lacks access to a computer and must handle all communications through her phone without assistance from others. "It's gut-wrenching," she said about managing the application process under these constraints. Despite completing all her required work, obtaining necessary permits, and securing the insurance coverage the program asked for, Bendel believes the program continues to introduce new requirements. She expressed frustration about what she perceives as unfair treatment, suggesting the program may be creating additional obstacles once homeowners have already finished their projects.

WPTV contacted the Florida Department of Financial Services in Tallahassee to inquire about Bendel's case and the current status of her grant application. Bendel, who describes herself as persistent and thorough, maintains that she has fulfilled every requirement presented to her. Her situation highlights potential challenges within the grant application process that may warrant further examination by state officials overseeing the program.

He is now under an order of supervision, which permits him to work, drive, and live in the United States. Despite the closure of Alligator Alcatraz, the fight for many detainees is far from over. The Baker County facility in North Florida remains operational, and uncertainty continues to cloud the futures of former detainees scattered across the country. Governor Ron DeSantis reported that approximately 21,000 people were deported from Alligator Alcatraz during its operation. Palm Beach County, home to 68,400 Haitian immigrants, faces upheaval after the Supreme Court sided with the Trump administration to eliminate Temporary Protected Status for thousands from Haiti and Syria.

Yesterday, 6:33 PM ET

The Supreme Court has sided with President Donald Trump's administration in eliminating Temporary Protected Status for thousands of people from Haiti and Syria, leaving hundreds of thousands at risk of deportation, including many in South Florida. Temporary Protected Status allows foreign citizens already in the United States to stay, work, and raise their families while their home countries deal with turmoil from natural disasters, political unrest, or violence. According to the Migration Policy Institute, Florida has the largest Haitian immigrant population in the United States, with Palm Beach County ranking third statewide and nationally at 68,400 Haitians, behind Broward County with 86,100 and Miami-Dade County with 78,800.

Stecko Dulcio, who has operated a Haitian restaurant in Riviera Beach for five and a half years, expressed alarm about the decision. Dulcio, a U.S. citizen, stated that gangs have taken over Haiti, claiming houses, controlling transportation, and fueling violence. Attorney Byrnes Guillaume, who is Haitian and represents many people losing TPS status, explained that elimination means people have no legal standing, can no longer work, and must go into the shadows. The ruling impacts roughly 350,000 Haitians and 6,000 Syrians nationwide. Since January of last year, the Department of Homeland Security has ended protections for people from more than 13 countries.

Clyburn said Meek understands the stakes because he has been active in the fight for voting rights and equality. Clyburn noted that Republicans across the South are working to weaken Black political power and erase Black representation in Congress, including in his own district. Meek carries significant family ties to the seat. Other candidates include Miami-Dade Commissioner Oliver Gilbert, state Sen. Shevrin Jones, physician-veteran Rudy Moise, and former County Commissioner Jean Monestime.
