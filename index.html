The School District of Indian River County announced Tuesday that students at six schools will have access to free meals during the 2026-2027 school year. The district is utilizing federal nutrition programs, specifically the National School Lunch Program and School Breakfast Program, to eliminate meal costs for eligible students and help families manage daily expenses.

Fellsmere Academy will be the most comprehensive of the participating schools, offering free breakfast and lunch to all students throughout the entire school year. Five other campuses will provide free breakfast at no cost to their student bodies. These schools include Pelican Island Classical Magnet, Citrus Elementary, Dodgertown Elementary, Vero Beach Elementary, and Indian River Academy. Students at these five schools will still need to pay for lunch unless they qualify for additional assistance programs.

Families whose students do not attend one of these six schools may still qualify for free or reduced-price meals based on their household size and income. The district is sending application letters to all homes with parents or guardians in the school district. Interested families can complete an online meal application and submit it to their child's school to determine eligibility for assistance programs based on financial need.

For more information about the meal programs, families can reach out to Shawn O'Keefe, the district's nutrition services director. O'Keefe can be contacted at (772) 564-5041 or shawn.okeefe@indianriverschools.org. The School District of Indian River County offices are located at 6055 62nd Ave in Vero Beach for those who prefer to apply in person or need additional assistance with the application process.

Yesterday, 10:19 PM ET

Diana Chaparro Contreras, 33, and Sandra Peralta Aguilar, 50, are facing multiple charges including human trafficking and profiting from or arranging prostitution. Both women are currently held in the Palm Beach County Jail. Authorities say they operated a trafficking scheme out of a Lake Worth Beach residence where a California woman was recruited under false pretenses and forced into sexual exploitation.

The investigation began in January when the victim responded to a housekeeping job posting on Indeed and was offered $35 per hour. After accepting the position, she was flown to Florida and brought to a home on S. M Street in Lake Worth Beach. Upon arrival, her passport, Social Security card, and work permit were confiscated. The victim reported being threatened with deportation and coerced into sexual acts with men who visited the residence. She was confined to a room with boarded windows and allegedly forced to engage in sexual activity with seven men before managing to escape. The victim used a lamp to break through the plywood covering the windows. She also reported that a possible 15-year-old girl was present at the location.

Law enforcement tracked the operation over several months, observing multiple men entering and leaving the property. In April, authorities discovered the operation had relocated to another address approximately one mile away. Financial records revealed that Chaparro handled more than $177,000 in cash and bank transactions associated with the trafficking scheme.

Laura Cusack, an advocate with the Human Trafficking Coalition of the Palm Beaches, emphasized that such operations can occur anywhere in the community. "It doesn't just happen in a vacuum or in the underground world. It could happen in the house next door," Cusack said. She stressed that community awareness serves as one of the most effective tools for identifying trafficking activity. Cusack noted that neighbors and community members who recognize suspicious patterns, such as unusually high foot traffic, can make a critical difference in stopping exploitation. "These predators are in our community. They can target our children so it just takes one person to make the call, report what they see and that can make all the difference," Cusack said. Anyone who suspects human trafficking is encouraged to contact the Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office or reach out to the Human Trafficking Coalition of the Palm Beaches at 561-267-5783 or htcoalitionpb@gmail.com.

Yesterday, 6:08 PM ET

The Continuum Company, a New York-based developer, has proposed Singer Island Gateway, a mixed-use project valued at approximately $14 million at 2525 Lake Drive. The development would feature hundreds of residential units, commercial and public space, and a marina near the base of Blue Heron Bridge at the entrance to Singer Island in Palm Beach County. The main structure would rise 25 stories above the waterfront location.

The project has drawn attention because it would be situated above Phil Foster Park, an underwater ecosystem that attracts divers and snorkelers from around the world. Visitors to the area describe the marine environment in glowing terms. "A few stingrays, some like rainbow fish," one visitor noted. Another described "blue and yellow fish, and some pink coral," while a third mentioned seeing "yellow coral, which I've never seen before, and striped fish." One diver called it simply "beautiful, amazing." The reef's global reputation stems from the unique combination of marine life found in these shallow waters beneath the bridge.

Longtime resident Gary Heiser, who grew up in the area, opposes the development based on both its scale and potential environmental impact. He believes the 25-story tower is inappropriate for the location, saying "It's way too drastic for this location. Twenty-five stories would be okay in Chicago, but it doesn't work right here." Beyond aesthetic concerns, Heiser worries that construction activity, shade from the tall building, and the marina could harm the marine ecosystem. He expressed skepticism about the developer's environmental conclusions, stating "I find it hard to believe that there'd be no impact. I would like to see more research."

The developer has published an environmental study on its website in which a geoscientist concluded the project would have no impact on marine life in the Lake Worth Lagoon, with the ecosystem remaining unchanged. Not all area visitors share Heiser's concerns. One diver indicated his position would depend on whether the project actually threatens the reef, saying "If it doesn't disturb the reef, I don't see a problem with it."

The project has not yet received approval. The developer was contacted for additional comment, with a response pending. Meanwhile, residents have organized a petition against the proposal.

10:40 AM ET

The Supreme Court on Tuesday upheld state laws barring transgender girls and women from playing on school athletic teams, in another setback for transgender people. The court's conservative majority ruled that state bans in Idaho and West Virginia don't violate the Constitution or the federal law known as Title IX, which prohibits sex discrimination in education. More than two dozen other Republican-led states have adopted bans on female transgender athletes, and the decision extends to them as well.

Becky Pepper-Jackson, a 16-year-old high school sophomore in Bridgeport, West Virginia, has been taking puberty-blocking medication, has publicly identified as a girl since age 8 and has been issued a West Virginia birth certificate recognizing her as female. She is the only transgender person who has sought to compete in girls sports in West Virginia. Pepper-Jackson has progressed from a back-of-the-pack cross-country runner in middle school to statewide champion in the shot put. She beat the second-place finisher by two feet in last month's West Virginia championship meet. In the Idaho case, Lindsay Hecox sued over the state's first-in-the-nation ban for the chance to try out for the women's track and cross-country teams at Boise State University in Idaho. She didn't make either squad because she was too slow, her lawyer Kathleen Hartnett told the court during arguments in January, but she competed in club-level soccer and running.

Idaho's law, state Solicitor General Alan Hurst said, is necessary for fair competition because, where sports are concerned, men and women are obviously not the same. Lawyers for Pepper-Jackson argued that such distinctions generally make sense but that their client has none of those advantages because of the unique circumstances of her early transition. NCAA president Charlie Baker told Congress in 2024 that he was aware of only 10 transgender athletes out of more than half a million students on college teams. Despite the small numbers, the issue has taken on outsize importance. Olympic and Paralympic Committees banned transgender women from women's sports after President Donald Trump, a Republican, signed an executive order aimed at barring their participation.

An Associated Press-NORC Center for Public Affairs Research poll conducted in October 2025 found that about 6 in 10 U.S. adults strongly or somewhat favored requiring transgender children and teenagers to compete only on sports teams that match the sex they were assigned at birth, not the gender they identify with, while about 2 in 10 were strongly or somewhat opposed and about one-quarter did not have an opinion. About 2.1 million adults, or 0.8%, and 724,000 people ages 13 to 17, or 3.3%, identify as transgender in the U.S., according to the Williams Institute at the UCLA School of Law.

Supreme Court upholds birthright citizenship, rejecting Trump limits on children of undocumented immigrants

The Supreme Court on Tuesday upheld a broad conception of birthright citizenship, rejecting President Donald Trump's executive order that would have denied citizenship to children born to people who are in the United States illegally or temporarily.

A few minutes ago

The justices relied on a long-settled understanding of the 14th Amendment, adopted after the Civil War, and more recent federal laws in ruling that anyone born in the country, with very limited exceptions, is a citizen. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote for the court that citizenship was the right to have rights and that the Framers of the Fourteenth Amendment extended that promise to every free-born person in this land. Three conservative justices would have allowed the restrictions to take effect.

The Republican president's restrictions had been blocked by several lower courts and had not taken effect anywhere in the U.S. During arguments in April, both conservative and liberal justices questioned the order's legality in a momentous case that was magnified by Trump's unprecedented attendance in the courtroom. Trump's order would have upended widely held views that the 14th Amendment confers citizenship on everyone born in the U.S., excluding only the children of foreign diplomats and those born to a foreign occupying force. The amendment was intended to ensure that Black people, including former slaves, had citizenship, though the Citizenship Clause is written more broadly.

More than one-quarter of a million babies born in the U.S. each year would have been affected by the executive order, according to research by the Migration Policy Institute and Pennsylvania State University's Population Research Institute. While Trump has largely focused on illegal immigration in his rhetoric and actions, the birthright citizenship restrictions also would have applied to people who are legally in the United States, including students and applicants for green cards, or permanent resident status.

A few minutes ago

Supreme Court strikes down federal limits on political party spending in coordination with candidates

The Supreme Court on Tuesday erased limits on how much political parties can spend in coordination with candidates for Congress and president, striking down a federal election law that is more than 50 years old.

A few minutes ago

Prodded by a Republican-led lawsuit that includes Vice President JD Vance, the court's conservative justices were again in the majority of the latest decision that upended congressionally enacted limits on raising and spending money to influence elections. The limits on party spending stem from a desire to prevent large donors from skirting caps on individual contributions to a candidate by directing unlimited sums to the party, with the understanding that the money will be spent on behalf of the candidate. The Supreme Court had previously upheld the limits in 2001.

The Republican committees for House and Senate candidates filed the lawsuit in Ohio in 2022, joined by Vance, then a senator from Ohio, and then-Rep. Byron Donalds. After President Donald Trump took office for his second term, the Federal Election Commission dropped its defense of the law and joined with Republicans in urging that it be overturned. Democrats had called on the court to uphold the law, even though there is wide agreement that the spending limits have hurt political parties in an era of unlimited spending by other organizations. Last year, the coordinated party spending for Senate races ranged from $127,200 in several states with small populations to nearly $4 million in California, the most populous state. For House races, the limits were $127,200 in states with only one representative and $63,600 everywhere else.

DeSantis cuts $2.2 million for low-income senior services in South Florida despite statewide increases

Gov. Ron DeSantis vetoed more than $2.2 million in funding for senior centers, meals-on-wheels programs and other services for low-income elderly residents in South Florida, part of $95 million in local South Florida project cuts.

A few minutes ago

Of more than $95 million DeSantis slashed from local South Florida projects this week, part of $1.6 billion in total vetoes in the coming $117.6 billion state budget, more than $2.2 million will affect the area's low-income seniors. The cuts went both right and left, with about as many Republican sponsors of the projects as Democrats. Miami-Dade saw twice as many vetoes as Broward, which had twice as many as Palm Beach County. While funding was removed for specific seniors-specific projects in the region, DeSantis increased overall spending on Florida's elderly residents, adding $7.5 million for a total of $131 million to increase services through the Community Care for the Elderly and Home Care for the Elderly programs, and $3 million more for a total of $73.1 million to serve additional seniors through Florida's Alzheimer's Disease Initiative.

The biggest-ticket appropriation to lose funding was a $600,000 set-aside for the construction of a multipurpose, weather-protected outdoor space for economically disadvantaged seniors enrolled in West Miami's hot meals program. Sen. Ileana Garcia and Coral Gables Republican Rep. Demi Busatta filed matching appropriation requests for the shovel-ready project, which received $300,000 from the state last year. DeSantis also vetoed $500,000 meant for the Age Well program, which provides nursing, therapy, social work and physician home visits for low- to moderate-income seniors who are not eligible for long-term care funding.
