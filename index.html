Florida Supreme Court adopts new AI courtroom rules after rampant misuse

Florida's highest court has adopted new rules requiring attorneys and self-represented litigants to certify that any evidence or case law they present in court actually exists and is accurate. The rules take effect June 15 in response to what legal and artificial intelligence experts describe as a growing crisis of AI misuse in courtrooms. Violators could face sanctions ranging...

Yesterday, 12:04 PM ET

Florida's highest court has adopted new rules requiring attorneys and self-represented litigants to certify that any evidence or case law they present in court actually exists and is accurate. The rules take effect June 15 in response to what legal and artificial intelligence experts describe as a growing crisis of AI misuse in courtrooms. Violators could face sanctions ranging from public reprimand to fines and suspension.

The problem has become serious enough that Florida's Supreme Court felt compelled to act. Federal Judge William Matthewman, chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has worked to address AI misuse in his own courtroom and remains concerned about the trajectory. "Unfortunately, I see it getting worse before it gets better. I think the Florida Supreme Court's order will help in Florida, but I unfortunately have seen over the last couple of years that it is growing and growing and growing," Matthewman said. He cautioned that lawyers and self-represented parties should verify everything they file. "I would caution lawyers and parties representing themselves to make sure everything you file in court is accurate, because if not, you could find yourself in hot water," he said.

A recent case in Polk County illustrates the problem. An attorney representing the Polk County School District filed a motion to dismiss in a school bus camera lawsuit containing fabricated facts and citations to cases that do not exist. The attorney apologized and stated he used ChatGPT as a drafting and research aid but had no excuse for failing to verify the information before filing. The school district said it takes accuracy and integrity seriously and is reviewing whether the attorney will continue receiving contracted work. Paul Figueroa, a Tampa criminal defense attorney who recently began using AI to improve his workflow, acknowledged the technology's limitations. "It can be something as simple as it gives a quote that's directly on point to my case, and you go, all right, this is brilliant, this is what I've been looking for, I could have searched 300 cases and not come up with anything better than this, and then you realize that's not in the case," Figueroa said. When asked if the new rules go far enough, he replied, "I do. I think it goes far enough for what we know now."

The consequences of AI misuse have extended beyond courtroom documents. Earlier this year, a Florida woman was jailed after a fake AI-generated text was used against her, though she was later cleared of all charges. In another case, a St. Petersburg woman created an AI-generated photo of a man she falsely claimed had burglarized and raped her. She later admitted the claim was fabricated, telling police, "I lied about it."

Fertilizer Ban Begins June 1 in Martin, St. Lucie, and Indian River Counties

Starting June 1, residents and businesses in Martin, St. Lucie, and Indian River counties must stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen. The seasonal restriction continues through the rainy season and applies to both residential and commercial applications. Anyone maintaining a lawn, garden, or landscaped area in these three counties will need to put fertilize...

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Starting June 1, residents and businesses in Martin, St. Lucie, and Indian River counties must stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen. The seasonal restriction continues through the rainy season and applies to both residential and commercial applications. Anyone maintaining a lawn, garden, or landscaped area in these three counties will need to put fertilizer use on hold until the ban is lifted.

The ban targets a significant environmental concern in the region. Jordan Skaggs, who works with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that nutrient-rich runoff during heavy rains can trigger algae blooms in local waterways including the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. These blooms create serious problems for aquatic ecosystems. They can block sunlight from reaching plants that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. In some cases, when algae blooms reach high concentrations, they produce toxins that threaten not only wildlife but also human health and the overall health of the ecosystem.

The counties implemented this ban as a preventive measure during the season when rainfall is heaviest. The nitrogen and phosphorus in fertilizers act as nutrients that fuel algae growth in waterways. By restricting these products during months when runoff is most likely to occur, the counties hope to reduce the amount of these nutrients reaching local rivers and lagoons. This approach targets the source of the problem rather than trying to manage blooms after they develop.

For people who want to maintain their landscapes during the ban, alternatives exist. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as a practical solution. According to Ricketts, composting provides nutrients to gardens and landscaping in a way that works within the seasonal restrictions. He noted that even something as simple as a small trash can can serve as an effective composting system. This method offers an accessible way for homeowners and landscapers to keep their plants healthy while supporting the environmental goals of the fertilizer ban. Those looking to learn more about composting techniques and other alternatives can reach out to local extension offices for guidance on maintaining their landscapes responsibly during the restricted season.

Starting June 1, residents across Martin, St. Lucie and Indian River counties will need to stop applying fertilizer to their lawns and landscaping. The seasonal restriction will remain in effect through September 30 and applies to all fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen. Jordan Skaggs, an official with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, confirmed that the ban covers these nutrient-based products during the region's rainy season.

The ban exists to protect local waterways from nutrient-rich runoff that accumulates during heavy summer rains. When excess nutrients enter water systems, they can trigger algae blooms in sensitive ecosystems like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. According to Skaggs, these algae blooms create several environmental problems. The excess algae can block sunlight and shade out the beneficial plants that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. In more serious cases, harmful algae blooms reach densities where they release toxins into the water, which can damage human health, harm wildlife populations and destabilize the overall ecosystem balance.

For residents who still want to nourish their gardens and landscaping during the fertilizer ban, Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as a practical alternative. Ricketts explained that composting provides the necessary nutrients to landscapes without relying on commercial fertilizers. He noted that residents don't need elaborate composting systems to make this work. A simple approach involves using a small trash can as a basic composting container, which Ricketts described as the easiest and most practical method for adding nutrients to landscaping during the restriction period.

The four-month ban represents a regional effort to improve water quality during the season when rainfall is heaviest and runoff poses the greatest threat to local waterways. Residents who maintain gardens and outdoor spaces should plan accordingly by transitioning to composting methods before June 1 arrives. This approach allows people to continue caring for their landscapes while supporting the environmental protection goals that drive the seasonal restriction.

State officials are carefully monitoring the situation to determine whether a statewide fireworks ban will be necessary. The Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services has indicated that current drought severity exceeds conditions from 1998, the last time the state implemented such a ban.

Every county in Florida is experiencing at least abnormally dry conditions. Palm Beach County and the Treasure Coast face particularly challenging circumstances, with areas there dealing with moderate to extreme drought conditions. In May, Florida Agriculture Commissioner Wilton Simpson provided an early warning about the possibility of restricting fireworks. Simpson emphasized the scale of the potential risk, stating "If we're still in drought conditions come Fourth of July, absolutely. He acknowledged the historic importance of the 250th anniversary celebration but encouraged residents to consider other ways to mark the occasion.

Despite the concerning outlook, some businesses remain cautiously optimistic about the situation. Sky King Fireworks in West Palm Beach has already experienced higher customer traffic as people shop early for the holiday. Bill Murphy from the store explained that the business closely tracks drought conditions to ensure adequate ground moisture levels. Murphy noted that weather forecasts suggest significant rainfall ahead, which could help alleviate the current crisis. The store continues to monitor the drought index carefully while maintaining hope that conditions will improve.

The Department of Agriculture has made clear that implementing a fireworks ban would represent a last resort option if conditions do not improve. State leaders are actively watching drought developments as the Fourth of July approaches to make their final decision about restrictions. The decision will ultimately depend on whether conditions improve before the holiday arrives. Florida's communities, both coastal and inland, remain united in dealing with the statewide drought that affects every region.

Jupiter Island erosion so severe ocean could breach island at Peck Lake, state warns

The threat extends from Blowing Rocks Preserve, where private development already faces risk, to Peck Lake, where a brea...

Yesterday, 4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report has documented critical erosion across nearly the entire 11.5-mile coastline of Jupiter Island, raising concerns that a future ocean breakthrough at Peck Lake could split the barrier island in half. The threat extends from Blowing Rocks Preserve, where private development already faces risk, to Peck Lake, where a breach would transform a narrow strip of land into an open inlet. This scenario has happened before: a storm breached Peck Lake in the early 1960s until the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers refilled the area with sand.

According to Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society, a future breach would disrupt ecosystems dependent on a precise balance between fresh and saltwater. The state report identifies numerous sites at risk, including the Joseph Reed Mound, an archaeological site between 3,000 and 4,000 years old that has already lost half its area to erosion. Three wildlife refuges face similar threats: Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park. Perry noted that more than 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit these areas. A second state assessment warns that erosion is also destroying mangrove swamps essential for habitat and coastal protection, while rising water levels could displace entire species on the island.

The Town of Jupiter Island combats erosion through continuous maintenance efforts. In April 2025, officials completed a major beach renourishment project that deposited enough sand to equal nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said no oceanfront homes face immediate danger only because of ongoing work. He described the situation candidly: "We are just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over. We will continue to do that as long as there's money to do it." The town currently spends about $3 million annually in taxpayer dollars on beach renourishment.

The sustainability of this approach remains uncertain. Field warned that a potential breach would require massive state and federal investment to repair. "We would have cataclysmic erosion if we didn't get federal funding and state funding," he said. He emphasized that if beach renourishment funding disappears across Florida, the state's entire coastline could transform rapidly and unfavorably. Field believes the recent FDEP assessment strengthens Jupiter Island's position to secure state and federal funding to address the erosion crisis. The report ultimately underscores how coastal erosion threatens not just homes and infrastructure, but also irreplaceable natural and historical resources.

Peters, a Republican seeking her second re-election after first securing the seat in 2018, said she was honored to earn DiCeglie's endorsement. She faces a Democratic challenger, David Glenn Jr. DiCeglie's endorsement comes about a week after U.S. Rep. Anna Paulina Luna endorsed Peters and follows a recent endorsement from Pinellas County Sheriff Bob Gualtieri.

Peters has raised $126,000 through her official campaign account and $24,500 through her affiliated political committee, Florida Speaks, as of the end of March. Glenn has raised $3,400. Peters also benefits from a significant voter registration advantage in District 6, where there are 23,000 more Republicans than Democrats, representing a 25-percentage-point advantage. Peters previously served in the Florida House from 2012 to 2018 and was Mayor of South Pasadena. She currently serves on the boards for Tampa Bay Water, the Tampa Bay Estuary Program, and the Department of Environmental Protection's Tampa Bay Aquatic Preserve Management Plan.
