Residents at the Colony Condos are pushing back against summer beachgoers who they say are trespassing on their private property since the town removed a boundary fence in 2023. The town initially took down the fence for safety reasons, leaving only vegetation to mark where the public walkway ends and private land begins. Colony resident Lori Hill described the recurring problem, explaining that beachgoers park in nearby spots and push back bushes to cut through with coolers, fishing rods, and lawn chairs. Hill said the issue intensifies during summer months when random cars fill the parking areas throughout the day.

Hill recalled that when the fence was in place, property lines were unmistakable and trespassing was less common. She noted that residents initially confronted people crossing onto private property, but those interactions often turned hostile. "We used to confront them and tell them this is private property, and they would get pretty rude," Hill said. "So, now what we've just been doing is just calling the police, and they've been coming up and confronting them." The ongoing tensions prompted the condo residents to bring their concerns before the Juno Beach Town Council.

Not all beachgoers acknowledge a significant problem. Michael House, a regular visitor to JB-7, said he accesses the beach from a public parking lot blocks away and has not observed the trespassing issues residents describe. "There's plenty of free parking. If they're damaging property and you know littering and stuff like that might be a different issue," House said. "I have never noticed anyone hopping the fence or the shrubs or anything like that."

During a recent town council meeting, officials clarified that the town has no legal obligation to install a fence. However, Vice Mayor Pro Tem DD Halpern suggested exploring more affordable alternatives to a full replacement. "I understand fencing is expensive, but I'd like to see maybe we can help them with a lower-cost fence or some combination of split rail and shrubs," Halpern said. Hill expressed hope that the town would reinstall some form of boundary to restore clear property lines and protect resident parking areas. The council decided to address the fence replacement question during budget discussions scheduled for July 15, leaving open the possibility that a solution could involve shared costs or hybrid approaches combining different barrier materials.

Misty Dawn White, 46, died in a house fire at her Hobe Sound home on Monday. Investigators from the sheriff's office are examining the blaze, with preliminary findings suggesting an electrical issue may have caused it. The investigation is still ongoing as authorities work to determine the exact cause of the fire.

White's fiancé, Mark, received support from friends and family following the tragedy. Nina Fenn, who had been friends with White for eight years, learned of the death through a phone call from Mark. "We received a call from Mark stating that Misty had passed away in a fire," Fenn said. Since the fire destroyed many of their belongings, friends launched a fundraiser to help Mark rebuild. Fenn described the situation as "completely a state of devastation."

Fenn spoke about White's character and how she wants her to be remembered. "Misty was a Christian woman. She loved gardening and plants. She plays the piano," Fenn said. "She was always the first to lend a hand. She loves kids; she loves her kids. She loves people." According to Fenn, White was known for her generosity and willingness to help others. The loss has been particularly difficult for those close to her, especially considering Mark's previous tragedy. "Mark lost his one and only child, Jacob, and lost him tragically. So, I just am trying whatever I can to support him," Fenn said.

The timing of White's death adds another layer of sadness for those who knew her. White's birthday fell on the Fourth of July, meaning she passed away just days before turning another year older. "Misty's birthday is the Fourth of July. So, she passed just a few days before her birthday," Fenn said. Despite the heartbreak of losing White so close to her birthday, those who knew her are holding onto her memory. "She's always been a good soul to me. She'll always be in our hearts," Fenn said.

Riviera Beach has earned national recognition as one of only 10 U.S. communities selected for the 2026 All-America City Award from the National Civic League. The announcement was made during the National Civic League conference in Denver, where Riviera Beach leaders presented the city's community engagement efforts to judges. As the sole Florida city receiving the award this year, Riviera Beach joins Chelsea, Massachusetts; Franklin, Tennessee; Harlingen, Texas; Montgomery, Alabama; Morrisville, North Carolina; Norfolk, Virginia; North Charleston, South Carolina; Roanoke, Virginia; and Woodburn, Oregon as this year's honorees. The award recognizes communities that unite residents, local government, businesses and organizations to address local challenges and create meaningful change.

Mayor Douglas Lawson expressed pride in the achievement, describing Riviera Beach as "one of the biggest, brightest, shining stars." He credited the city's success to years of deliberate effort aimed at revitalizing the community and enhancing quality of life for residents. Lawson noted that residents are witnessing the transformation that city leaders envisioned. The recognition reflects an extended commitment to strengthening civic health, building trust between residents and government, and ensuring residents actively shape the city's direction. Councilwoman KaShamba Miller-Anderson emphasized that the award belongs to the entire community, stating "This award is a tribute to the heart and determination of our residents. Every event we host, every project we complete, and every challenge we overcome happens because our community shows up for one another."

The city's application showcased three signature programs demonstrating civic participation and community investment. The Riviera Beach Youth Council engages high school students in civic leadership and advocacy. The Citizens Leadership Academy, a civic education program, boasts a 98 percent completion rate. The Neighborhood Reclaim Home Rehabilitation Program provides mini-grants to longtime homeowners for necessary repairs. Miller-Anderson stated that these initiatives reflect the city's commitment to treating civic engagement as an ongoing partnership involving government, residents, faith groups, schools and community organizations. She noted that the city continues developing innovative approaches to ensure residents feel invested in the city's progress.

The All-America City Award, presented annually since 1949, ranks among the nation's most respected civic honors. City leaders view the designation as validation of Riviera Beach's progress and momentum. The award positions the community for future growth and opportunities while signaling to the nation that collaboration and resident involvement drive results in Riviera Beach. Officials emphasized that the recognition reinforces community pride and demonstrates the city's commitment to shaping a stronger future through collective effort.

They had a lot of success in their career, and hopefully their senior year is the best season that we've had," Robertson said. "We knew that we wanted to take our program to the next level. So we really have a challenging schedule for this year because we wanna compete. The team's definition of success this season goes beyond regular season performance. Senior Jack Whelche stressed the importance of advancing deeper into the playoffs. The Scots are using their offseason training to build the momentum they believe will carry them toward their state title goal.

12:34 PM ET

Florida education officials this week moved forward with significant new barriers for undocumented students seeking higher education in the state. The State Board of Education voted to advance rules that would prevent undocumented immigrants from enrolling in any of Florida's 28 state colleges and adult education programs. The restrictions would also apply to GED preparation courses, which many students use as a pathway to college or workforce training opportunities.

This action by the State Board of Education follows a similar decision by the state university system's Board of Governors, which approved a plan to bar undocumented students from Florida's public universities starting in the 2027-28 academic year. Students already enrolled at these universities would not be affected by the new policy. Together, these measures represent an escalating effort by state officials to limit educational access for undocumented immigrants, building on Florida's previous decision to eliminate in-state tuition rates for many undocumented students, including those protected under DACA.

Supporters of the restrictions contend that taxpayer-funded educational institutions should be available only to students who are legally present in the United States. However, immigrant advocates and students have voiced strong opposition to the new rules. Alexander Vallejos, a student Dreamer, challenged the restrictions by saying "We're just asking for an opportunity to earn our future through education. Because when Dreamers succeed, Florida succeeds." Advocates argue that the restrictions would deny opportunities to young people who have grown up in Florida and graduated from Florida high schools.

These education policy moves occurred during a week marked by other significant developments in Florida politics. Governor Ron DeSantis announced that Florida would use a new state law to designate organizations as terrorist groups. DeSantis stated the state would seek to designate the Council on American-Islamic Relations, the Muslim Brotherhood, and Antifa, along with more than 90 federally designated foreign terrorist organizations. The law, which took effect July 1, allows the governor and Florida Cabinet to make these designations based on recommendations from state domestic security officials. If approved by the Cabinet, the designations could block public funding and contracts from listed groups and create penalties for providing material support. CAIR and civil rights organizations quickly sued in federal court, arguing that the process violates free speech and due process rights, and that DeSantis cannot unilaterally brand organizations with such designations without judicial review.

Beth Clark, 48, was denied coverage for breast reconstruction surgery less than 24 hours before her procedure was scheduled to begin. Three months earlier, Clark had been diagnosed with invasive ductal carcinoma, the most common type of breast cancer, and underwent a full double bilateral mastectomy. The denial from Anthem Blue Cross and Blue Shield rejected coverage for the reconstruction using GalaFlex surgical mesh, claiming the treatment is still considered investigational with insufficient evidence that it improves health outcomes.

The surgical mesh at the center of the dispute has become controversial in the medical community. While the FDA has approved GalaFlex for some uses throughout the body, it has not approved it specifically for breast reconstruction, making it an off-label treatment. In a 2023 letter to health care providers, the FDA stated that the efficacy and safety of GalaFlex for breast cancer patients have not been determined. Despite this regulatory status, Dr. Alicia Billington, Clark's plastic surgeon, uses GalaFlex mesh on most of her breast reconstruction patients and says it produces the least amount of complications compared to other options. Billington has joined other physicians in publicly criticizing insurers on social media, calling for them to review published literature demonstrating the treatment's success.

Anthem Blue Cross and Blue Shield responded by stating it regularly reviews medical coverage policies to ensure they align with medical evidence and current treatment standards. The company emphasized that without FDA approval for breast reconstruction, the mesh does not meet its coverage requirements, though it covers many other evidence-based alternatives. The insurer also noted that Clark retains the right to appeal the decision and confirmed ongoing communication with her physician. The company's position reflects broader industry standards around FDA approval and coverage decisions.

Despite the denial, Clark received her reconstruction surgery with GalaFlex mesh about a week later after her surgeon found a way to make the procedure happen. Clark and Billington continue fighting the insurance denial. Last year, Cigna became the first and only major insurer to begin approving GalaFlex for breast reconstruction after investigative reporting highlighted stories of patients being denied the procedure. A new bill has been introduced to modernize the Women's Health and Cancer Rights Act, a federal law designed to protect breast cancer patients from being denied treatments during their cancer treatment journeys.
