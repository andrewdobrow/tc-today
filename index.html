Palm Beach County Haitians face deportation after Supreme Court eliminates protected status

The Supreme Court has sided with President Donald Trump's administration in eliminating Temporary Protected Status for thousands of people from Haiti and Syria. This decision puts hundreds of thousands at immediate risk of deportation, with significant impacts on South Florida. Temporary Protected Status allows foreign citizens already in the United States to remain, work, and...

Yesterday, 6:33 PM ET

The Supreme Court has sided with President Donald Trump's administration in eliminating Temporary Protected Status for thousands of people from Haiti and Syria. This decision puts hundreds of thousands at immediate risk of deportation, with significant impacts on South Florida. Temporary Protected Status allows foreign citizens already in the United States to remain, work, and support their families while their home countries experience crises such as natural disasters, political unrest, or violence. For Haitians and Syrians, this protection is now ending. The ruling affects approximately 350,000 Haitians and 6,000 Syrians nationwide.

Florida is home to the largest Haitian immigrant population in the United States. Within the state, Palm Beach County ranks third with 68,400 Haitian immigrants, behind Miami-Dade County with 78,800 and Broward County with 86,100. Attorney Byrnes Guillaume, who is Haitian and represents many people facing loss of TPS, explains the practical consequences. "It means that they have no status," Guillaume said. "It means that if you have a job you no longer are able to work. It means that you were once legal and now you have to go in the shadow." He raises concerns about families with American-born children: "For people with American-born kids, what you going to do, are you going to leave the kids here or are you going to take them to Haiti, a country they've never known? I do think lives will be at risk."

Stecko Dulcio, who owns a Haitian restaurant in Riviera Beach where he has served cuisine for five and a half years, became a U.S. citizen and considers himself fortunate to have security. He knows many friends with TPS and worries about their prospects. "That's going to be a disaster," Dulcio said. "Not only are their lives in danger, and even if they go they don't get killed, but they're going to have a miserable life, because there's no work, no job, and they lose everything they have." Dulcio notes that gangs have taken control in Haiti, seizing houses, controlling transportation, and perpetuating violence.

The Trump administration argues that TPS was designed as temporary relief during emergencies and that conditions in Haiti and Syria have sufficiently improved to no longer warrant the protection. Advocates strongly dispute this assessment. The Department of Homeland Security maintains it has sole discretion to determine when protections should end. Guillaume said no timeline for deportations has been established. Since January of last year, DHS has ended protections for people from more than 13 countries. Dulcio urges action: "Pray to the government of the United States to do something to help those people."

The airline's closure removes another budget carrier from the market and raises concerns about the future of affordable air travel options for consumers. A Treasure Coast entrepreneur named LeRoy Gillead II has submitted a proposal...

Yesterday, 4:13 PM ET

Spirit Airlines is undergoing bankruptcy proceedings to liquidate its assets, including aircraft, airport gates, and potentially its headquarters. The airline's closure removes another budget carrier from the market and raises concerns about the future of affordable air travel options for consumers.

A Treasure Coast entrepreneur named LeRoy Gillead II has submitted a proposal to acquire Spirit Airlines in its entirety, rehire its workforce, and rebrand the company as Florida Air Express, a low-cost airline based in West Palm Beach. According to court documents, Gillead is seeking to raise approximately $250 million from private investors to fund this venture. However, Clint Henderson, managing editor of news at The Points Guy travel website, expressed skepticism about the proposal's likelihood of success. Henderson stated that major airlines will probably acquire Spirit's assets through a bidding process rather than a single buyer establishing a new carrier. The bidding deadline is set for July.

For many travelers, Spirit's disappearance represents a genuine setback. Passengers relied on the airline because of its low fares, which provided an affordable travel option during a period of rising costs across the economy. The airline's distinctive yellow jets have become iconic in the aviation industry. Henderson noted that low-cost carriers are becoming increasingly scarce in the United States, as the budget airline business model struggles to generate profits. He pointed out that Frontier and Allegiant remain in the low-cost category, though options continue to narrow.

Henderson offered practical advice for consumers navigating the changing airline landscape. He recommended that travelers stop being brand-loyal and instead focus on finding the cheapest available fares across all carriers. He suggested using Google Flights to compare prices and encouraged consumers to put effort into researching bargains rather than defaulting to familiar airlines. The aircraft, gates, and other Spirit assets have value to potential buyers, though the airline's signature yellow jets will likely be repainted when transferred to new operators. Henderson summed up the broader trend by saying that the low-cost carrier model in the United States faces fundamental challenges, suggesting travelers should adapt their approach to booking flights by prioritizing price over airline preference.

Yesterday, 6:09 PM ET

Josney Peroza, owner of Ukelele Foods Venezuelan food truck in Delray Beach, learned of a devastating disaster when news alerts reported two powerful earthquakes striking his home country seconds apart. The quakes measured 7.2 and 7.5 magnitudes and struck the coastal state of La Guaira. When initial images arrived, Peroza saw a video showing the building where his mother lived had collapsed. Hours later, his brother confirmed she had been inside when it fell. For the next eight hours, Peroza could not reach his mother as communication throughout Venezuela shut down due to widespread power outages and severed connections. Around 3 a.m., the family finally received a call confirming his mother was alive but trapped under the rubble. Relatives and neighbors worked with their bare hands to free her from the debris.

Despite her rescue, Peroza's mother faced serious injuries and loss. She sustained a broken hip requiring surgery and lost her home entirely in the collapse. Peroza described the broader humanitarian catastrophe unfolding across the region, where officials confirmed nearly 200 deaths and at least 1,000 injured. The country lacked any infrastructure to handle a disaster of this magnitude, he said. Most buildings in Venezuela are constructed from concrete and metal, making rescue efforts extremely difficult without proper equipment. Residents had been forced to use prayer and their bare hands as their main tools for helping people still trapped in the wreckage.

Peroza emphasized the urgent need for immediate international assistance. He noted that Venezuela had no power, no water, and no shelter for displaced survivors. He described widespread uncertainty about food, housing, and employment in the coming days. The United States announced it would send rescue crews to help. Peroza said he believed he could better assist his mother and his country by remaining in Florida rather than traveling to the disaster zone. From there, he could work toward rebuilding her life and support the broader relief efforts while continuing to operate his food truck.

Peroza described his business as more than a place to eat, calling it "a cultural Venezuelan experience" and "Venezuela without a passport." He planned to continue serving customers while sharing his country's story and helping survivors. He viewed his mother's survival as proof that more lives could still be saved with rapid international response. For those still waiting for word from loved ones in Venezuela, Peroza offered a message of faith and resilience, emphasizing the importance of trusting in the Lord during the crisis.

Yesterday, 11:46 AM ET

The George Snow Scholarship Fund in Boca Raton has distributed more than $7.5 million in scholarships and support services to 470 students throughout Palm Beach and Broward counties in 2026. President Tim Snow announced the record-breaking milestone to WPTV. Over its four-decade history, the nonprofit organization has awarded more than $45 million to over 4,100 students, establishing itself as the largest scholarship provider in Palm Beach County. The fund's core mission focuses on addressing the gap between traditional financial aid packages and what students and their families can realistically afford. Scholarship recipients receive four-year commitments, which Snow said helps provide financial stability throughout their college years.

The organization's support extends well beyond monetary scholarships. Vice President of Development Jay Brandt emphasized that GSSF offers mentorship, professional development opportunities, and educational resources including the College Compass podcast. These comprehensive services are designed to give scholars the tools they need to succeed academically and professionally. Among the 2026 recipients is Zya Chow, a Wellington Community High School graduate who earned two scholarships: the Royal Palm Hearing Aid Scholarship and the Davidowitz Family Caregiving Youth Scholarship. Chow plans to attend Georgetown University and expressed her appreciation for the organization's support.

One distinctive program within GSSF is the Doyle-Whelchel Scholars Boutique, directed by Scholar Services Coordinator Garvey Antoine. The boutique provides professional, formal, and casual clothing to help scholars prepare for important life transitions. Students can access appropriate wear for job interviews, internships, and their first days on campus. The boutique receives donations of both new and gently used clothing along with footwear and accessories from the community.

The George Snow Scholarship Fund's comprehensive approach to student support reflects growing recognition that removing financial barriers alone is insufficient for true educational equity. As college costs continue rising, organizations like GSSF are addressing multiple dimensions of student success. By combining financial aid with mentorship, professional development, and practical resources like clothing for interviews and campus life, the fund tackles obstacles that often prevent capable students from underserved communities from reaching their full potential. The organization's 2026 results demonstrate that South Florida communities continue to invest in the next generation's educational opportunities.

SB 382, filed by Republican Sen. Keith Truenow, sought to create a statewide system for tracking e-bike and scooter crashes. Currently, local and state agencies record these incidents differently, leaving no consistent data for policymakers to assess the scale of the problem. The legislation would have established a task force to deliver recommendations by Oct. 1 on regulating micromobility devices. The bill also aimed to cap e-bike speeds at 10 miles per hour on sidewalks when pedestrians are nearby and require riders to yield on shared paths, with violations treated as non-criminal traffic infractions. DeSantis' veto comes weeks after an Orlando boy was killed on his e-scooter in Lake Nona when the 13-year-old cut in front of a truck while picking up flowers for Mother's Day.

DeSantis also rejected HB 461, which would have allowed high school students to volunteer as poll workers in exchange for community service hours for graduation requirements or Bright Futures scholarships. The bill, sponsored by Republican Reps. Kiyan Michael and Susan Valdés, would have loosened rules on privately funded election-related expenses to allow students to volunteer at polls before the August Primary. The measure was backed by the League of Women Voters and the Southern Poverty Law Center. The Governor additionally vetoed HB 4075, a local bill for the town of Davie in Broward County, sponsored by Democrat Rep. Michael Gottlieb, that would have exempted agricultural land from outdoor advertising laws.

Judge sets July 29 hearing on challenge to DeSantis property tax ballot language

A Leon County judge is fast-tracking a lawsuit that challenges the wording of Governor Ron DeSantis's property tax constitutional amendment before counties begin printing ballots for the General Election.

Yesterday, 6:35 PM ET

Circuit Judge Angela Dempsey has set a July 29 hearing for a lawsuit challenging the ballot language of Gov. Ron DeSantis' property tax amendment. The case, filed by Save Our Voters From Misleading Ballot Language and joined by former Stuart Mayor Thomas Campenni and former Key Biscayne Mayor Michael Davey, disputes the wording of the proposed constitutional amendment. The plaintiffs argue that the ballot summary promises to ensure funding for core services, but the amendment would actually require local governments to spend whatever property tax revenue remains on public safety, schools, infrastructure, and natural resources. According to the lawsuit, by substantially reducing the local property tax base, the amendment is likely to decrease revenues available to local governments to fund core services.

Lawmakers passed House Joint Resolution 1F during this month's Special Session, placing the measure on the ballot. If approved by at least 60% of voters, the amendment would raise the nonschool homestead exemption to $150,000 starting Jan. 1, 2027, and to $250,000 the following year. It would also cut the annual cap on assessment increases for non-homestead property from 10% to 5%. State analysts estimate the proposal would shrink local property tax collections by almost $5 billion in the 2027-28 fiscal year, with yearly impact rising to more than $10.7 billion by 2030-31. DeSantis defends the ballot summary, noting that lawmakers amended state law to allow more explanatory language than the usual 75-word limit.
