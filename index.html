Nearly 4.6 million Floridians are expected to travel at least 50 miles from home during the Fourth of July weekend, representing a 3% increase from the previous year. About 4 million of these travelers will drive, while an additional 330,000 will fly and roughly 204,000 will use buses, trains, cruises, or other transportation methods. As one of the nation's top domestic travel destinations, Florida will experience significant traffic congestion on major highways including I-4, I-95, and roads leading to beaches and theme parks. Travel timing matters during this busy period. AAA and INRIX data recommend that drivers avoid afternoon and evening travel, particularly on July 2 when people are heading out and on July 5 and 6 when they return home. Early morning hours generally offer the best driving conditions.

The statistics surrounding holiday traffic safety paint a concerning picture. On a typical July day, Florida experiences an average of 7 to 8 traffic fatalities. However, the Fourth of July itself has historically produced a 44% spike in fatalities, averaging about 11 deaths on that single day based on data from 2017 through 2021. Throughout entire holiday weekends, the state has seen between 25 and 32 or more deaths, with many of these accidents linked to alcohol impairment and failure to use seatbelts.

Vehicle maintenance plays a critical role in preventing accidents during holiday travel. Benny Garcia at Palm Beach Tire Pros emphasizes the importance of checking tire pressure before long drives. To determine if tires need replacement, drivers can use the penny test by placing a coin upside down in the tire treads. If you can see the entire outline of Abraham Lincoln's head, the tires should be changed. Understanding tire age is equally important. The date code on a tire's sidewall provides this information, with the first two numbers indicating the week of manufacture and the last two numbers showing the year. Most tires typically last between 5 and 6 years.

Before embarking on Fourth of July travel, drivers should perform a complete vehicle inspection. Check tire pressure, battery condition, and all fluid levels, including oil. Pack an emergency kit in your vehicle in case of unexpected problems. Once on the road, maintaining focus is essential for safety. Put away your phone and eliminate distractions. Remember that fatigued driving poses dangers similar to impaired driving. By taking these precautions and planning your travel schedule carefully, you can help ensure a safer holiday weekend for yourself and your family.

The DEA's Miami drug lab, the busiest in the nation, is detecting disturbing quantities of fentanyl and carfentanil in seized drug samples. Chemist Alyssa Sanchez, who has worked at the facility for three years, expressed concern about the trend. "I see a lot of fentanyl, but even more recently I've seen a lot of carfentanil in my samples; it's scary," she said. The Miami lab's prominent role stems from its coastal location and proximity to South America, making it a critical testing facility for drugs entering the United States. Sanchez recently analyzed a kilogram brick of fentanyl that tested at approximately 16 percent purity and also contained carfentanil, a substance thousands of times more potent than fentanyl itself.

Fentanyl is a powerful synthetic opioid used medically to treat pain, but it has become the leading cause of death for Americans ages 18 to 45. The drug is 100 times stronger than morphine and 50 times stronger than heroin. Just two milligrams, roughly the size of a salt grain, can be lethal. Carfentanil has no approved use for humans and exists solely as a tranquilizer for large animals like elephants. A single kilogram brick containing fentanyl at the concentrations Sanchez discovered could kill thousands of people. When asked about the potency level, Sanchez confirmed that 16 percent concentration is considered high for fentanyl samples.

According to Miles Aley, special agent in charge of the DEA's Miami Division, Mexican drug cartels have shifted toward using carfentanil after the Chinese government restricted precursor chemicals needed to manufacture fentanyl. The cartels have sourced both chemicals and expertise through the dark web to enhance the potency of their drugs. Aley emphasized that the agency considers fentanyl and carfentanil a top priority. He noted that while overdose deaths have declined from 110,000 annually to 70,000, this reduction does not necessarily reflect decreased drug use. Instead, more people are surviving overdoses thanks to Narcan, an over-the-counter nasal spray that reverses opioid overdose effects.

The DEA plans to address the crisis through its inaugural Fentanyl Free America Summit scheduled for next month in Orlando, designed to increase public awareness about the current fentanyl emergency. Aley also warned about emerging drugs that resist Narcan treatment. These new compounds represent a different chemical structure that renders Narcan ineffective against overdoses. Aley acknowledged that solving the fentanyl crisis requires sustained effort from multiple organizations and agencies, with no single solution available.

According to court documents filed during Spirit's bankruptcy proceedings, Gillead is seeking to raise approximately $250 million from private investors to fund the acquisition. The deadline for all bids is scheduled for July.

The bankruptcy sale includes Spirit's valuable assets such as aircraft, airport gates, and potentially its headquarters building. Clint Henderson, managing editor of news at The Points Guy travel website, expressed doubt about whether Gillead's plan will come to fruition. Henderson believes it is far more probable that Spirit's assets will be distributed to the highest bidders, which are likely to be major established airlines rather than a new startup carrier. "This seems like a pie in the sky thing. If they can get the money together, maybe they'll establish a new low-cost carrier, but right now they don't fly any routes at all," Henderson said.

The disappearance of Spirit Airlines marks a significant shift in the budget travel market, leaving fewer options for cost-conscious passengers. Many travelers relied on Spirit for affordable fares during a time when travel expenses continue to rise. Henderson noted that the low-cost carrier model faces serious challenges in the United States, with existing budget airlines struggling to maintain profitability. Currently, only Frontier and Allegiant remain operating as low-cost carriers in the industry.

For consumers seeking affordable airfare following Spirit's collapse, Henderson recommends a flexible approach rather than airline loyalty. "Be airline agnostic; that's the best advice I have for consumers," Henderson said. "Go with the cheapest airline you can find. Use Google Flights and don't try to be loyal to one airline." This strategy requires travelers to invest additional time comparing prices across carriers but increases the likelihood of finding the best available fares. The Spirit Airlines bankruptcy signals broader challenges facing budget air travel in the United States, and the eventual fate of its distinctive yellow aircraft remains uncertain as they await potential repainting in new company colors.

Yesterday, 6:09 PM ET

Josney Peroza, owner of Ukelele Foods, a Venezuelan food truck in Delray Beach, describes his business as more than just a place to eat. "This is a cultural Venezuelan experience that we share with the community. We call it Venezuela without a passport," he said. That connection to his homeland became deeply personal when two earthquakes measuring 7.2 and 7.5 magnitudes struck Venezuela seconds apart. Peroza first learned of the disaster through news alerts, but the reality hit hard when he saw video footage of the building where his mother lived. Hours later, his brother confirmed she had been inside when the structure collapsed around 3 a.m.

Communication in Venezuela became nearly impossible as power failed and connections were cut. Peroza spent anxious hours not knowing if his mother was alive, relying on prayer to maintain peace. After about eight hours, the call finally came confirming she had survived. His relatives and neighbors had worked with their bare hands to free her from the rubble. "The Venezuelan culture is well known because of the service for the people, and especially in this kind of situation," Peroza said. His mother suffered a broken hip requiring surgery and lost her home in the collapse in the coastal state of La Guaira.

The disaster revealed a wider humanitarian crisis affecting the entire region. Peroza described a population struggling without power, water, or shelter. Officials confirm nearly 200 people are dead and at least 1,000 others are injured. Most buildings are made of concrete and metal, making rescue efforts extremely difficult with limited tools. "The country doesn't have any structure for this kind of situation," Peroza said. "We need international help immediately." The United States announced it would send rescue crews to assist. Peroza emphasized the urgency of the situation, noting that survivors face unclear prospects for food, work, and shelter in the coming days.

Despite the disaster affecting his family, Peroza believes he can help more effectively from Florida than by traveling to Venezuela. "We don't have any plans to go down there. I think we are more helpful from here," he explained, noting that his mother and other survivors will need housing and resources that require commercial activity and employment opportunities. He plans to continue operating his food truck while raising awareness about Venezuela's needs. Peroza called his mother's rescue "absolutely a miracle" and said it proves more lives can still be saved with international response. "There are families in Venezuela waiting for rescue, medical attention, information, and hope," he said. "Right now, Venezuela needs urgent humanitarian help."

The Supreme Court's 6-3 decision has cleared the way for the Department of Homeland Security to end temporary protected status for 350,000 Haitians across the country. Within hours of the ruling, Rep. Frederica Wilson of Miami-Dade responded by introducing the Haitian Refugee Immigration Fairness Act in the House. The bill would create a pathway to green cards for eligible Haitian nationals who have lived in the United States since June 26, 2024, as well as their family members.

Florida will experience the effects of this decision more than any other state. According to the National Immigration Forum, approximately 404,000 TPS holders currently live in Florida, significantly more than Texas with roughly 147,000. The Migration Policy Institute reports that nearly half of all Haitians in the United States, about 49 percent, live in Florida. The Supreme Court's ruling affects Haitians who have been living legally in the country for years, many of whom registered, paid required fees, worked, paid taxes, and established roots in their communities.

Wilson, who has represented Miami-Dade for more than four decades, called the Supreme Court decision heartbreaking but emphasized that Congress retains the power to protect these families. She stated that her bill would move Haitian families from uncertainty to stability, and that TPS was never meant to become a lifetime of uncertainty. Her bill already has support from other Florida Democrats, including Reps. Maxwell Frost of Orlando and Debbie Wasserman Schultz of Broward County, who became original co-sponsors. In April, the House previously passed a separate bill to extend TPS for Haitians through a discharge petition, which received support from 10 Republicans including South Florida representatives Mario Diaz-Balart, Carlos Gimenez, and Maria Elvira Salazar.

The path forward in the Senate presents a more significant challenge. Any legislation will require 60 votes to advance, a difficult threshold even with leadership backing. Wilson argued that her bill creates a responsible pathway to lawful permanent resident status while maintaining safeguards to protect public safety. She emphasized that Haitian families have complied with legal requirements and strengthened communities by working and paying taxes. The Supreme Court's decision puts hundreds of thousands of Haitian families at risk of deportation after years of legal residence in the United States.

DeSantis Signs Law Creating Felony for Rental Application Fraud in Florida

Gov. Ron DeSantis signed HB 1293 into law earlier this month, creating a new third-degree felony for people who knowingly gain access to residential housing through false statements, forged documents or identity misrepresentation.

10:27 AM ET

The Florida Apartment Association is celebrating the passage of HB 1293, a new law signed by Gov. Ron DeSantis on June 12 that creates a third-degree felony for rental application fraud. The bill passed unanimously in both chambers and goes into effect Oct. 1. The law targets people who knowingly and willfully gain access to residential housing through materially false statements, forged documents, counterfeit records, or identity misrepresentation. The FAA identified the growing problem, worked with industry stakeholders to develop a policy solution, and championed the legislation throughout the legislative process. The issue is particularly a problem in the Miami-Fort Lauderdale area, where people use altered pay stubs, false employment records, synthetic identities, and counterfeit bank statements to lie on rental applications.

The consequences of rental application fraud extend beyond housing providers, according to the FAA. When fraudsters obtain affordable housing units for which they are not eligible, they reduce access for working families and individuals who legitimately qualify for those limited units. Tenants who commit application fraud often default on rent, leading to costly legal proceedings and financial losses that affect housing operations and affordability. The new legislation also makes application fraud a lease violation, allowing a tenant's lease to be terminated legally through existing legal processes. "Florida has become one of the nation's hotspots for rental application fraud, creating significant challenges for housing providers," said Amanda White, Vice President of the Florida Apartment Association.

Florida Property Tax Amendment Has 64% Support But Deep Partisan Divide in New Poll

A constitutional amendment to raise Florida's homestead exemption to $250,000 would likely pass if the November election were held today, with nearly two-thirds of voters supporting it, according to new polling.

A few minutes ago

A new Sachs Media poll shows Florida's proposed property tax amendment would likely pass if the November election were held today, with 64% overall support. However, backing fractures sharply along party lines, with 87% of Republicans supporting the measure compared with 62% of independents and 35% of Democrats. The survey of 850 Florida voters conducted June 22-24 had a 3.8-percentage-point margin of error. The amendment would exempt the first $250,000 of homestead property value from taxation on most local levies. Support cuts across homeowner and renter lines nearly identically at 65% and 64% respectively. Among homeowners, 57% said they would be more likely to stay in their current home if it passes, while 42% of renters said they would be more likely to buy a home.

State economists estimate the amendment could cost local governments $11.86 billion annually once fully implemented. By fiscal year 2028-29, counties could lose $8.775 billion in revenue. Miami-Dade County would face $445 million in cuts, while smaller rural counties dependent on homestead property taxes could lose up to 35% of their revenue. The Legislature passed the measure during a Special Session on June 2, placing it on the November 3 ballot. If approved by at least 60% of voters, homestead exemptions would progressively rise to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, with subsequent increases tied to inflation.

The shelter's expert team recommends several critical preparatory steps, including ensuring pets wear collars with current identification and have updated microchips registered with correct contact information.
