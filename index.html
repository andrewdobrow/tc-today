4:13 PM ET

LeRoy Gillead II, a Treasure Coast entrepreneur, has submitted a proposal to purchase Spirit Airlines in its entirety, rehire its workforce, and rebrand the carrier as Florida Air Express, a low-cost airline based in West Palm Beach. According to court documents, Gillead is attempting to secure up to $250 million from private investors to fund the acquisition. The deadline for all bids is scheduled for July.

Spirit Airlines is currently in bankruptcy court liquidating its assets, which include aircraft, airport gates, and potentially its headquarters. These physical assets represent significant value in the aviation industry. The airline's closure removes another budget-friendly option for cost-conscious travelers at a time when airfare prices remain high. Many passengers relied on Spirit's low fares to make air travel affordable.

Clint Henderson, managing editor of news at The Points Guy travel website, expressed skepticism about Gillead's proposal becoming reality. "This seems like a pie in the sky thing," Henderson said. While acknowledging that money could potentially be raised to establish a new low-cost carrier, he noted that such an operation currently has no routes or operational infrastructure. Henderson believes it is far more probable that Spirit's assets will be distributed among multiple bidders, likely major airlines. Those major carriers would acquire the valuable planes and gates rather than seeing them consolidated under a single new operator.

The disappearance of Spirit Airlines reflects a larger trend affecting the budget airline sector. Henderson noted that low-cost carriers are struggling nationwide, with the business model facing significant challenges. Only Frontier and Allegiant remain operating in the true low-cost category. For travelers seeking affordable fares, Henderson recommends a flexible approach. "Be airline agnostic," he advised consumers. "Go with the cheapest airline you can find. Use Google Flights and don't try to be loyal to one airline." This strategy requires more effort to find bargains but offers the best chance of securing discounted tickets in the current market. The iconic yellow aircraft Spirit was known for will likely be repainted and operated under different airline brands if purchased by larger carriers.

The center will be led by husband-and-wife physicians Dr. Meron Kristos and Dr. Gabitto, who met during residency at the former St. Vincent's hospital in New York City.

According to the Cleveland Clinic, Indian River is designated a health professional shortage area and medically underserved area. Dr. Kristos said the shortage leads to longer wait times for patients, with people waiting months to see a doctor. Christine Stenback, who has lived in Indian River County nearly three decades, said she experienced this problem when she moved here with her daughter. 6:09 PM ET

Josney Peroza, who owns and operates Ukelele Foods, a Venezuelan food truck in Delray Beach, learned of a devastating disaster through news alerts reporting two earthquakes measuring 7.2 and 7.5 magnitudes that struck seconds apart in Venezuela. When videos of the collapsed building where his mother lived began circulating, his worst fears intensified. Hours later, his brother confirmed that their mother had been inside the structure when it fell. With power outages and severed connections throughout Venezuela, Peroza spent an agonizing stretch of time unable to reach anyone or confirm whether his mother had survived. About eight hours after the collapse, he finally received a call at 3 a.m. stating that his mother was alive but trapped under the rubble. Relatives and neighbors worked with their bare hands to dig her out. His mother sustained a broken hip requiring surgery and lost her home in the disaster.

Peroza describes his food truck as more than just a place to eat. "This is more than a food truck. It is a cultural Venezuelan experience that we share with the community," he said. "We call it Venezuela without a passport." The broader humanitarian crisis extends far beyond his family's tragedy. The coastal state of La Guaira and surrounding areas now face critical shortages of power and water, leaving thousands homeless. Officials confirm nearly 200 people have died and at least 1,000 others have been injured. Peroza emphasized that Venezuela lacks the infrastructure and resources to handle a disaster of this magnitude, calling for immediate international assistance. The United States has committed to sending rescue crews to support recovery efforts.

Peroza highlighted the challenges facing rescue workers operating in the disaster zone. Most buildings in the area are constructed from concrete and metal, making rescue efforts extremely difficult without proper equipment. "How can you help with your bare hands?" he asked, noting that residents are forced to rely on faith and prayer while awaiting international support. When asked if he considers his mother's survival a miracle, Peroza responded without hesitation: "Yeah, it's absolutely a miracle." He expressed deep concern for the thousands of other survivors facing similar circumstances, describing the building where his mother lived as having 12 to 15 floors that likely housed about 100 families.

Rather than traveling to Venezuela, Peroza believes he can provide more meaningful assistance from Florida. With no commercial activity expected in the coming days and limited employment opportunities, he plans to help his mother secure housing from afar. He intends to continue operating his food truck while working to support his country's recovery. "I believe this is part of our culture, get over the circumstances, and I think this is the way we can help," Peroza said. "We pray for Venezuela, but we work for Venezuela too, because it's not about my mom, it's no longer about us, it's about our people down there."

The George Snow Scholarship Fund has announced a major achievement, awarding more than $7.5 million in scholarships and support services to 470 students across Palm Beach and Broward counties in 2026. According to President Tim Snow, who spoke with WPTV, this record-breaking distribution reflects the nonprofit's continued expansion of financial and educational support for students in South Florida.

Since its founding over 40 years ago, GSSF has distributed more than $45 million to over 4,100 students, establishing itself as the largest scholarship provider in Palm Beach County. Snow explained that the program bridges the gap between traditional financial aid and what students and families can actually afford to pay. The nonprofit's approach includes four-year scholarship commitments that provide stability and predictability as students work through their college education. Beyond monetary assistance, Vice President of Development Jay Brandt noted that GSSF offers mentorship, professional development opportunities, and educational resources including the College Compass podcast to help scholars succeed both academically and professionally.

One recipient benefiting from the fund's support is Zya Chow, a Wellington Community High School graduate heading to Georgetown University. Chow earned both the Royal Palm Hearing Aid Scholarship and the Davidowitz Family Caregiving Youth Scholarship. Her selection highlights how GSSF's diverse funding sources allow the organization to support students with varying backgrounds and circumstances.

The scholarship fund has also expanded its services beyond financial aid. The Doyle-Whelchel Scholars Boutique, led by Scholar Services Coordinator Garvey Antoine, provides professional, formal, and casual clothing to help students prepare for interviews, internships, and college life. The boutique accepts donations of new or gently used clothing, footwear, and accessories from the community. This comprehensive approach to student support reflects GSSF's understanding that removing barriers to success involves more than tuition assistance. As higher education costs continue to rise across the nation, organizations like the George Snow Scholarship Fund play an increasingly important role in making college accessible to underserved students in South Florida.

7:17 PM ET

The Supreme Court voted 6-3 to allow the Trump administration to end temporary protected status for migrants from Haiti and Syria, clearing the way for potential deportations of hundreds of thousands of people. The decision overturned lower court orders blocking the Department of Homeland Security from ending the program, which currently protects 1.3 million people from 17 countries. The conservative majority ruled that immigration authorities have sole control over the program and that judges cannot intervene in such decisions. Justices Ketanji Brown Jackson, Elena Kagan and Sonia Sotomayor dissented. This marks the second immigration policy victory for President Trump at the high court this week, following a decision that allows revival of restrictions on asylum seekers.

Florida politicians responded quickly and sharply to the ruling. Governor Ron DeSantis called it a correct decision, arguing that the three liberal justices believed temporary status was essentially permanent. U.S. Representatives Maxwell Frost, Jared Moskowitz and Debbie Wasserman Schultz all opposed the decision. Frost said it will devastate families, communities and the country. Moskowitz warned that deporting Haitian TPS holders to a nation the State Department considers too dangerous to visit will create severe economic consequences. Wasserman Schultz called the decision despicable and announced plans to introduce legislation to reverse it, noting that hundreds of thousands of families who followed all legal requirements and paid taxes now face separation and deportation. Florida House Democratic Leader Fentrice Driskell said TPS recipients work legally, pay taxes and raise families, and are now uncertain whether America's promise still applies to them.

Miami-Dade Commissioner Marleine Bastien, who previously ran a nonprofit assisting the Haitian community, warned that hundreds of thousands of jobs could become vacant in healthcare, hospitality, construction, manufacturing and business services. She emphasized that Haiti remains in crisis with extreme violence and instability, making forced returns unconscionable. Commissioner Oliver Gilbert characterized Trump's motivation as patently racist, while former Broward County Mayor Dale Holness called the decision legally reckless and morally bankrupt. State Rep. Angie Nixon, a Senate candidate, said the ruling puts millions of lives into uncertainty and fear. Senate candidate Alex Vindman described it as a broken promise to thousands of immigrants who built lives and contributed to Florida communities while waiting for conditions to improve in their home countries.

6:33 PM ET

The Supreme Court has sided with President Donald Trump's administration in eliminating Temporary Protected Status for thousands of people from Haiti and Syria. This decision leaves hundreds of thousands suddenly vulnerable to deportation, including many living in South Florida. Temporary Protected Status allows foreign citizens already in the United States to remain, work, and raise families while their home countries face turmoil from natural disasters, political unrest, or violence. For Haitians and Syrians, that protection is now ending.

The Trump administration argues that TPS was designed only as short-term relief during crises and claims conditions in Haiti and Syria have improved enough to no longer justify the protection. Advocates strongly dispute this claim. The administration maintains that the Department of Homeland Security has sole discretion to determine when protections end and that courts should not interfere. Florida has the largest Haitian immigrant population in the United States. Palm Beach County ranks third nationally with 68,400 Haitian immigrants, according to the Migration Policy Institute, behind Miami-Dade County with 78,800 and Broward County with 86,100.

Stecko Dulcio, who owns a restaurant serving Haitian cuisine in Riviera Beach, is a citizen but expresses deep concern for friends with TPS status. He describes Haiti as dangerous, noting that gangs have taken control of houses, transportation, and fuel widespread violence. "That's going to be a disaster," Dulcio said. "Not only are their lives in danger, but even if they go they don't get killed, they're going to have a miserable life, because there's no work, no job, and they lose everything they have." He urged the U.S. government to take action to help affected people.

Attorney Byrnes Guillaume, who is Haitian and represents many people losing TPS, explains the practical impact of losing this status. "It means that if you have a job you no longer are able to work, it means that you were once legal and now you have to go in the shadow," Guillaume said. He raises additional concerns for families with American-born children, questioning whether parents would leave their children in the United States or take them to Haiti, a country they have never known. "I do think lives will be at risk," Guillaume said. The ruling affects roughly 350,000 Haitians and 6,000 Syrians nationwide. Guillaume noted that no timeline on deportations has been set. Since January of last year, DHS has ended protections for people from more than 13 countries.

A Leon County judge is fast-tracking a lawsuit that challenges the wording of Gov. Ron DeSantis' property tax amendment before counties begin printing ballots for the General Election.

6:35 PM ET

Circuit Judge Angela Dempsey set a July 29 hearing for a lawsuit challenging the wording of Gov. Ron DeSantis' proposed property tax constitutional amendment. The plaintiffs, a group called Save Our Voters From Misleading Ballot Language joined by former Stuart Mayor Thomas Campenni and former Key Biscayne Mayor Michael Davey, are not asking to remove the amendment entirely but argue that the ballot title and summary mislead voters. They say the ballot summary promises to ensure funding for core services, but the amendment would actually require local governments to spend whatever property tax revenue remains on public safety, schools, infrastructure, and natural resources. If at least 60% of voters approve, the amendment would raise the nonschool homestead exemption to $150,000 starting Jan. 1, 2027, and then to $250,000 the following year. It would also cut the annual cap on assessment increases for non-homestead property from 10% to 5%. State analysts estimate the proposal would shrink local property tax collections by almost $5 billion in the 2027-28 fiscal year, with yearly impact rising to more than $10.7 billion by 2030-31. DeSantis emphasized the need for quick resolution before counties print ballots for the General Election.

Peggy Adams Animal Rescue League urges South Florida pet owners to prepare hurricane plans

With hurricane season in full swing, Peggy Adams Animal Rescue League is urging South Florida residents to create a disaster plan for their pets before the next storm hits.

Yesterday, 1:34 PM ET

With hurricane season in full swing, Peggy Adams Animal Rescue League is urging South Florida residents to create a disaster plan for their pets before the next storm hits. Dr. Alyssa Comroe, CEO of Peggy Adams, told WPTV the safest place for pets during a hurricane is right alongside their owners. The shelter's expert team recommends several key steps for pet safety, including ensuring pets wear a collar with current identification and have an updated microchip registered with correct contact information. Pet owners should introduce pets to a designated safe room ahead of time, such as a walk-in closet or downstairs bathroom, and conduct practice runs so the hurricane isn't their first time there.

Pet owners should prepare an emergency kit with a three-day supply of food and water in airtight containers, bowls, a sturdy carrier, bedding, towels, medications for two weeks, vaccination records, first-aid supplies, litter and box for cats, leashes, harnesses, cool packs, wipes, waste bags, and multiple forms of visible ID. Organizers recommend keeping a printed list of local pet-friendly shelters and hotels in an accessible place and maintaining recent photos of pets, ideally with their owner, to help with identification if separated. Conducting hurricane drills that include pets' daily routines, such as feeding, drinking water, and resting inside the safe room, can maximize comfort later. Florida's hurricane season runs June through November, overlapping with extreme summer heat.
