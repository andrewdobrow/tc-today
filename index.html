Two graduates highlighted at the celebration were Sabrina Diaz and Brian Saintfort, both of whom experienced the loss of a parent and sought assistance from ChildNet during their challenging times. The organization provided them with the tools and resources needed to overcome their circumstances and continue their education. Diaz is pursuing pre-med coursework at Palm Beach State College with aspirations of becoming a physician. Saintfort has chosen a different path, enrolling in a vocational program to become a plumber. Both young adults credited their success to perseverance and belief in themselves.

Diaz shared advice for other young people facing similar hardships. She emphasized the importance of maintaining confidence in future success and dedicating effort toward achieving personal goals. Most critically, she stressed the need to stay committed to education and never abandon one's dreams, even when confronted with painful circumstances. "Belief that you're going to be big one day and just put effort into it, never give up on school and never give up on dreams. If you like push past with the pain and just keep trying hard," Diaz said. Saintfort delivered a similar message to his peers, reinforcing the power of self-belief and determination. "Believe that you'll make it, then you're gonna make it and stuff," Saintfort said.

Larry N. Rein, CEO of ChildNet, reflected on the meaning behind the graduation celebration and acknowledged the collective effort that made these student successes possible. He recognized the contributions of case workers employed by ChildNet and the Department of Children and Family Services, while placing special emphasis on the dedication of the graduates themselves. "This evening is the fruition of great work by everyone in the community, including case workers at ChildNet, DCF and especially the kids who have worked so hard. We are to celebrate them!" Rein said. The ceremony served as a testament to the organization's mission and the resilience of young people overcoming adversity through education and community support.

Residents in Martin, St.

Jupiter Island erosion threatens to split island at Peck Lake, putting wildlife refuges and archaeological sites at risk

A new state report warns that nearly the entire coastline of Jupiter Island is critically eroded, with the ocean potentially breaking through at Peck Lake and splitting the island in half.

Yesterday, 4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report warns that Jupiter Island's entire 11.5-mile shoreline has eroded so severely that ocean water could break through at Peck Lake, potentially splitting the island in half by turning a thin strip of land into an open inlet. The threat stretches from Blowing Rocks Preserve, where private development is already at risk, to Peck Lake. A storm previously breached Peck Lake in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers refilled it with sand. Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society warned that a future breach could upend ecosystems that depend on a delicate balance of fresh and saltwater to survive. Perry noted there are over 33 threatened and endangered species in the affected areas. A second state assessment warned that erosion threatens mangrove swamps critical for habitat and coastal protection, while rising water levels could displace entire species on the island.

Among the sites at risk is the Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old archaeological site already half lost to erosion, along with Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park. Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said the town fights erosion through constant maintenance. In April 2025, the town completed a beach renourishment project adding sand equivalent to nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. The town spends about $3 million annually in taxpayer dollars on beach renourishment. Field warned that if funding for beach renourishment disappeared, a breach could require massive state and federal investment for repairs.

Florida shark diving operators fight federal feeding ban in state waters

A federal proposal to ban shark feeding within 200 miles of Florida's coast has cleared the U.S. House, drawing sharp criticism from dive operators who warn it could devastate conservation-driven tourism businesses.

Yesterday, 5:55 PM ET

Florida's shark diving industry is fighting a federal proposal to ban shark feeding off the state's coast. The measure, H.R. 3831, known as the Safe Florida Seas Act, would prohibit shark feeding in federal waters within 200 miles of Florida, with an exception for harvesting. Nathan Campbell, owner of Emerald Charters, said Congress has not asked operators to present data or explain their practices. Operators say they avoid surface chumming and instead take bait underwater during dives, which they argue is less likely to condition sharks to approach boats looking for food, unlike some commercial harvesting operations that release hundreds of gallons of chum at the surface.

In his letter to Congress, Campbell is urging senators to delay action, gather research and involve all stakeholders in shaping new rules. Dr. Matt Ajemian, an associate research professor at Florida Atlantic University, says shark diving in Florida has a near perfect safety record for customers. Campbell said the industry is not scared of accountability and is requesting the chance to speak and share the science and facts with lawmakers. The bill cites Hawaii's 2006 ban on baited shark diving in federal waters as precedent, but Florida operators argue that since Hawaii's ban, shark incidents have increased.
