4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report has warned that Jupiter Island's entire 11.5-mile coastline is experiencing severe erosion. The erosion poses such a significant risk that the ocean could break through at Peck Lake, potentially splitting the island in half. This threat extends from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake, an area where a previous storm breach occurred in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers refilled it with sand. A future breach could transform a thin strip of land into an open inlet, fundamentally altering the island's geography.

The ecological and cultural consequences of such a breach would be devastating. Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society explained that a breach could destroy ecosystems dependent on the precise balance between fresh and saltwater environments. The report identifies more than 33 threatened and endangered species living in these areas, along with critical habitats including mangrove swamps that provide both wildlife shelter and coastal protection. Rising water levels could displace entire species from the island. Among the most at-risk sites is the Joseph Reed Mound, an archaeological site between 3,000 and 4,000 years old that has already lost half its area to erosion. Wildlife refuges like Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park are also in danger.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field acknowledged that the town combats erosion through continuous maintenance efforts. In April 2025, the town completed a major beach renourishment project that added enough sand to fill nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Field emphasized that oceanfront homes remain safe from immediate danger only because of this ongoing work. "We are just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over," Field said. "We will continue to do that as long as there's money to do it." The town currently spends approximately $3 million in local taxpayer dollars annually on beach renourishment.

However, this funding strategy is not sustainable indefinitely. If Jupiter Island experiences a catastrophic breach, repairs would demand massive investment from state and federal governments. Field warned of the broader implications for Florida's coastline, stating that "if funding ever goes away for beach renourishment in Florida, the coastline of Florida is going to change very quickly, and not in a way that anybody's particularly happy with." Field indicated that the new FDEP assessment could help Jupiter Island compete more effectively for state and federal funding to address erosion. Sea levels have already risen significantly since the 1950s, with the rate of increase accelerating over the past 15 years.

The Florida Supreme Court recently adopted new rules requiring attorneys and self-represented litigants to certify that what they cite in court exists and is accurate. The rules take effect June 15 in response to what many in the legal and AI field have described as a growing crisis: the misuse of artificial intelligence in courtrooms. Fake evidence, fabricated facts and bogus case law have become so much of a concern in Florida courtrooms that the state's highest court was compelled to act. Sanctions for violations could range from a public reprimand to fines and even suspension.

Federal Judge William Matthewman, the chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has been involved in efforts to control how AI is used in courts after its misuse became rampant in his own courtroom. He said the situation is unlikely to improve quickly, predicting that it will "get worse before it gets better." In Polk County, an attorney representing the Polk County School District in an ongoing lawsuit over school bus cameras was recently chastised after a judge discovered fabricated facts and cited cases that don't exist were filed as part of a motion to dismiss the case. The attorney said he used ChatGPT as a drafting and research aid but admitted there was no excuse for his failure to verify before filing. A school district spokesperson said they are reviewing this matter to determine if the attorney will be eligible for contracted services again in the future.

Hillsborough County Commissioner Joshua Wostal indicated the Tampa Bay Rays ballpark deal could face resistance from the Tampa Community Redevelopment Agency in the coming week. During an appearance on The Ryan Gorman Show, Wostal suggested the CRA may vote to remove the Rays memorandum of understanding from its upcoming agenda, which could force the deal back to the negotiating table. The current MOU promises $80 million from the city of Tampa and $100 million from the CRA, along with additional public funding.

Wostal emphasized that the Drew Park CRA, the only legal body authorized to approve CRA dollars, declined to vote on the proposal at its last meeting and postponed action until June. He noted that county attorneys confirmed no executed MOU has been advertised because neither the Tampa Mayor nor the Tampa City Council can approve CRA funds. If the CRA votes to remove the item from the agenda, the entire MOU structure changes and the agreement must return to all parties for passage. Some Tampa City Council members, including swing vote Bill Carlson, have expressed frustration that the Tampa Bay Rays negotiated primarily with Mayor Jane Castor while excluding council members and their requests from discussions. Carlson told Florida Politics the Rays failed to negotiate meaningfully with council since the MOU was approved by the city and county last month.

Wostal voiced multiple concerns about the financial aspects of the proposal. He stated the county has not received sufficient information to justify the subsidy size, including detailed site plans, proof of financing, or documentation supporting the team's claimed $2.3 billion project cost. An outside review raised questions about projections that the development would generate $6 billion in tax revenue over 35 years, with Wostal arguing the actual return could be substantially lower. He criticized the current MOU for using local tax dollars, disaster recovery money, infrastructure funds, and potentially public safety funds to support the stadium. Instead, Wostal argued the stadium should be funded through user fees and tourism tax dollars alone.

Wostal also highlighted other upcoming sports facility obligations facing Hillsborough County, including potential renovations at Raymond James Stadium and future needs at Amalie Arena. He emphasized that funding requirements for the Tampa Bay Buccaneers and competing community investment priorities make protecting taxpayer dollars essential. While Wostal said he opposes keeping the Rays in the Tampa Bay area, he stated he cannot support a deal without stronger financial protections. "I can't sell people down the river," Wostal said on the radio show.

Fertilizer ban begins June 1 in St. Lucie, Martin and Indian River counties for rainy season

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen. The restriction continues through September 30, covering the entire rainy season when nutrient runoff poses the greatest environmental risk. Jordan Skaggs, who works with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the ban a...

5:36 PM ET

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen. The restriction continues through September 30, covering the entire rainy season when nutrient runoff poses the greatest environmental risk.

Jordan Skaggs, who works with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the ban aims to prevent nutrient-laden runoff from entering local waterways during heavy rains. When excess nutrients reach water bodies like the Indian River lagoon and St. Lucie River, they can trigger algae blooms that damage these ecosystems. The algae can block sunlight that beneficial aquatic plants need to survive, harming the fish and wildlife that depend on those plants for food and shelter. In some cases, these harmful algae blooms become dense enough to produce toxins that threaten human health, wildlife populations and the overall health of the ecosystem.

Rather than using traditional fertilizers during the ban period, Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as a practical alternative for gardens and landscaping. Ricketts describes composting as simple and effective, comparing it to using a small trash can. He notes that composting represents the easiest and most practical method for adding necessary nutrients to landscaped areas without contributing to the nutrient runoff problem.

Residents who maintain yards and gardens during these four months should plan ahead to switch to composting methods or delay fertilizer applications until October. This temporary restriction protects the region's valuable waterways during the season when rainfall is heaviest and runoff most likely to carry chemicals into rivers and lagoons. By reducing nutrient pollution during this critical period, the three-county ban helps protect both the natural environment and public health in the area.

5:36 PM ET

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop using fertilizers containing phosphorus or nitrogen on their lawns and landscaping. The restriction will remain in place through September 30, covering a four-month period during the region's rainy season. The ban applies equally to all three Treasure Coast counties and will lift after the summer months end.

The seasonal prohibition aims to address a significant environmental concern. Jordan Skaggs, with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the ban targets nutrient-rich runoff that flows into waterways during heavy rains. This excess nutrients can trigger algae blooms in sensitive ecosystems like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. These blooms create a cascade of ecological problems, as Skaggs noted: "It can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife." Beyond ecological damage, the blooms pose direct threats to public health. According to Skaggs, "At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health."

For residents seeking to maintain their gardens and landscaping during the ban period, experts recommend practical alternatives. Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, suggests composting as an effective substitute. Rather than requiring complex systems, Ricketts noted that "a small trash can" can serve as an accessible composting method. This approach provides nutrients to landscapes without relying on commercial fertilizers that contain restricted nutrients.

Residents should prepare now for the June 1 start date by using any remaining nitrogen or phosphorus fertilizers before the deadline. After September 30, when the ban ends, normal fertilizer applications can resume. The four-month restriction represents a collaborative effort by the three counties to protect the region's waterways and ecosystems during the season when rainfall is heaviest and runoff poses the greatest threat to water quality.

Florida Attorney General Files Bill to Increase Penalties for Harming Police Animals

Attorney General Ashley Moody filed legislation to strengthen penalties for anyone who intentionally harms law enforcement animals, including canine officers and patrol horses.

4:03 PM ET

Attorney General Ashley Moody, a Plant City Republican, filed the LEO K-9 Protection Act to strengthen penalties for anyone who intentionally harms law enforcement animals. The legislation treats violence against police animals as an aggravating factor similar to assaults on law enforcement officers. According to Moody, highly trained animals help law enforcement apprehend dangerous criminals, detect narcotics and explosives, assist with searches, find missing persons, and save lives. She stated that law enforcement animals serving with brave men and women deserve protection in return.

The legislation is named for K-9 Leo, a Marion County canine deputy who was shot and killed in the line of duty in 2024. In addition to increasing penalties for using deadly weapons against law enforcement animals, the bill directs the U.S. Department of Transportation to issue regulations permitting emergency medical service providers to transport injured police animals at their discretion. The measure also addresses patrol horses in addition to canine officers. The bill mirrors legislation already introduced in the House by Rep. Aaron Bean, a Fernandina Beach Republican.
