Rosa Mena, 50, of Port St. Lucie has been sentenced to three years in prison and five years of probation after operating an unlicensed medical spa out of a shed behind her home on the 5000 block of Northwest Coosa Drive. Mena ran the business, called Miracle Hand and Spa, performing Botox injections and other cosmetic procedures without a valid medical license.

A victim came forward to Port St. Lucie Police in May 2025 after experiencing partial facial paralysis following a session in which Mena administered 62 units of Botox. The victim had been referred to Mena by coworkers who had previously used her services. When the victim reported the paralysis, Mena refunded the $325 fee and asked her to return for another injection of vitamins to reverse the problem. The paralysis persisted, prompting the victim to request proof of Mena's medical credentials. Mena provided an altered phlebotomy certificate that had expired in February 2024. She also falsely claimed to be a doctor from the Dominican Republic.

A multi-agency investigation involving Port St. Lucie Police, PSL Code Enforcement, the PSL Building Department, PSL Business Tax, and the Florida Department of Health led to a search warrant at Mena's residence. Detectives found medical beds, fat sculpting machines, laser hair removal equipment, laser liposuction machines, and injection devices for fillers and Botox inside the backyard shed.

Mena was convicted on multiple charges including three counts of aggravated battery causing bodily harm, three counts of practicing medicine without an active license, fraud charges for obtaining property valued at $300 or more, and use of a two-way communication device to facilitate a felony. The case highlights the dangers of unlicensed cosmetic procedures and the importance of verifying medical credentials before receiving treatment.

22 hours ago

Dozens of Treasure Coast residents gathered at Blake Library in Martin County to support efforts to save the Oculina Bank, a rare deep-water coral reef found nowhere else on Earth. The reef, located just off the coast of Fort Pierce hundreds of feet below the surface, faces an uncertain future after a federally appointed council voted to roll back longstanding protections that have safeguarded the ecosystem for decades.

The Oculina Bank is home to more than 2,000 marine species and thrives in deep, dark waters where most coral cannot survive. Scientists say trawling destroyed as much as 90% of the reef before federal protections were put in place. Dr. Grant Gilmore, a scientist who previously dove the rare reef, warned that removing habitat would devastate fish populations. "Very concerned about an ecosystem point of view, where your fish don't exist in a vacuum, they need a habitat, they need food," Gilmore said. "The coral reef was giving them habitat and food. You take that away, you don't have the fish."

Two proposals would reopen parts of the reef to shrimp trawling, following a Trump Executive order issued in April 2025 that directed eight regional councils nationwide to find ways to reinvigorate fishing industries. Bob Zales, executive director of the Southeastern Fishing Association and a generational shrimper, told local reporters he believes reopening shrimping access is essential for the struggling rock shrimp industry. Council data shows the rock shrimp industry has plummeted from over 700 trips per year in the mid-1990s to under 100 since 2010, with the number of active rock shrimpers dropping from 144 in 1996 to less than 30, and just 12 active boats in 2018.

Mark Perry, Executive Director of the Florida Oceanographic Society, joined Gilmore in urging the public to help save the reef. "Corals are in trouble worldwide. We shouldn't be destroying anymore," Gilmore said. Scientists at the gathering emphasized that the corals grow very slowly and have been there for a long time, and that snapper and grouper in the area rely on the reef ecosystem. The federal agency is preparing a final ruling on the fate of the Oculina Bank protections.

Major Ruben Romero said Johnny Batista, 19, Roberto Hernandez-Castro, 39, and Roberto Aldana-Ferrera, 31 were involved in a series of burglaries at hardware stores across Martin, Palm Beach and St. Lucie counties from December 2025 to April 2026.

At least 40 stores across 13 counties were burglarized, with stolen goods taken back to Miami-Dade County where the suspects lived to be sold. Roughly $500,000 worth of property was stolen, in addition to damage to stores. According to the affidavit from Martin County Sheriff's Office, hardware stores in Stuart, Fort Pierce, Lake Worth, Delray Beach, Boynton Beach, West Palm Beach and Jupiter were targeted, as were stores in Broward County.

The crews used a sledgehammer or other blunt object to smash the glass and often completed the thefts within minutes, before police arrived or alarms were triggered. Investigators say Batista is connected to the first known theft at Sewell Hardware in Stuart in December 2025. Romero said the thefts impact the entire community through higher costs. "There's already inflation that we notice severely in our state, and the more they steal, the more damage they cause, the more expensive things are. People can't go to work because they can't afford the tools."

Aldana-Ferrera is in custody in Georgia, Batista was arrested in February for a vehicle theft and is in Martin County Jail, and Hernandez-Castro has a warrant out for his arrest. A fourth suspect, Maikell Fleitas, is deceased. The suspects are facing charges of racketeering, burglary over $1,000 in damage while masked, burglary of an occupied structure and burglary of an unoccupied structure. Romero said there are burglaries that happened previous to December, and investigators are still looking for one other suspect.

2 hours ago

The bill would increase the homestead exemption from $50,000 to $250,000 over two years, with inflation adjustments beginning in 2029. The measure would also lower assessment caps on non-homesteaded properties from 10% to 5%. Voters must approve the proposal with 60% support in November before it can take effect.

Local experts are divided on what the ripple effects could mean for Treasure Coast residents. Real estate attorney Steve Wallace, based in Boynton Beach, raised the possibility of new fees tied to local services. "There could potentially be non-ad valorem taxes assigned to the sheriff's office, fire rescue, some of the other things. Additionally, there may be some increased fees because the only other way that the county or the local governments can derive revenue," Wallace said. CPA Paul Miller suggested businesses could end up carrying more of the financial burden to offset lost revenue.

Municipal attorney Raul Gastesi offered a different perspective, suggesting the measure could push home prices higher. "I expect home prices could actually increase because now home buyers have more money at their disposal, and that in the past has resulted in home prices going up," Gastesi said. Realtor Danielle Blake was skeptical of that theory, pointing to interest rates as a more significant barrier to sales than property taxes alone. All four experts acknowledged there are significant unknowns about how counties, municipalities and markets might respond to decreasing property tax revenue. As with any ballot measure that passes, lawmakers would need to return to Tallahassee to work out the specifics in a bill to implement the changes.

Activists rally in Martin County to save rare deep-sea coral reef off Fort Pierce

Dozens gathered at Blake Library after a federally appointed council voted to roll back protections for the Oculina Bank, a unique deep-water coral reef found nowhere else on Earth.

22 hours ago

Dozens of community members gathered at the Blake Library in Martin County after a federally appointed council voted to roll back longstanding protections for the Oculina Bank, a rare deep-water coral reef found nowhere else on Earth. Located just off the coast of Fort Pierce, the reef sits hundreds of feet below the surface and thrives in deep, dark waters, unlike most coral that needs shallow sunlight to survive. The Oculina Bank is home to more than 2,000 marine species.

For months, two proposals have been under consideration that would reopen parts of the reef to shrimp trawling, even though scientists say trawling destroyed as much as 90 percent of the reef before federal protections were put in place decades ago. Dr. Grant Gilmore, a scientist who previously dived the reef, said the coral provides essential habitat and food for fish. Mark Perry, Executive Director of the Florida Oceanographic Society, and Gilmore both urged the public to help save the reef at the gathering. The proposals followed a Trump Executive order issued in April 2025 that tasked eight regional councils nationwide to find ways to reinvigorate the fishing industries.

Bob Zales, executive director of the Southeastern Fishing Association and a generational shrimper, said reopening shrimping access to the Oculina Bank is essential for the struggling rock shrimp industry. Council data shows the rock shrimp industry has plummeted from over 700 trips per year in the mid-1990s to under 100 since 2010, with the number of active rock shrimpers dropping from 144 in 1996 to less than 30, and just 12 active boats in 2018. Scientists warned reopening the reef could irreparably damage what little remains of the coral ecosystem.
