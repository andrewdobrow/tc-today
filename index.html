Proposed homestead exemption expansion could cost Martin County over $67 million

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could provide tax relief for homeowners, but critics worry the benefit may come at the expense of renters if local governments need to raise rates to compensate for lost revenue. According to analysis from the Florida Policy Institute, Martin County could lose more than $67 million if voters approve the amendment in November...

Yesterday, 4:49 PM ET

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could provide tax relief for homeowners, but critics worry the benefit may come at the expense of renters if local governments need to raise rates to compensate for lost revenue. According to analysis from the Florida Policy Institute, Martin County could lose more than $67 million if voters approve the amendment in November. The statewide impact could be even more significant, with local governments across Florida potentially losing nearly $4.8 billion in total revenue.

Mike Readling, executive director of Habitat for Humanity of Martin County, expressed concern that the proposed tax cut would not help the people his organization typically serves. He noted a recent increase in people seeking assistance with rent and bills, with 95 percent being renters. "The housing situation is so dire. It's gotten progressively worse. We have families coming in everyday," Readling said. He believes the exemption would not benefit the demographic his organization works with. Attorney John Tolley explained that local governments facing budget shortfalls would have limited options: reduce services, use reserve funds, or increase millage rates.

Realtor Michael Caputo acknowledged that if millage rates rise, landlords without homestead exemptions could face higher tax bills and potentially pass those costs to renters. However, he noted the amendment would also lower the property tax cap on non-homesteaded properties, including commercial apartment complexes, from 10 percent down to 5 percent. A 2025 Florida Senate analysis found only 24 affordable and available rental units exist for every 100 extremely low-income renters in the state.

Caputo expressed skepticism that municipalities would actually raise millage rates, suggesting the market dynamics might prevent significant rent increases. "If they're paying more for their property, they're going to try and increase that on the tenant and give it a try. If it doesn't happen for them, then they'll have to readjust their pricing," Caputo said. Attorney Tolley added that the lower tax cap on rental properties could potentially lead to reduced rents. Despite these competing perspectives, the core concern remains whether the homestead exemption expansion will ultimately burden renters who do not benefit from the tax break while local governments grapple with reduced revenue.

Located behind iron gates in a South Miami industrial park, the Drug Enforcement Administration's Southeast Regional Drug Laboratory stands as the busiest DEA facility in the country. The lab receives approximately 70 percent of all drug evidence seized by the agency nationwide, a volume that reflects South Florida's critical role in the nation's drug supply chain. Allen Catterton, who has worked as a chemist for the DEA for 28 years, directs operations at the facility. The lab's 20 chemists analyze seized narcotics from the Southeast and Caribbean regions, with cocaine representing the dominant substance they encounter.

South Florida's geography makes it particularly vulnerable to drug trafficking. The region's coastal location and proximity to South America have established it as a major entry point for illegal drug smuggling, both by land and sea. This strategic positioning explains why cocaine remains the primary focus of the lab's work. Forensic chemists like Jenna Hamilton and Alyssa Sanchez spend their days testing seized drugs to determine their composition and verify their identity. Hamilton demonstrated the initial testing process for cocaine samples, using chemical solutions to identify substances. Last year, chemists at the lab testified in federal court approximately 152 times, translating their chemical findings into legal evidence.

The lab's mission has become increasingly complex as America's illegal drug supply evolves. Chemists now regularly encounter sophisticated synthetic opioids like fentanyl and carfentanil, a powerful animal tranquilizer that is exponentially more potent than fentanyl. One sample Sanchez analyzed contained roughly 16 percent fentanyl mixed with carfentanil, creating an extremely dangerous substance. To address these emerging threats, the lab has invested in advanced equipment including a Nuclear Magnetic Resonance machine that functions similarly to an MRI, breaking down unknown narcotics to the molecular level. According to Catterton, "We're constantly playing catch-up" as drug manufacturers continuously design new compounds intended to circumvent existing controlled substance laws.

The evidence vault currently stores approximately 50,000 pieces of evidence worth billions of dollars on the streets, with cocaine comprising most of the stockpile. Drugs remain in storage until their corresponding court cases conclude, a process that can extend from months to decades. Once cases close, the accumulated narcotics are destroyed in an incinerator. Last year alone, the facility incinerated roughly 300,000 pounds of drugs, averaging approximately 15 tons monthly. This behind-the-scenes operation represents a crucial but rarely publicized component of America's drug enforcement efforts, where scientific expertise works to understand and combat an ever-shifting black market that continues to claim lives.

Rapids Water Park in Riviera Beach has discontinued its interim youth supervision policy that previously required visitors under 18 to have an adult accompany them. The park operates this policy on a flexible basis, adjusting it based on collaboration with local law enforcement and monitoring of social media activity. The requirement is no longer currently in place.

Tina Hatcher, speaking on behalf of the park, emphasized the facility's dedication to maintaining a safe and family-friendly atmosphere for all guests. She noted that Rapids Water Park partners with local officials and tracks social media trends while enforcing comprehensive safety measures including a formal code of conduct, police officers stationed on the premises, and visible security personnel. The interim supervision policy was introduced specifically for guests aged 17 and younger to help create a positive experience for the entire park community.

During the time the policy was active, it established clear requirements for minors under 18. Any young person in this age group had to be accompanied by a parent, legal guardian, or another responsible adult who was at least 21 years old and part of their group for the visit. Adults serving as chaperones needed to present a valid government-issued photo identification that included their date of birth when entering the park. They were also required to remain at the facility for the entire duration of the visit and could supervise a maximum of five minors per day.

For guests planning a visit to Rapids Water Park, additional information about current policies and procedures is available on the park's official website at rapidswaterpark.com. Potential visitors are encouraged to review the website before arriving to familiarize themselves with any guidelines or requirements that may be in effect.

Jacquelyn Koester launched a Little Free Pantry outside her Hobe Sound home to help neighbors manage the impact of rising food and gas costs. What began as a simple Facebook post has grown into a community resource that provides basic necessities to anyone in need without judgment. The pantry, located behind the Hobe Sound Public Library, stocks food items and baby essentials for families facing financial hardship. "I knew the second I posted on that group that Hobe Sound was going to show up for us because that's what we do, we are a tight knit community," Koester said.

The pantry quickly became popular as families struggled with inflation. Koester found herself constantly restocking shelves as items disappeared rapidly. "When gas prices went up and food prices went up it was just emptying about faster than I can replace it," she explained. Rather than become discouraged, Koester turned to the community for support through a Facebook appeal. The response exceeded her expectations, with neighbors regularly donating items to keep the shelves stocked and available for those who needed assistance.

The impact of the pantry extended beyond her immediate neighborhood. One person anonymously posted in the Facebook group about traveling from Jupiter to access the pantry's resources. "Someone posted in the Facebook group anonymously saying I drove up from Jupiter for this, this is going to help get me through my week," Koester shared. These messages of gratitude and stories of genuine need reinforce her commitment to maintaining the pantry and ensuring it remains accessible to anyone in the community who requires help.

Koester reflects on how community support has evolved while remaining strong. She believes that while the methods of helping one another have changed, the underlying desire to support neighbors persists. "The saying it takes a village is so true and the villages look a lot different than they used to, but people still show up for each other and people want to help," she said. The pantry represents more than just a collection of donated items; it has become a symbol of community connection and mutual support during challenging economic times. For those interested in accessing the pantry or learning more about how to contribute, Koester maintains a Facebook group with information and updates about the initiative.

Rapids Water Park in Riviera Beach ends youth supervision policy requiring adult chaperones

Rapids Water Park has ended its interim policy requiring guests ages 17 and younger to be accompanied by an adult chaperone.

10:26 AM ET

Rapids Water Park in Riviera Beach has ended its interim youth supervision policy, which required guests ages 17 and younger to be accompanied by an adult chaperone. The park said the policy was intermittent and pulled in and out based on work with local authorities and social media trends. It is no longer in effect.

While in effect, the policy required minors ages 17 and under to be accompanied by a parent, legal guardian or other responsible adult who was at least 21 years old and had traveled to the park with the minor's group. Adult chaperones were required to present a valid government-issued ID showing both a photograph and date of birth upon entry, remain on-site for the duration of the visit and were limited to supervising no more than five guests ages 17 and under per day. Guests can visit Rapids Water Park's website at rapidswaterpark.com for additional details before planning their visit.
