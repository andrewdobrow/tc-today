The Fort Pierce Planning Board on Monday reviewed the Causeway Cove development project, a proposal that would bring five high-rise buildings to the area off Seaway Drive. The tallest structure would reach 17 stories and 192 feet, more than four times the city's height limit. Plans also call for two hotels and more than 70,000 square feet of retail space. The development qualifies under the Live Local Act, a state law passed in 2023 that allows developers to bypass height and density restrictions as long as they set aside 40% of units for affordable housing for at least 30 years. The project includes more than 900 apartments and condos, with 365 of those designated as affordable housing.

Neighbors packed the chamber during Monday's planning board meeting to voice concerns about the project's impact on the area. Buster Brewster, a boater at Causeway Cove Marina, said he worries the project will change the character of the area. "We're going to put this big old conglomerate up here, and I'm just afraid it's going to take away from the beauty of this place," Brewster said. Dennis Fein attended the meeting and raised concerns about traffic. "Nobody's thought this out," Fein said. "You're going to have massive traffic jams. Nobody's going to be able to get on the island, off the island."

City Planning Director Kev Freeman said developers must add traffic mitigation efforts and any required intersection or access improvements. City Commissioner Michael Broderick told attendees that the city has no authority over whether the project is approved. "The city does not have a play here. We do not have a position in this. This is regulated by the state of Florida in the entirety. The city has been eliminated from the process," Broderick said.

City commissioners are scheduled to hear a review of the project as the next step in the process. The Live Local Act's provisions allow developers to exceed local zoning restrictions in exchange for dedicating a substantial portion of units to affordable housing. The law requires that 40% of residential units remain affordable for at least 30 years, which in this project translates to 365 units out of the total planned housing.

5:12 PM ET

Florida Democrats say recent special-election victories demonstrate they can break Republican supermajorities in the state Legislature during the 2026 midterm elections. Florida Democratic Party Chair Nikki Fried said the party has flipped more than 30 local and special-election seats since the 2024 election, though this tally includes local offices and not solely Florida Legislature seats. The party attributed the wins to its Pendulum Project, a year-round organizing operation designed to maintain voter contact between major elections, along with more than 3,000 volunteers, increased candidate recruitment and an advantage in requests for vote-by-mail ballots. Fried said the latest special-election results provide a proof of concept that the party's organizing and affordability message can work, including in districts Trump previously carried.

The climb remains steep for Democrats. Republicans currently hold supermajorities in both chambers of the Florida Legislature, at or above the two-thirds threshold. A two-thirds majority amounts to 80 of the 120 House seats and 27 of the 40 Senate seats when every member is present. Democrats also face a voter-registration deficit of more than 1.5 million. As of May 31, Florida had about 5.54 million active registered Republicans and 4.03 million Democrats, according to the state Division of Elections. Another 3.32 million voters were registered without a party affiliation. Gov. Ron DeSantis defeated Democrat Charlie Crist by roughly 19 percentage points in 2022. In 2024, Trump carried Florida over Kamala Harris by about 13 points, the largest Republican presidential margin in the state since 1988.

Democrats are building their message around affordability, blaming nearly three decades of Republican control for rising housing, property insurance, healthcare and everyday living costs. The party will also challenge a Republican-backed property tax amendment headed to voters, arguing that cutting local government revenue could threaten services or shift costs elsewhere. Fried said if Republicans wanted to reduce the cost of living for everyday Floridians, they could have done that through the legislative process. Instead, she said, they are making local governments the big boogeyman. Senate Democratic Leader-designate Tracie Davis said the party has put in place the programs needed to capitalize and break these supermajorities once and for all.

Democrats will continue making their case at Leadership Blue, the state party's annual gathering, scheduled for July 17 through 19 at Disney's Coronado Springs Resort in Lake Buena Vista. Former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg will deliver the keynote address at the July 18 gala. Political analyst Susan MacManus said Democrats must generate enthusiasm among supporters who have skipped recent elections while also winning a sizable share of unaffiliated voters to overcome their registration deficit.

Martin County faces $2.1 billion transportation funding gap over next 20 years

5:41 PM ET

Martin County is facing a $2.1 billion transportation funding shortfall that could shape how residents get around for decades. The Martin County Metropolitan Planning Organization estimates the county will need approximately $2.8 billion for transportation improvements over the next 20 years, but only $701 million is currently available, meaning the county can fund just about one quarter of its identified needs. Of the $701 million currently available, all but $10 million comes from state and federal funding sources. Beth Beltran, administrator for Martin County's Metropolitan Planning Organization, said funding shortfalls of this scale are common, adding she does not know of one MPO in the whole country where the needs are equal to the revenue.

Nine roads the county identified as requiring widening as traffic grows remain unfunded. The total cost to widen those roads is approximately $213 million, leaving all nine projects out of the county's draft Cost Feasible Plan. Those roads include SW Martin Downs Boulevard, SR-714/SW Martin Highway, SW 96th Street, SW Bridge Road, CR-714/SW Martin Highway, SW Allapattah Road/CR-609, SE Green River Parkway, SW Indiantown Avenue, and SE Bridge Road/CR-708. May and Maddy Brumlow, who travel Martin Downs Boulevard nearly every day, said the congestion is already noticeable. Maddy Brumlow said there is always traffic and it is always backed up. When told about the scale of the funding gap, May Brumlow asked if there was an itemized bill where they could break it down.

The draft Cost Feasible Plan totals approximately $746 million in year-of-expenditure dollars and covers a range of transportation categories. Highway and roadway projects receive $213.97 million, including SR-710 improvements, widening of Cove Road, SW Newfield Parkway, High Meadow Avenue, and a new Willoughby Boulevard Extension. Turnpike projects receive $274.81 million, including a new interchange study at milepost 125, direct connect ramps, and widening north of SR-714 to north of Becker Road. Transit receives $160.4 million, covering existing fixed route and paratransit service, new microtransit zones, new fixed route service along Kanner Highway and a Jensen Beach Trolley, and capital improvements including fleet replacement and bus stop upgrades. Other categories include $23.9 million for Transportation System Management and Operations, $34.7 million for Complete Streets and non-motorized improvements, $44.02 million for aviation capital improvements at Witham Field Airport, $27.3 million for Strategic Intermodal System projects, and $10.4 million for other improvements.

State Road 710 is among the county's highest priorities due to repeated safety concerns. Beltran said fatal crashes as well as serious injury crashes were just enormous on that corridor. The road improvements are estimated at about $300 million. The draft plan includes several SR-710 projects that are already funded or programmed, including a realignment at CR-714/Martin Highway, widening from CR-609 to SW Van Buren Road, and additional widening segments totaling nearly 16 miles. Residents can submit input to the Martin County MPO by contacting Principal Planner Ricardo Vazquez at rvazquez@martin.fl.us or by calling 772-223-7983.

Registration is now open for Port St. Lucie City University, a free nine-session program designed to help residents better understand how their local government operates.

Classes begin Wednesday, Aug. 12, and will be held from 5:30 to 8:30 p.m. in the Council Chambers at Port St. Lucie City Hall, located at 121 SW Port St. Lucie Blvd.

Participants will learn about City departments, municipal services and the decision-making processes that shape the community. The program also gives residents opportunities to meet City staff, ask questions and gain a closer look at the work involved in operating one of Florida's largest cities.

Residents who complete the course will receive a certificate of completion and may also choose to become a City Ambassador, helping share information about City programs and services throughout the community.

City University is intended to encourage civic engagement while strengthening connections between residents and local government. Since the program began in 2016, more than 600 residents have participated.

Enrollment is limited to 160 participants.

Residents who are unable to attend the in-person classes can also access City U on Demand, which offers a more flexible way to learn about Port St. Lucie government and municipal operations.

The Jupiter Inlet Lighthouse stands as a 165-year-old sentinel on Florida's Atlantic coast, where its red brick tower has guided mariners since 1860. Built at the base of the Jupiter Inlet, the lighthouse continues to function as an active navigational aid while welcoming visitors who want to explore its history and experience the views from its observation gallery. The structure has served continuously as a beacon for ships navigating the coastal waters, maintaining its original purpose while also opening its doors to the public.

Reaching the top of the lighthouse requires climbing 105 steps up a narrow spiral staircase that winds through the interior of the brick tower. This is the same route that lighthouse keepers climbed night after night throughout history to maintain the powerful Fresnel lens that projected light across the water. The spiral staircase follows the path of these early keepers who ensured the beacon remained operational for passing ships. The climb takes visitors upward through the tower to the gallery that sits at the top of the structure.

The observation gallery provides panoramic views that stretch across multiple waterways and the ocean. The Atlantic Ocean extends to the east, while below the tower, the turquoise waters of the Jupiter Inlet converge with the Loxahatchee and Indian rivers. Visitors standing at the elevated vantage point can watch boats glide through the channel as sea breezes sweep across the observation area. The commanding perspective reveals why this particular location proved ideal for a lighthouse, offering clear sightlines across the coastal landscape and waterways.

The museum grounds preserve more than just the tower itself. Restored historic buildings chronicle the lighthouse's journey from its construction before the Civil War through its years as an active Coast Guard station. These structures provide information about the lighthouse's role in regional maritime history and offer details about the daily lives of the keepers who operated the station. The site maintains the supporting buildings that allowed the lighthouse station to function across more than a century and a half of continuous service, preserving the complete story of this working navigational aid that has marked the Florida coastline for generations of mariners.
