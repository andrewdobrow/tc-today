Community rallies to save rare coral reef off Fort Pierce as federal decision looms

Yesterday, 1:28 AM ET

Dozens of residents gathered at Blake Library in Martin County to fight for the Oculina Bank, a rare deep-water coral reef located just off the coast of Fort Pierce. A federally appointed council recently voted to roll back longstanding protections for the reef, which is home to more than 2,000 marine species and found nowhere else on Earth. The reef thrives in deep, dark waters hundreds of feet below the surface, unlike most coral that needs shallow sunlight to survive.

Two proposals now under consideration would reopen parts of the reef to shrimp trawling, even though scientists say trawling destroyed as much as 90% of the reef before federal protections were put in place decades ago. Dr. Grant Gilmore, a scientist who previously dived the rare reef, warned that removing the habitat would devastate the ecosystem. "Your fish don't exist in a vacuum, they need a habitat, they need food," Gilmore said. "The coral reef was giving them habitat and food. You take that away, you don't have the fish." Mark Perry, Executive Director of the Florida Oceanographic Society, joined Gilmore in urging the public to help save the reef at the gathering.

The proposals followed a Trump executive order issued in April 2025 that tasked eight regional councils nationwide to find ways to reinvigorate the fishing industries. Bob Zales, executive director of the Southeastern Fishing Association and a generational shrimper, told reporters he believes reopening shrimping access to the Oculina Bank is essential for the struggling rock shrimp industry. "The shrimping industry is teetering on the brink of extinction," Zales said. Council data shows the rock shrimp industry has plummeted from over 700 trips per year in the mid-1990s to under 100 since 2010, with the number of active rock shrimpers dropping from 144 in 1996 to less than 30, with just 12 active boats in 2018.

Scientists warned that reopening the reef could irreparably damage what little remains of the slow-growing coral. "They grow very, very slowly, and they've been there for a long time," Perry said. Gilmore added: "Corals are in trouble worldwide. We shouldn't be destroying anymore." The federal agency is now preparing its final ruling on whether to allow shrimp trawling in areas that have been protected for decades.

Yesterday, 5:03 PM ET

More than 100 paddlers will cross 80 miles of open Atlantic Ocean this June to raise awareness and funds for families living with cystic fibrosis, arriving at Lake Worth Beach after their overnight journey. Participants will launch from Bimini, Bahamas, at midnight on June 13 and paddle for 12 to 16 hours before reaching the beach, where spectators are invited to cheer them on during a daylong celebration.

The annual Crossing for Cystic Fibrosis benefits the Pipers Angels Foundation, established by event founder Travis Suit in honor of his daughter, Piper, who was diagnosed with cystic fibrosis at age 4. Suit, an avid paddler, was inspired to create the event after learning about the natural respiratory benefits of ocean salt air. The foundation provides direct support for cystic fibrosis patients and families, connecting them with resources and community.

The crossing is part of a four-day schedule beginning June 10 with arrivals and a poolside social at Bimini Cove. Thursday brings a 4Ocean beach cleanup and community buffet dinner. Friday includes a Paddle Out Rose Ceremony to honor cystic fibrosis warriors, followed by rest and safety checks before the midnight launch. All paddlers must wear Coast Guard-approved personal flotation devices, waterproof whistles, and reflective gear during dark hours, along with GPS trackers provided by the event.

Paddlers must raise at least $2,000 for the cause, while crew or virtual participants have a $500 minimum. Final contributions are due July 31. Cystic fibrosis is a life-shortening genetic condition affecting the lungs and digestive system. More information, registration, and donation details are available at crossingforcysticfibrosis.com and pipersangels.org.

Detectives discovered medical beds, fat sculpting machines, laser hair removal equipment, laser liposuction machines and injection devices for fillers and Botox inside the shed.

Mena was charged with three counts of aggravated battery causing bodily harm, three counts of practicing medicine without an active license, one count of fraud to obtain property valued at $300 or more, one count of fraud to obtain property under $20,000, and one count of using a two-way communication device to facilitate a felony. Florida homestead tax exemption vote could reshape local government budgets across Treasure Coast

Florida lawmakers passed a property tax cut proposal Tuesday that would increase the homestead exemption from $50,000 to $250,000 over two years. The measure needs approval from 60% of voters in November, but experts are divided on what the consequences could be for local government services.

21 hours ago

Florida lawmakers on Tuesday passed a property tax cut proposal that would dramatically increase the homestead exemption from $50,000 to $250,000 over two years, with inflation adjustments beginning in 2029. The bill would still need approval from 60% of voters in November before taking effect. The measure would also lower assessment caps on non-homesteaded properties from 10% to 5%.

Experts are divided on how local governments across the Treasure Coast might make up for lost revenue. Real estate attorney Steve Wallace, based in Boynton Beach, raised the possibility of new fees tied to local services. "There could potentially be non-ad valorem taxes assigned to the sheriff's office, fire rescue, some of the other things. Additionally, there may be some increased fees because the only other way that the county or the local governments can derive revenue," Wallace said. CPA Paul Miller suggested businesses could end up carrying more of the financial burden to offset lost revenue.

Municipal attorney Raul Gastesi offered a different perspective, suggesting the measure could actually push home prices higher. "I expect home prices could actually increase because now home buyers have more money at their disposal, and that in the past has resulted in home prices going up," Gastesi said. Danielle Blake, Chief of Residential Advocacy at MIAMI REALTORS, was skeptical of that theory, pointing to interest rates as a more significant barrier to sales than property taxes alone. All four experts acknowledged significant unknowns about how counties and municipalities might respond to decreasing property tax revenue. As with any ballot measure that passes, lawmakers would need to return to Tallahassee to work out the specifics in a bill to implement the changes.
