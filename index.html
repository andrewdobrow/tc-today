Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties will need to stop applying fertilizer containing phosphorus or nitrogen to their lawns and landscaping. The three-county ban is designed to protect local waterways from the effects of nutrient-rich runoff during Florida's rainy season.

According to Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, the ban targets a significant environmental problem. When fertilizer containing nitrogen and phosphorus washes into waterways during heavy rains, it can trigger algae blooms. These blooms pose serious threats to ecosystems like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. Skaggs explained that algae blooms can damage aquatic environments in multiple ways. They shade out beneficial plants that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. In some cases, when algae blooms reach high densities, they release toxins into the water. These toxins can harm human health, wildlife health and the overall balance of the ecosystem.

For residents looking for ways to maintain their gardens and landscaping during the ban, composting offers a practical solution. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective alternative to chemical fertilizers. According to Ricketts, a composting system can function like a small trash can and serves as the easiest and most practical method for adding nutrients back into the landscape. Composting converts organic waste into nutrient-rich material that naturally enriches soil without the risk of creating harmful runoff.

The ban represents an effort by the three counties to manage the seasonal challenges posed by Florida's rainy season. By restricting fertilizer use during this critical period, officials hope to reduce the amount of excess nutrients entering local waterways and prevent the damaging algae blooms that have become increasingly common in the region. Residents should prepare for the June 1 start date by exploring composting options or other fertilizer alternatives that do not contain phosphorus or nitrogen.

Jupiter Island Faces Potential Split as Erosion Threatens to Breach Peck Lake

Yesterday, 4:42 PM ET

The 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report found nearly the entire 11.5-mile coastline of Jupiter Island is critically eroded, threatening roads, buildings, drainage systems, natural habitats and wildlife. The erosion is most severe at Peck Lake, where a thin strip of land could breach and turn into an open inlet, potentially splitting the island in half. A storm breached Peck Lake in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers filled it back in with sand. The report identified threatened sites including the Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old archaeological site already half lost to erosion, along with Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park. Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society said a future breach could upend ecosystems that depend on a delicate balance of fresh and saltwater and noted there are over 33 threatened and endangered species in those areas.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said the town fights erosion through constant maintenance, with about $3 million in town taxpayer dollars going toward beach renourishment every year. In April 2025, the town completed a major beach renourishment project that dumped enough sand to fill nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Field said no oceanfront homes are in immediate danger because of ongoing work. "We are just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over," Field said. He said if the island were to breach, repairs could require massive state and federal investment.

Starting June 15, Florida attorneys and people representing themselves in court must certify that any evidence or legal citations they present are genuine and accurate. The Florida Supreme Court recently established new rules designed to address what legal experts and AI professionals consider a growing problem: the misuse of artificial intelligence in courtrooms. Violations of these rules can result in serious consequences, including public reprimands, fines, and suspension from practicing law.

Federal Judge William Matthewman, who serves as chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has been working to control how AI is used in courts after witnessing widespread misuse in his own courtroom. Matthewman acknowledged the severity of the situation, saying "It's really a problem." He expressed concern that the issue will worsen before improving, noting that he has observed the problem growing consistently over the past couple of years. While he believes the Florida Supreme Court's new order will help within the state, he remains pessimistic about the broader trend.

Paul Figueroa, a criminal defense attorney in Tampa who recently began using AI to improve his work processes, emphasized that the technology is unreliable. He explained that AI can produce seemingly perfect results, such as legal quotes that appear directly relevant to a case, only for the attorney to later discover the information does not actually exist in the cited case. Despite these concerns, Figueroa believes the new rules are sufficient for addressing current known problems.

A recent case in Polk County illustrates the dangers of unverified AI-generated content. An attorney representing the Polk County School District filed a motion to dismiss in a school bus camera lawsuit that contained fabricated facts and citations to cases that do not exist. The attorney apologized to the court and admitted to using ChatGPT as a drafting tool while accepting full responsibility for failing to verify the content before filing. The school district stated it takes legal accuracy seriously and is reviewing whether to continue working with this attorney. Matthewman warned all legal professionals and self-represented parties to verify the accuracy of everything they file in court, cautioning that those who fail to do so risk serious consequences.

More than 100 paddlers will cross 80 miles of the open Atlantic Ocean this June to raise awareness and funds for families living with cystic fibrosis in the annual Crossing for Cystic Fibrosis. Participants will launch from Bimini, Bahamas, at midnight on June 13, arriving at Lake Worth Beach after 12 to 16 hours of paddling. The endurance trek benefits the Pipers Angels Foundation, established by event founder Travis Suit in honor of his daughter, Piper, who was diagnosed with cystic fibrosis at age 4.

The crossing is part of a four-day schedule beginning June 10 with arrivals and a poolside social at Bimini Cove. On Thursday, participants take part in a 4Ocean beach cleanup and a community buffet dinner. Friday brings a Paddle Out Rose Ceremony to honor cystic fibrosis warriors, followed by rest and safety checks before launch night. All paddlers are required to wear a U.S. Coast Guard-approved personal flotation device, waterproof whistle, and reflective gear during dark hours. GPS trackers provided by the event must be worn and active at all times, and mandatory safety meetings will be held.

Paddlers must raise at least $2,000 for the cause, while crew or virtual participants have a $500 minimum. Final contributions are due July 31. Suit, an avid paddler, was inspired to create the event after learning about the natural respiratory benefits of ocean salt air. The foundation has since grown to provide direct support for cystic fibrosis patients and families, connecting them with resources and community.

Spectators are invited to Lake Worth Beach on June 13 to cheer paddlers during a daylong beach celebration marking their arrival. More information, registration, and donation details are available at crossingforcysticfibrosis.com and pipersangels.org.

A storm created a breach at Peck Lake during the early 1960s, though th...

Yesterday, 4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report has identified a critical erosion threat along Jupiter Island's 11.5-mile shoreline. The assessment warns that ocean water could breach through at Peck Lake, potentially splitting the barrier island in half. This scenario has historical precedent. A storm created a breach at Peck Lake during the early 1960s, though the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers later filled it back in with sand. Today, the erosion crisis spans from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake, endangering roads, buildings, drainage systems, and natural landscapes across the island.

The erosion poses significant risks to archaeological and ecological resources. The Joseph Reed Mound, an ancient site dating back 3,000 to 4,000 years, has already lost half its structure to erosion. Wildlife refuges including Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park face similar threats. According to the report, more than 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit these areas. Mark Perry of the Florida Oceanographic Society explained that a future breach could devastate ecosystems dependent on a careful balance between freshwater and saltwater. Perry noted that rising sea levels and increased storm surge frequency would intensify these vulnerabilities, threatening mangrove swamps critical for both habitat and coastal protection.

Jupiter Island commissioners acknowledge the ongoing challenge and current response efforts. Commissioner Marshall Field stated that the town continuously battles erosion through maintenance work. In April 2025, the town completed a significant beach renourishment project, depositing enough sand to equal nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Field emphasized that no oceanfront homes currently face immediate danger, but only because of these persistent efforts. He described the approach candidly, saying "We are just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over." The town dedicates approximately 3 million dollars annually in taxpayer funds toward beach renourishment.

Funding sustainability remains a critical concern for the island's future. Field warned that if a breach occurs, repairs would demand substantial state and federal investment. More broadly, he cautioned that without continued funding for beach renourishment across Florida, the state's coastline "is going to change very quickly, and not in a way that anybody's particularly happy with." Field indicated the recent state assessment positions Jupiter Island favorably for competing for additional state and federal mitigation resources. The report essentially strengthens the island's case for securing the ongoing funding required to prevent the catastrophic erosion scenario officials fear most.

Florida dive operators fight federal shark feeding ban that could devastate tourism businesses

A federal proposal to ban shark feeding off Florida's coast is drawing sharp criticism from the state's shark diving industry, with operators warning it could devastate conservation-driven tourism businesses.

Yesterday, 5:55 PM ET

The Safe Florida Seas Act, designated as H.R. 3831, has cleared the U.S. House and would prohibit shark feeding in federal waters within 200 miles of Florida, with an exception for harvesting. Supporters argue it is a common-sense safety measure to reduce human-shark interactions. However, a coalition of dive operators contends the bill unfairly targets their work, often veteran-owned small businesses and nonprofits focused on education, conservation and responsible shark encounters, while leaving fishing practices untouched. Nathan Campbell, owner of Emerald Charters, stated that no one from Congress has asked them to present data or explain their practices. Operators say they avoid surface chumming and instead take bait underwater during dives, which they argue is less likely to condition sharks to approach boats looking for food.

The bill cites Hawaii's 2006 ban on baited shark diving in federal waters as precedent. However, operators and some researchers point to a different outcome there. Dr. Matt Ajemian, an associate research professor at Florida Atlantic University, says shark diving in Florida has a "near perfect safety record" for customers. Industry members note that since Hawaii's ban, shark incidents have increased and fatalities have been reported. According to the Western Pacific Regional Fishery Management Council, at least one in four fishing trips ends with a shark biting off part of the catch. Campbell has sent a letter to Congress urging senators to delay action, gather research and involve all stakeholders in shaping any new rules.

Florida Attorney General files legislation to strengthen penalties for harming law enforcement animals

Attorney General Ashley Moody filed the LEO K-9 Protection Act, which would increase penalties for anyone who intentionally harms police animals and allow emergency medical services to transport injured K-9s.

Yesterday, 4:03 PM ET

Florida Attorney General Ashley Moody, a Plant City Republican, filed the LEO K-9 Protection Act to strengthen penalties for anyone who intentionally harms law enforcement animals, including canine officers and patrol horses. These highly trained animals help law enforcement apprehend dangerous criminals, detect narcotics and explosives, assist with searches, find missing persons, and save lives. The legislation is named for K-9 Leo, a Marion County canine deputy who was shot and killed in the line of duty in 2024.

The bill increases penalties for using deadly weapons against law enforcement animals and directs the U.S. Department of Transportation to issue regulations permitting emergency medical service providers to transport injured police animals at their discretion. Moody stated that anyone who intentionally harms police animals in the line of duty should face serious consequences, noting that law enforcement animals serving with officers protect communities and deserve protection in return. The legislation mirrors a bill already introduced in the House by Representative Aaron Bean, a Fernandina Beach Republican.

Jupiter Island erosion threatens to split island in half at Peck Lake

A new state report warns that erosion on Jupiter Island has become so severe the ocean could break through at Peck Lake, potentially splitting the barrier island in two.

Yesterday, 4:42 PM ET

A new state report warns that nearly the entire coastline of Jupiter Island is critically eroded, threatening roads, buildings, drainage systems, natural habitats, wildlife and ancient sites. The 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report found Jupiter Island's 11.5-mile shoreline has eroded so severely the ocean could break through at Peck Lake, potentially splitting the island in half. The threat stretches from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake, where a breach could turn a thin strip of land into an open inlet. A storm breached Peck Lake in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers filled it back in with sand.

Among the sites at risk is the Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old archaeological site the report says is already half lost to erosion, along with Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park. Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said the town continues to fight erosion through constant maintenance. In April 2025, the town completed a major beach renourishment project. Field said about $3 million in town taxpayer dollars goes toward beach renourishment every year, and cautioned that if funding ever goes away for beach renourishment in Florida, the coastline of Florida is going to change very quickly.

Palm Beach County's Emergency Rental Assistance Program receives about 8,000 calls for housing assistance per month but can only serve a fraction of applicants due to limited funding.

Yesterday, 6:03 PM ET

Palm Beach County's Community Services Department has nearly $2 million in combined grant funding for its Emergency Rental Assistance Program, designed to help residents who can't pay rent. Green said the recent surge in applications is partly due to rising costs of living including housing, gas and food. For those who cannot get into the county's program, 211 is another important option available 24/7, where callers will be connected with nonprofits and other assistance programs in the area.

Fertilizer ban begins June 1 in Martin, St. Lucie, and Indian River counties

Residents cannot use fertilizer containing phosphorus or nitrogen on lawns or landscaping from June 1 through September 30.

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties have implemented a ban on any fertilizer containing phosphorus or nitrogen starting June 1 through September 30. According to Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, the ban aims to reduce nutrient-rich runoff during the rainy season, which can cause algae blooms that disrupt waterways like the Indian River lagoon and St. Lucie River.

Harmful algae blooms can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife, and at certain densities can release toxins that affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health, Skaggs said. Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an alternative during the ban. Ricketts described composting as similar to using a small trash can and called it "the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape."

Florida dive operators challenge federal shark feeding ban as industry warns of economic devastation

A federal proposal to ban shark feeding in waters off Florida has drawn sharp criticism from the state's shark diving industry, which argues the measure unfairly targets conservation-focused businesses.

Yesterday, 5:55 PM ET

The Safe Florida Seas Act, H.R. 3831, cleared the U.S. House and would prohibit shark feeding in federal waters within 200 miles of Florida, with an exception for harvesting. Supporters call it a common-sense safety measure to reduce human-shark interactions. However, a coalition of dive operators says the bill unfairly targets veteran-owned small businesses and nonprofits focused on education and conservation while leaving fishing practices untouched. Nathan Campbell, owner of Emerald Charters, said no one from Congress has asked operators to present data or explain their practices. Operators say they avoid surface chumming, instead taking bait underwater during dives, which they argue is less likely to condition sharks to approach boats for food compared to some commercial harvesting operations that can release hundreds of gallons of chum at the surface.

The bill cites Hawaii's 2006 ban on baited shark diving in federal waters as precedent, but Florida operators and researchers offer a different assessment. Dr. Matt Ajemian, an associate research professor at Florida Atlantic University, says shark diving in Florida has a near perfect safety record for customers. Industry members note that since Hawaii's ban, shark incidents have increased and fatalities have been reported. According to the Western Pacific Regional Fishery Management Council, at least one in four fishing trips ends with a shark biting off part of the catch. Campbell is urging senators to delay action, gather research, and involve all stakeholders in shaping new rules.

Hillsborough County Commissioner says Tampa CRA may block Rays ballpark funding next week

Commissioner Joshua Wostal indicated that the Tampa Community Redevelopment Agency could remove the Rays memorandum of understanding from its agenda next week, potentially forcing a renegotiation of the stadium deal.

Yesterday, 5:07 PM ET

Hillsborough County Commissioner Joshua Wostal said during an appearance on The Ryan Gorman Show that the Tampa Bay Rays ballpark deal could face resistance from the Tampa Community Redevelopment Agency next week. Wostal indicated the CRA may vote to remove the Rays memorandum of understanding from its upcoming agenda, potentially forcing a renegotiation. The MOU currently promises $80 million from the city of Tampa and another $100 million from the CRA, in addition to other public dollars. Wostal said county attorneys confirmed there is not an executed MOU that has been advertised because the only legal body that can approve or negotiate CRA dollars is the Drew Park CRA, not the Tampa Mayor or Tampa City Council. He said the CRA declined to make the vote at its last board meeting and postponed it to June, and to his understanding, they do not have the votes for it to pass.

Tampa City Council member Bill Carlson recently told Florida Politics that the Rays have primarily negotiated with Tampa Mayor Jane Castor, largely leaving Council members and many of their requests absent from the negotiation table. Carlson noted that Castor is not authorized to negotiate on behalf of the city's CRA. Wostal argued the stadium should be funded through user fees and tourism tax dollars alone, criticizing the current MOU for leveraging local tax dollars, disaster recovery money, infrastructure funds, and potentially money tied to public safety needs. He said the county has not received enough information to justify the subsidy, including detailed site plans, proof of financing, or documentation supporting the team's claimed $2.3 billion project cost. Wostal said he is not opposed to keeping the Rays in Tampa Bay but cannot support a deal without stronger financial protections for taxpayers.
