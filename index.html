The 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report warned erosion threatens roads, buildings, drainage systems, natural habitats, wildlife and ancient sites on Jupiter Island. The threat stretches from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake, where a breach could turn a thin strip of land into an open inlet. A storm breached Peck Lake in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers filled it back in with sand.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said the town continues to fight erosion through constant maintenance. In April 2025, the town completed a major beach renourishment project, dumping enough sand to fill nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Field said about $3 million in town taxpayer dollars goes toward beach renourishment every year. He said no oceanfront homes are in immediate danger but only because of ongoing work.

Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society said the consequences extend well beyond the shoreline. The Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old archaeological site, is already half lost to erosion, according to the report. Wildlife refuges including Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park are also at risk. Perry said over 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit those areas.

St. Lucie, Martin, Indian River counties face millions in potential tax revenue losses under property tax referendum

Three Treasure Coast counties could see significant drops in property tax revenue if voters approve a November referendum to expand the homestead exemption, according to research from the Florida Association of Counties. St. Lucie County estimates it could lose $80 million in 2027-28 and $140 million in 2028-29. Martin County projects losses of $50 million in 2027-28 and $93 m...

Jun 4, 5:40 PM ET

Three Treasure Coast counties could see significant drops in property tax revenue if voters approve a November referendum to expand the homestead exemption, according to research from the Florida Association of Counties.

St. Lucie County estimates it could lose $80 million in 2027-28 and $140 million in 2028-29. Martin County projects losses of $50 million in 2027-28 and $93 million in 2028-29. Indian River County expects to lose $32 million in 2027-28 and $57 million in 2028-29. The referendum would raise the homestead exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, which would lower property tax bills for eligible homeowners. The savings could range from hundreds to thousands of dollars depending on the county, city, and assessed home value.

Local government officials are now assessing how these revenue losses could affect services and budgets. Palm Beach County Commissioner Bobby Powell said officials are working with their administrator and the Office of Management and Budget to understand the impact. Powell acknowledged that if property taxes are cut, local governments will face real shortfalls and challenges, requiring new revenue sources or budget cuts. He suggested potential solutions could include increases in sales tax or user fees for other services, though he emphasized no decisions have been made. "We don't know if it will be an increase in sales tax, we don't know if it will be an increase in user fees for other services, we don't know right now," Powell said. While local officials are restricted by state law from opposing or endorsing the referendum, they can provide educational information to the public.

Republican lawmakers, including Governor Ron DeSantis, argue that local governments collect too much tax revenue and spend excessively. State Representative Toby Overdorf, R-Stuart, characterized the proposal as providing relief to working families struggling to afford homes. "This is more of a break for the struggling family that is in a $300,000 home and working several jobs to make sure ends meet," Overdorf said. He expressed support for allowing voters to decide the issue.

The referendum requires 60 percent approval to pass. A similar homestead exemption proposal failed in 2018. In Palm Beach County, over half of properties currently have the homestead exemption, which is higher than the state average of 47 percent. The November vote will determine whether the expanded exemption becomes law.

Fertilizer ban takes effect in Martin, St. Lucie and Indian River counties

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Starting June 30, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop using fertilizers containing phosphorus or nitrogen on their lawns and landscaping. The ban applies during Florida's rainy season to reduce nutrient-rich runoff that flows into local waterways. According to Jordan Skaggs, with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, this runoff causes algae blooms that can disrupt the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. "It can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife," Skaggs said. At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health.

Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an alternative during the ban period. "It can be like a small trash can," Ricketts said. "That is the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape." This method allows residents to add nutrients to their gardens and landscaping without using commercial fertilizers.

Fertilizer ban begins June 1 in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties cannot use any fertilizer containing phosphorus or nitrogen through Sept. 30.

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties have implemented a ban on any fertilizer containing phosphorus or nitrogen starting June 1. Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County said the ban is designed to reduce nutrient-rich runoff during the rainy season, which can cause algae blooms that disrupt waterways like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River.

According to Skaggs, algae blooms can shade out beneficial plants that fish and wildlife depend on. At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health. Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an alternative during the ban. Ricketts said composting can work like a small trash can and is the easiest and most practical way to add nutrients to landscapes and gardens.

Yesterday, 5:55 PM ET

A federal proposal to ban shark feeding off Florida's coast is drawing sharp criticism from the state's shark diving industry, with operators warning it could devastate conservation-driven tourism businesses. The measure, H.R. 3831, known as the Safe Florida Seas Act, cleared the U.S. House and would prohibit shark feeding in federal waters within 200 miles of Florida, with an exception for harvesting. A coalition of dive operators says the bill unfairly targets their work, often veteran-owned small businesses and nonprofits focused on education and conservation, while leaving fishing practices untouched.

Nathan Campbell, owner of Emerald Charters, said operators are requesting the chance to speak and share the science and facts. "No one from Congress has yet asked us to present data or explain our practices," Campbell said. Operators say they avoid surface chumming, instead taking bait underwater during dives, arguing this is less likely to condition sharks to approach boats looking for food, unlike some commercial harvesting operations that can release hundreds of gallons of chum at the surface. Dr. Matt Ajemian, an associate research professor at Florida Atlantic University, says shark diving in Florida has a "near perfect safety record" for customers. In a letter to Congress, Campbell urged senators to delay action, gather research and involve all stakeholders in shaping any new rules.
