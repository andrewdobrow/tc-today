Port St. Lucie residents to receive credits after $24 million settlement with former trash hauler

Port St. Lucie residents will receive money back after the city secured a $24 million settlement with Waste Pro, their former trash hauler. The city and company filed lawsuits against each other in 2021 during the pandemic. The city claimed Waste Pro breached its service standards, while the company blamed a labor shortage caused by the pandemic. The city voted to return all s...

6:03 PM ET

Port St. Lucie residents will receive money back after the city secured a $24 million settlement with Waste Pro, their former trash hauler. The city and company filed lawsuits against each other in 2021 during the pandemic. The city claimed Waste Pro breached its service standards, while the company blamed a labor shortage caused by the pandemic.

The city voted to return all settlement funds to residents through credits on their property tax bills rather than issuing physical checks. Mayor Shannon Martin said he maintained from the beginning that the funds should benefit residents who experienced problems in 2021 and 2022. Councilman Anthony Bonna said the city reached the sweet spot with the distribution plan. More than 5,700 households with the same owner since 2022 will receive $364, as the city determined they experienced the worst of the service issues, including service interruptions. Councilman David Pickett noted these residents suffered during the litigation process and deserve to be rewarded for that hardship.

Thousands of other homes will receive a $64 credit. This credit accounts for rate increases that occurred during Waste Pro's breach of contract, covering costs for a new waste site, litigation expenses, and the transition to a new provider. Additionally, 24,676 properties that had a title change since 2022 will receive a $64 credit. The city said it cannot determine whether title changes resulted from trusts, marriages, divorces, or relocations, and former owners cannot be reached. New properties numbering 13,241 will also receive the $64 credit because these households also paid the rate increases during the service breach.

Residents who sold a home and purchased another within the city during the same period can submit documentation proving continuous ownership to receive the remaining $300 credit on the following year's tax bill. The city will establish the claims process after staff and the legal department review it. The claims window will last 180 days. A resident named Kelly Lee expressed her satisfaction with the settlement outcome, saying she was pleasantly surprised and that the city presented well thought out plans for distributing the funds fairly among different property owner categories.

4:32 PM ET

For three years, Happy Beach Dancing has brought together dozens and sometimes hundreds of people each week at Jupiter's beach access 55. The gatherings have attracted participants of all ages, including families, children, and dogs who come together for a recreational event. However, the popularity of the weekly meetup has raised concerns among some residents about its potential impact on the area's sea turtle population. Rather than calling for the event to be canceled entirely, concerned residents are asking the organizers to move the dance parties to a different location during sea turtle nesting season.

Jupiter's beaches rank among the densest loggerhead sea turtle nesting sites anywhere in the world, with approximately 150 nests documented in this relatively small area. This year has seen record numbers of loggerhead and leatherback sea turtle nests across Florida beaches overall. According to Joanna Walczak, Vice President of Conservation at the nearby Loggerhead Marinelife Center, the presence of large crowds on the beach can discourage mother turtles from nesting, regardless of what the people are doing. "Just the fact that a large crowd gathered on the beach, no matter what they're doing, can deter the mama turtles from doing their nesting," Walczak said. The Loggerhead Marinelife Center scheduled a meeting with dance party organizers to discuss potential solutions.

Local perspectives on the issue reflect both support for the community event and commitment to environmental protection. Isabel Nylen, a longtime Jupiter resident who has attended several dance parties, said she sees value in both sides of the debate. "I think people come and have a great time. Every age, people come with families, little kids, with dogs. It's open to everybody," Nylen said. At the same time, she recognizes the importance of protecting sea turtles and noted the community's dedication to turtle-friendly practices, such as the red lighting used on nearby buildings to avoid disrupting nesting. Her daughter, Camila Nylen, emphasized that Jupiter's beaches are special and require careful stewardship. "You got to be super wary and super careful about where you put your stuff and where you walk around," she said.

The Town of Jupiter is bringing together all interested stakeholders to work through the controversy. The founder of Happy Beach Dancing declined to comment on the situation. While the Loggerhead Marinelife Center is not a regulatory agency, Walczak emphasized that the center remains focused on doing what is best for the turtles.

Tampa General Hospital to launch Florida's first newborn genome sequencing program

4:35 PM ET

Florida will become a national leader in newborn care this summer when Tampa General Hospital launches the Sunshine Genetics Newborn Screening Program, marking the first statewide effort to offer whole genome sequencing for newborns. The program aims to detect serious genetic diseases before symptoms appear. Established through HB 907, the Sunshine Genetics Act filed in 2025, the legislation was championed by Representative Larry Anderson, whose personal family tragedy drove his determination to create the program. Anderson's son Andrew, known as Drew, was born healthy in 2015 but was diagnosed with Tay-Sachs disease after enduring more than a year of misdiagnoses. Drew died at just 4 years old, prompting Anderson to work toward preventing other families from experiencing the same devastating journey.

The new program represents a significant expansion of Florida's newborn screening capabilities, broadening coverage from 60 conditions to nearly 900 rare genetic diseases. By identifying these conditions early, before symptoms develop, the program could fundamentally change how Florida approaches pediatric care. Anderson announced the legislation before an audience of more than 150 lawmakers, medical students, and patient advocates during Tampa General Hospital's annual advocacy day at the Capitol. "My hope is that families will get the answers that they need, and that babies born today won't have to live that same nightmare of that diagnostic odyssey that I did and that so many families around the country do every day," Anderson said.

The program, developed in partnership with genomic medicine company GeneDx, operates on a voluntary and free basis for participating families. Tampa General will serve as the flagship launch site this summer, with subsequent expansions planned for Orlando and Miami later in the year, leading to a statewide rollout over the next five years. Dr. Patricia Emmanuel, chief of pediatrics at Muma Children's Hospital at TGH, emphasized the program's potential. "It will greatly expand our ability to diagnose these rare conditions," she said. "My hope is that we are able to have a partnership with families to really understand some of the complexities of genetic diseases and to really advance cures. That is our ultimate goal in pediatrics. We want happy, healthy, growing kids."

Supporters of the Sunshine Genetics Newborn Screening Program project significant benefits for Florida's families and healthcare system. Early diagnosis could save the state hundreds of millions of dollars by reducing the lengthy and costly diagnostic process that many rare disease families currently endure. The program also opens pathways to gene therapies and precision treatments that can begin before symptoms appear. The legislature's decision to anchor the program's statewide launch at Tampa General Hospital reflects lawmakers' confidence in the institution's ability to advance innovative care approaches throughout Florida.

Florida property tax amendment officially certified for November ballot

A proposed constitutional amendment aimed at limiting property tax increases is officially headed to Florida voters in November.

6:30 PM ET

A proposed constitutional amendment aimed at limiting property tax increases is officially headed to Florida voters in November. House Joint Resolution 1-F, titled "Save Our Homes from Excessive Property Taxes," was signed Tuesday by House and Senate officers and filed with Secretary of State Cord Byrd, completing the final procedural step needed to place the measure on the ballot. Because the proposal is a joint resolution rather than a bill, it does not require the Governor's signature. Under Florida law, constitutional amendments can reach the ballot through several avenues, including legislative referral with a three-fifths vote of both chambers.

The measure would require future votes of the electorate before local governments could increase property tax collections beyond certain thresholds. The proposal emerged from this month's Special Session on property tax relief, where lawmakers and the Governor spent weeks negotiating competing visions for what voters should consider. Lawmakers excluded school district property taxes from the amendment, a significant departure from Governor Ron DeSantis's broader push for more sweeping property tax relief. This carveout prompted criticism from the Governor, who signaled disappointment with the final product even as legislative leaders advanced the measure toward the ballot. Constitutional amendments in Florida must receive at least 60 percent voter approval to take effect.

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could lower tax bills for homeowners, but critics worry renters may face higher costs if local governments raise rates to offset lost revenue. A Florida Policy Institute analysis estimates Martin County alone could lose more than $67 million if the amendment passes in November, with statewide losses reaching nearly $4.8 billion. At Habitat for Humanity of Martin County, Executive Director Mike Readling reports an uptick in people seeking help with rent and bills, 95% of whom are renters. "The housing situation is so dire. It's gotten progressively worse," Readling said, adding that the proposed tax cut would do little for the organization's clients.

Attorney John Tolley says local governments facing revenue shortfalls could cut services, dip into reserves, or raise millage rates. If rates rise, homesteaded homeowners would still benefit from the exemption, according to realtor Michael Caputo. However, landlords without the exemption could face higher bills and pass costs to renters. A 2025 Florida Senate analysis found only 24 affordable rental units available for every 100 extremely low-income renters. The amendment would also lower the property tax cap on non-homesteaded properties, including commercial apartment complexes, from 10% to 5%. Tolley suggested this could lead to lower rents, while Caputo said the market may stabilize through supply and demand.

Sargassum seaweed helps and hurts sea turtles along Florida beaches

Sargassum seaweed provides essential habitat for young sea turtles offshore, but can become an obstacle for hatchlings when it washes ashore in large amounts.

5:16 PM ET

Sargassum plays a dual role for sea turtles along Florida beaches, serving as critical habitat offshore while becoming an obstacle when it washes ashore in excessive amounts. Emily Turla, laboratory coordinator at Florida Atlantic University's Marine Science Laboratory and a Ph.D. student, explained that sargassum is important habitat for young sea turtles when floating in ocean currents. After hatching, young sea turtles spend their first days swimming away from shore, and floating mats of sargassum provide shelter from predators, food sources, and habitat during one of the most vulnerable stages of their lives. Research professor Dr. Brian LaPointe with FAU's Harbor Branch Oceanographic Institute noted that when sargassum is in the open ocean, it is deemed essential fish habitat and critical habitat for hatchlings leaving the beach.

The problem emerges when excessive amounts of sargassum accumulate along shorelines. Scientists say large piles of seaweed become obstacles for hatchlings attempting to reach the ocean. Turla observed that sometimes sea turtles can be seen stuck in the seaweed on beaches. LaPointe explained that the extra time hatchlings spend navigating around sargassum increases their time getting into the water, making them more vulnerable to predators. Researchers say finding ways to manage excessive sargassum while preserving its ecological benefits will continue to be an important challenge for coastal communities.
