4:49 PM ET

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could benefit homeowners while potentially raising costs for renters, according to recent analysis. The Florida Policy Institute estimates Martin County could lose more than $67 million in tax revenue if voters approve the amendment in November. Statewide, local governments could lose nearly $4.8 billion under the proposal.

The expansion raises concerns for vulnerable renters already struggling with housing costs. At Habitat for Humanity of Martin County, Executive Director Mike Readling reports that 95 percent of people seeking assistance with rent and bills are renters. "The housing situation is so dire," Readling said. "It's gotten progressively worse. We have families coming in every day." He believes the proposed tax cut would not help the organization's clients. "The demographic that we're working with and Habitat for Humanity tries to help, it's not going to help them at all," Readling said. A 2025 Florida Senate analysis found there are just 24 affordable and available rental units for every 100 extremely low-income renters, highlighting the acute shortage of affordable housing.

When local governments face revenue shortfalls, they typically have limited options. Attorney John Tolley explained that municipalities could cut services, reduce reserves, or increase millage rates. If rates rise, homesteaded homeowners would still benefit from the tax exemption, but landlords without that exemption could face higher tax bills. Realtor Michael Caputo acknowledged the potential impact: "There could be an effect where we see some people getting priced out of these rentals, which are already pretty high." However, Caputo suggested that landlords facing increased costs might not immediately raise rents, and the market could stabilize through supply and demand forces. "The landlords are just going to have a flow through effect," Caputo said. "If it doesn't happen for them, then they'll have to readjust their pricing."

The amendment would also lower the property tax cap on non-homesteaded properties, including commercial apartment complexes, from 10 percent to 5 percent. Some observers see potential benefits in this provision. Tolley noted that lower property taxes could potentially lead to reduced rents. "The fact that you could have less taxes, you actually might see a trend where rents go down even further than they were before," Tolley said. Caputo added that he believes there is a high probability local municipalities may not raise millage rates at all. Despite differing perspectives, the core concern remains whether renters would ultimately bear the cost of lost tax revenue through higher housing expenses.

4:37 PM ET

A Palm Beach Gardens resident discovered she was receiving toll bills for roads she never traveled after her husband's temporary license tag was stolen and used fraudulently. Vera Keswick-Paine first noticed the problem in March when a toll bill arrived for multiple charges along Florida's Gulf Coast. The bill displayed a license plate number matching a temporary tag registered to her husband, but it referenced a black Mercedes. The family drives a white Nissan that has carried a permanent tag for months.

Keswick-Paine reported the issue to Palm Beach Gardens Police and filed a dispute with SunPass, the state's toll collection system. However, the situation escalated beyond a simple billing error. "The guy runs a red light, the tag is in my husband's name. The guy hits and runs, tag is under my husband's name," she explained, highlighting the potential legal consequences of having someone else's crimes linked to her family's identity. Despite her efforts, a second invoice arrived, and SunPass closed the dispute without providing clear confirmation that the matter was resolved. A third toll bill came after she reached out to authorities in April.

The problem is more widespread than many realize, according to personal injury attorney Doug Burnetti. "It's happening a lot," he said. Temporary tags are attractive to criminals because they are difficult to track and allow people to drive without insurance or conduct other illegal activities without detection. The Florida Department of Law Enforcement does not maintain readily available statewide data on stolen temporary tags, which makes investigating these crimes challenging. Security consultant and retired police officer Gene Petrino described the difficulty: "It's one of those things where it's just a nightmare to follow the breadcrumbs of the crime."

Petrino recommended that people protect themselves by maintaining detailed records of police reports and toll disputes. He also advised drivers to destroy their temporary tags when transitioning to permanent plates by cutting them into pieces or removing personal information before surrendering them. Palm Beach Gardens Police characterized Keswick-Paine's case as isolated, though experts disagree about how common the problem actually is. Florida enacted a new law last year that increased penalties for fraudulent use of temporary tags and improved the state's ability to track them. Keswick-Paine's attempts to contact the Florida Department of Transportation went unanswered despite multiple follow-up inquiries over nearly a month.

The Palm Beach County Library System is running its 2026 Summer Reading Challenge, themed "Unearth a Story," through July 31. According to Community Engagement Manager Lindsey McMullen, the program provides free activities, incentives and grand prizes designed to help residents reconnect with reading. Anyone can participate by registering at any library branch, completing an activity log and returning it to claim a prize while supplies last. Those who submit their logs also enter an automatic drawing for one of several grand prizes.

The program offers age-appropriate rewards for different groups. Children up to age 4 receive a rubber duck, while kids ages 5 to 11 can choose between a fidget spinner, dinosaur-themed pop keychain or dino pencil. Teenagers ages 12 to 17 and adults have the option of selecting a fidget spinner, chip clip or notebook. All prizes are distributed on a while supplies last basis.

Beyond the core reading challenge, each library branch is hosting special events throughout the summer. These include children's story times and craft sessions alongside adult workshops and wellness programs. Families will also have opportunities to meet Dewey the Library Owl and Penelope Rex, a dinosaur character created for the challenge, at various branch locations and special appearances.

Summer reading initiatives like this one serve an important educational purpose. According to the American Library Association, these programs help prevent the "summer slide," which refers to the decline in student learning that can occur during extended school breaks. Activity logs can be picked up and submitted at any library location. The challenge welcomes all county residents and library cardholders to participate.

The amendment, which state lawmakers approved for the November ballot, would raise the homestead exemption for non-school p...

3:55 PM ET

Tampa Mayor Jane Castor is urging voters to carefully consider a proposed constitutional amendment that would increase the homestead property tax exemption, saying it could force the city to make difficult budget decisions affecting public safety services. The amendment, which state lawmakers approved for the November ballot, would raise the homestead exemption for non-school property taxes to $150,000 in 2027 and then to $250,000 in 2028. The measure requires approval from 60% of voters to become law.

Castor emphasized that property taxes currently generate $380 million for Tampa's 2026 budget, yet this revenue barely covers the city's police and fire departments as they operate today. She projected that the amendment would reduce city property tax revenue by at least $35 million in 2028 and at least $60 million by 2029. The mayor connected the tax issue directly to public safety, noting that Tampa has hired more than 100 firefighters and 60 police officers since 2019 while crime has dropped by more than 20% during that period. "Public safety isn't optional," Castor said, suggesting these staffing increases have contributed to improved safety outcomes.

Castor raised concerns about how the city might respond to significant revenue losses, asking whether it would need to cut recreation programs or charge businesses and tax-exempt property owners public safety fees to maintain services. "Your City Council members and I already make hard budget choices every year, but this proposal could turn those into brutal choices," she said. "Do we slash recreation programs? Do we need to start charging businesses and tax-exempt property owners public safety fees to avoid cutting services? How much will the burden of funding basic government services shift toward renters and businesses?"

Tampa joins other Florida cities expressing concern about the state's property tax proposal. Supporters of the amendment argue it would provide relief to homeowners struggling with rising housing costs, insurance premiums, and tax bills. However, local government officials have warned that reducing property tax collections could either shift the financial burden to other revenue sources or require cuts to essential services. Castor called on voters to thoroughly research the proposal before voting, stating that the amendment "stands to have a tangible impact on the quality of life that has made Tampa one of America's most exciting and appealing cities."

Port St. Lucie city council voted 4-1 to distribute the $24 million settlement from former trash hauler Waste Pro by crediting the funds directly to properties via the tax roll. This method uses an established system to channel the money to residents rather than issuing individual checks. Council members also decided against reimbursing the city for emergency solid waste expenses that had been covered by the general fund during the service failures.

The settlement stems from years of trash collection problems that plagued the city during the pandemic. Waste Pro failed to pick up overflowing bins regularly, leaving trash piled up on residents' yards. Both the city and Waste Pro filed lawsuits against each other in 2021. Waste Pro blamed labor shortages for the service failures, while the city argued the company breached its contract by failing to maintain adequate service standards. The dispute ultimately resulted in the $24 million settlement agreement.

Kelly Lee, a Port St. Lucie resident, attended Monday's council meeting to weigh in on how the money should be distributed. She recalled the frustration neighbors experienced when their trash accumulated as "a stinky mess" on their yards due to missed pickups. However, Lee raised concerns about fairness in the distribution process. She questioned how the city would handle compensation for people who experienced the problems but have since moved away, as well as for newer residents who were not impacted by Waste Pro's failures. "People have moved, the residents are no longer here, some of them are brand new residents that didn't pay into waste pro," Lee said, asking where those who left would receive their fair share.

Scott Samples with the city acknowledged the complexity of the situation, noting that staff have been working to collect data and develop different distribution scenarios for council recommendations. He emphasized that the city's goal is to make the best possible decision that helps address the hardship residents endured during that period. Mayor Shannon Martin previously stated her commitment to ensuring residents receive compensation for their troubles. Lee urged city leaders to involve residents in the decision-making process. "Have us weigh in on something to at least feel part of the process. One size can't fit all," she said.

Florida Medicaid enrollment dips below pre-pandemic levels as CHIP numbers drop 31%

Florida's combined Medicaid and Children's Health Insurance Program enrollment has fallen slightly below pre-pandemic levels, with child enrollment in CHIP declining sharply.

5:23 PM ET

Florida's combined Medicaid and Children's Health Insurance Program enrollment has dipped slightly below pre-pandemic levels, according to February 2026 data compiled by KFF. The state had 3.58 million Floridians enrolled in Medicaid or CHIP as of February, down from 3.60 million in February 2020, before the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted enrollment patterns nationwide. Florida is among a minority of states where combined enrollment has fallen below the February 2020 baseline. Nationally, Medicaid and CHIP enrollment remains 5% above pre-pandemic levels.

Child enrollment in Medicaid and CHIP fell from 2.41 million in February 2020 to 2.30 million in February 2026, a decline of roughly 107,000 children or 4%. Adult enrollment increased to 1.28 million from 1.19 million before the pandemic, an increase of about 87,000 adults or 7%. The most significant decline occurred within CHIP, where enrollment dropped from 240,730 children to 165,278 in February, a 31% decrease. Medicaid enrollment alone remains slightly above pre-pandemic levels at 3.41 million in February 2026, up from 3.36 million. The CHIP decline comes amid Florida's ongoing dispute over a federal rule requiring 12 months of continuous eligibility for children enrolled in the program, even if their parents miss a premium payment. This dispute has delayed implementation of a 2023 law expected to expand eligibility to about 40,000 children.

A new national report examining vaccine access laws finds Florida has expanded the role pharmacies play in administering adult vaccines but still falls short of some advocate recommendations.

A new report examining vaccine access laws nationwide finds Florida has expanded the role pharmacies play in administering adult vaccines, but still falls short of policies advocates say would further improve access. The study notes nearly 90% of states now allow pharmacy technicians to administer vaccines, up from fewer than 30% in 2021, with the number of states authorizing pharmacy technicians to vaccinate growing from 14 in 2021 to 46 in 2024.

Researchers found that community pharmacies have become an increasingly important point of access for vaccinations, noting that 93% of Americans live within five miles of a pharmacy. The report identifies three policy approaches it says would improve adult vaccine access: broadly authorizing FDA-approved or CDC-recommended adult vaccines, reducing prescription and physician protocol requirements, and allowing trained pharmacy interns and technicians to administer the same adult vaccines as pharmacists under appropriate supervision. Kayla McFeely, vice president of Health and Wellness Strategy and Policy for the National Association of Chain Drug Stores, noted that pharmacies help address access barriers by offering walk-in appointments, extended hours and convenient locations.

Critics warn homestead exemption expansion could shift tax burden to renters

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could lower tax bills for homeowners, but critics fear renters may end up paying the price if local governments raise rates to offset lost revenue.

4:49 PM ET

A proposed expansion of Florida's homestead exemption could lower tax bills for homeowners, but critics worry renters may face higher costs if local governments raise tax rates to offset lost revenue. A Florida Policy Institute analysis estimates Martin County alone could lose more than $67 million if the amendment passes in November, while statewide local governments could lose nearly $4.8 billion. Attorney John Tolley said governments facing revenue shortfalls could cut services, dip into reserves, or raise millage rates. If rates rise, homesteaded homeowners would still benefit from the tax break, according to realtor Michael Caputo. However, landlords without the exemption could face higher bills and pass those costs to renters, who receive no tax benefit.

Mike Readling, Executive Director of Habitat for Humanity of Martin County, says he's seeing an uptick in people seeking help with rent and bills, with 95 percent being renters. Readling said the proposed tax cut would do little for the people his organization serves. A 2025 Florida Senate analysis found there are just 24 affordable and available rental units for every 100 extremely low-income renters. The amendment would also lower the property tax cap on non-homesteaded properties, including commercial apartment complexes, from 10 percent down to 5 percent.

Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office deputy Tina Izzarone got an unexpected surprise at an awards ceremony this month when rapper Vanilla Ice appeared to honor her 30 years of service.

11:59 AM ET

Deputy Tina Izzarone was honored at the Palm Beach County Sheriff's Office awards ceremony this month, celebrating 30 years of service. She received an unexpected surprise when rapper Rob Van Winkle, known as Vanilla Ice, appeared at the event. "That was amazing," Izzarone said of the moment.

The surprise was arranged by a young man named Raffie, whose connection to both the deputy and the rapper made the moment possible. The story began when Izzarone pulled over Raffie's mother during a traffic stop and noticed Raffie. "I noticed immediately of his specialness, and I made him feel really at ease knowing that his mother was not in trouble," Izzarone said. Raffie remembered the deputy's kindness and approached her again when they saw each other a few months later. Raffie has a longstanding friendship with Van Winkle, who said "Raffie and I go way back when he was a little kid and just a great honor to come out and do this." Van Winkle described his bond with Raffie, noting that when Raffie visits his house, he sits in Van Winkle's big monster truck. Adding to the moment, Van Winkle and Izzarone share the same birthday, October 31. Raffie's mother received a ticket during the original traffic stop.
