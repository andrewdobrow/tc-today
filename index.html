Yesterday, 9:22 PM ET

A 6.1 magnitude earthquake struck off the west-northwest coast of Cuba on Tuesday, sending tremors across Florida that were felt as far north as Jacksonville. The seismic activity triggered immediate building evacuations throughout the Miami area as residents and workers experienced the unexpected shaking. The quake's effects were significant enough to interrupt daily activities at multiple locations across the city, though the National Weather Service confirmed there was no tsunami risk from the earthquake and no injuries were reported.

The evacuation procedures at several Miami buildings caught many people off guard. Students at Fusion Academy were evacuated along with others in nearby structures. Maria Torrado, a student at the academy, described how those on higher floors experienced the earthquake more intensely than those lower down. She noted that the evacuation alarm sounded different from typical fire drills, with officials explicitly stating this was not a drill, which made the situation feel particularly alarming to students present.

Interestingly, not all employees and students in evacuated buildings actually felt the earthquake firsthand. At the Swatch Watchmaking School, staff and students were evacuated without initially understanding why. Jack Hodapp explained that while people in the break room sensed some type of earthquake and began evacuating, he and others nearby felt nothing at all. This confusion about whether the evacuation was necessary created some skepticism among those unaffected by the tremors. Streets surrounding the affected buildings were temporarily blocked off as the Miami-Dade Sheriff's Office established a command center to manage the situation and coordinate response efforts. Within a short time, those who had been evacuated were allowed to return inside their buildings.

For some people caught in the evacuation, the confusion extended beyond not feeling the earthquake. Juan Cardona initially misinterpreted the crowded streets and blocked-off areas as signs of a criminal incident rather than a natural disaster. It was only after speaking with other people at the scene that he learned about the earthquake in Cuba and understood how it had impacted the Florida area. His experience reflects how the sudden nature of the evacuation and the general confusion left some unclear about what had actually occurred until they gathered more information from those around them.

St. Lucie County implements 45-day anchoring limit in Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor

St. Lucie County launched new anchoring restrictions on June 1 to address growing concerns about abandoned vessels in local waterways. The county established two anchor limitation areas in the Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor near the South Causeway Bridge. Under these new rules, boaters cannot anchor their vessels for more than 45 consecutive days within any six-mont...

Yesterday, 5:58 PM ET

St. Lucie County launched new anchoring restrictions on June 1 to address growing concerns about abandoned vessels in local waterways. The county established two anchor limitation areas in the Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor near the South Causeway Bridge. Under these new rules, boaters cannot anchor their vessels for more than 45 consecutive days within any six-month period in the designated areas. If boaters wish to anchor again after reaching this limit, they must leave all anchoring limitation areas for at least 24 hours before returning.

The county implemented these restrictions in response to an increasing number of derelict vessels appearing in the waterways. According to Erick Gill with St. Lucie County, the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission collected approximately two dozen abandoned vessels in the area last year. These discarded boats create multiple problems for the region's water quality and navigation. James Beams with St. Lucie County Port, Inlet and Beaches explained that long-abandoned vessels pose environmental hazards, particularly from diesel and oil tanks and holding tanks that can contaminate the water.

Gill emphasized that the new anchoring restrictions serve multiple purposes for the community. The limitations will help keep the waterways clear and navigable, ensuring that vessels can move freely through these important passages. By reducing the number of abandoned vessels remaining in the water, the county aims to prevent pollutants from entering the lagoon ecosystem. Gill noted the ecological significance of protecting these waters, stating that "there's over 4,000 species of animals and plants and birds that live in the lagoon."

The restrictions represent St. Lucie County's effort to balance recreational boating access with environmental protection and waterway maintenance. By limiting how long vessels can remain anchored without interruption, the county hopes to discourage the abandonment of boats while still allowing legitimate anchoring for temporary purposes. Boaters who plan to stay in the anchoring limitation areas for extended periods will need to plan breaks from the waterways or relocate their vessels. St. Lucie County residents and visitors can find a detailed site map of the anchor limitation areas to help them understand where these new rules apply.

A West Palm Beach resident is traveling to Miami this month to witness Cape Verde's first-ever World Cup appearance. Eridjon Dacruz grew up in the island nation, an archipelago of 10 islands located off the African coast with a population of roughly half a million people, before relocating to the United States. The team, nicknamed the Tubarão Azul or Blue Sharks, has qualified for the World Cup tournament for the first time in the country's history. As Dacruz looked through his closet at his collection of national team jerseys, he reflected on what this achievement means for his home country, which gained independence from Portugal in the 1970s.

The qualification has sparked enormous celebration throughout Cape Verde. Dacruz, who still has many family members living on the islands, says the World Cup achievement has dominated conversations there. He recalls how difficult it once was to find Cape Verdean players represented in soccer video games, with only a handful appearing in the games he played growing up. His girlfriend surprised him with an unforgettable anniversary gift: a handwritten note inside a box that read "I'm going to the World Cup." Dacruz will attend Cape Verde's matchup against Uruguay in Miami, making the gift even more meaningful. He is currently waiting to receive the newest Cape Verde national jersey to wear to the game.

Cape Verde will open the tournament against Spain, one of the competition's top contenders. Despite facing strong opposition, Dacruz remains optimistic about his team's prospects. He believes that winning a couple of games could allow Cape Verde to advance further in the tournament than many might expect. The World Cup qualification carries significant financial benefits for the nation. Making the tournament will provide Cape Verde's soccer federation with more than 10 million dollars to invest in developing future generations of players.

Cape Verde's location near Africa means the islands frequently appear in weather discussions during hurricane season. Tropical systems often develop in the region before moving westward across the Atlantic toward the United States. Meteorologist Sami Squires explained that weather patterns originating near Cape Verde are tracked throughout hurricane season as they travel across the Atlantic, with forecasters monitoring their potential path toward the U.S. coast. For Dacruz, however, the islands represent something far more personal and meaningful than weather systems: they are his childhood home and the birthplace of a national team achieving what once seemed impossible.

The governor highlighted Simpson's response to natural disasters, including this year's wildfires, as evidence of his effectiveness in the role. President Donald Trump also backs Simpson's candidacy for a second term.

Simpson, an egg farmer from Trilby who previously served as state Senate President, was first elected agriculture commissioner in 2022. He has pursued legislative reform measures during his tenure, most notably the Florida Farm Bill passed during this legislative session. Simpson promoted the bill as protecting Floridians from dangerous and disruptive technologies, organized fraud, and government overreach. The legislation accomplished multiple goals, including preventing local governments from banning gasoline-powered equipment, establishing guardrails on development, expanding agritourism opportunities, creating the Florida Native Seed Research and Marketing Program, and making permanent the Farmers Feeding Florida Program.

Simpson maintains a substantial financial advantage over his opponents. Simpson also chairs multiple other committees with significant resources, including the Florida Green PAC with nearly 11 million dollars on hand, the Future Florida political committee with nearly 12 million dollars, and the Home Grown PAC with more than 300,000 dollars. The other Republican candidates have far less financial backing. Matt the Welder of Plant City reported less than 40,000 dollars on hand, while Richard Olle of Valrico had not reported any fundraising as of the March reporting deadline.

Democrats Donald Prichard and Chase Anderson Romagnano have also opened campaign accounts, along with no-party candidate Kyle Gibson. Neither Democrat had raised funds as of the first quarter filing deadline, with Gibson reporting approximately 1,000 dollars on hand. DeSantis joins a growing list of endorsers backing Simpson's reelection. As of Wednesday morning, none of the candidates had officially qualified for the race, though filing deadlines were approaching.

A mobile prenatal clinic program is now serving expecting mothers in rural Martin County who face barriers to accessing maternity care. Mom Mobile, launched by the Martin County Healthy Start Coalition, delivers prenatal care and support directly to pregnant women and their families in underserved communities. The program has proven so successful that it has expanded to neighboring Okeechobee County, where the need for such services is equally critical.

Twenty-year-old Evelyn Allen discovered Mom Mobile after struggling to find affordable prenatal care options. Allen explained that available resources either required out-of-pocket payments or had expectations she could not meet. When she first learned she was pregnant while trying to improve her circumstances, she felt terrified and unsure where to turn. The discovery of Mom Mobile changed her experience significantly. According to Allen, "I couldn't find any resources and the resources I did find you have to come out of pocket for it, or they would expect you to do more and you can't." She added that it was difficult to find places willing to provide help without barriers.

The expansion to Okeechobee County came after Andrea Medellin with the Okeechobee Healthy Start Coalition witnessed the program's impact and recognized her community had the same urgent need. Okeechobee County has no hospitals that deliver babies, forcing pregnant women to travel to Fort Pierce or Highlands County depending on their location. The 38-foot mobile clinic is now stationed at the public library on West 16th Street every Tuesday, offering hour-long appointments designed specifically to provide quality care for high-risk and high-need mothers. Medellin explained the intentional approach: "This bus looks like an hour-long visit for moms. It's intentional, it's specifically set not to serve more but to serve quality for our high-risk and need moms."

The demand for the service became clear immediately upon arrival in Okeechobee. During the first three days of operation, fifteen women received care, and appointment requests have continued to come in. Allen appreciates the ongoing support from nursing staff with the Okeechobee Healthy Start Coalition, who assured her she could always call with concerns. Having professional support throughout her pregnancy journey has made a significant difference for Allen. She describes the Mom Mobile program as a blessing, reflecting on how the accessible, judgment-free care has transformed her experience during a time when she felt vulnerable and uncertain.

St. Lucie County established new anchoring restrictions for boaters in the Indian River Lagoon and Fort Pierce Harbor near the South Causeway Bridge, effective June 1. Under the new rules, boaters cannot anchor a vessel for more than 45 consecutive days within any six-month period in designated anchoring limitation areas. Anyone anchoring in these zones must leave all anchoring limitation areas for at least 24 hours before returning.

County officials say the restrictions directly address the problem of abandoned vessels accumulating in these waterways. According to Erick Gill with St. Lucie County, the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission collected approximately two dozen derelict vessels from the area during the previous year. Gill explained that the new anchoring limits will improve navigation by keeping the waterways clear and accessible for other boats. "We need to make sure that highway for vessels is clear and free for other vessels to come and go," Gill said.

James Beams with St. Lucie County Port, Inlet and Beaches previously warned that abandoned vessels sitting in the water for extended periods could harm water quality. These deteriorating vessels pose significant environmental risks because they contain diesel tanks, oil tanks, and holding tanks that can leak or rupture. Gill stated that the new anchoring limits will help prevent these pollutants from contaminating the water. The Indian River Lagoon supports an extraordinarily diverse ecosystem, with over 4,000 species of animals, plants, and birds living there. Gill emphasized the county's commitment to protecting this sensitive environment from contamination.

St. Lucie County residents and boaters who want more information about the anchor limitation areas can access a site map through the county. The restrictions represent an effort to balance the needs of the boating community with environmental protection and water quality concerns in one of Florida's most ecologically important waterways.
