He pointed out that anchor stores including Macy's, Dillard's and JCPenney remain high-performing locations. 10:19 PM ET

Homeowners in an unincorporated Palm Beach County neighborhood are facing unexpected bills for years of unpaid sewer fees from the city of Delray Beach, with some residents receiving notices totaling more than $4,000. Several homeowners along Franwood Drive received demands for back payments covering up to three years of charges, plus new monthly sewer fees going forward. Candace Etzler received a notice for $3,139, and at least a dozen other homeowners in the neighborhood received similar bills. Ruth Stephens said her total bill came to $4,494.92. The situation began in 1998 when nearby development disrupted the residents' septic systems, and state law required them to connect to the closest municipal sewer system. The city of Delray Beach completed an audit in April 2026, which triggered the retroactive billing.

The sudden bills caught residents off guard, and many are questioning how the charges were calculated. Stephens said the notices were shocking because they had attempted to pay previously but were told by the city they did not need to. Etzler challenged the calculation method, asking how the city arrived at the specific 36-month period at $90 per month. Both residents expressed concern about the financial burden of these unexpected expenses. Some homeowners have already paid the fees or enrolled in payment plans because they fear their sewer service could be disconnected if they do not settle the bills.

City Commissioner Juli Casale issued a statement defending the billing practices. She said when a sewer connection is identified, the city has a responsibility to determine what costs were incurred and recover the applicable fees. Casale explained that this approach ensures public utility costs are paid by those actually receiving the service rather than being shifted to taxpayers. The city maintains that the billing follows standard utility recovery procedures.

County Commissioner Marci Woodward said she has contacted the county attorney to examine the legality of the back charges and the monthly payments moving forward. The county attorney's office will investigate whether the city's retroactive billing and fee structure comply with applicable laws. For now, residents remain frustrated with the situation, hoping for answers and potential relief from the substantial bills they were not expecting to receive.

Benjamin Franklin artifact collection opening July 6 at Historical Society of Palm Beach County

One of the largest collections of Benjamin Franklin artifacts will be on display at the Historical Society of Palm Beach County in downtown West Palm Beach starting July 6. The exhibit, titled Benjamin Franklin: Creating an American Icon...

5:28 PM ET

One of the largest collections of Benjamin Franklin artifacts will be on display at the Historical Society of Palm Beach County in downtown West Palm Beach starting July 6. Zachary Jacobson of Palm Beach Gardens owns the collection with his brother, and they are displaying it to celebrate America's 250th birthday. The exhibit, titled Benjamin Franklin: Creating an American Icon, features more than 120 pieces spanning Franklin's life as a statesman, printer, and inventor.

The exhibition showcases over 50 books written by, printed by, or about Franklin, along with items from his printing business. "There's so much more to Franklin than just his years as a founding father," Jacobson said. Among the standout pieces is a grandfather clock originally from Franklin's home in Philadelphia, a plate inscribed "B. Franklin," and original medallions created when Franklin represented France during the American Revolution. The collection also includes artwork and an original portrait of the founding father. One of the most distinctive items is the glass armonica, which Franklin called his favorite invention. According to Jacobson, "You play it by wetting your fingers in a bowl, have the glass bowls spin and play it like a piano."

Jacobson's stepfather started collecting Franklin artifacts 50 years ago. Jacobson and his brother eventually purchased the remaining pieces of the collection and have continued adding to it for 15 years. "My brother and I have been collecting Franklin in tandem ever since," Jacobson said. Notable highlights include a copy of Experiments and Observations on Electricity Made at Philadelphia that was owned by Franklin's son William, a tall-case tidal clock that Franklin kept in his Philadelphia home, and a plaster model for the Libertas Americana medal, which Franklin designed and Augustin Dupre executed.

The exhibit is free and open to the public from July 6 through August at the Historical Society of Palm Beach County, located at 300 N Dixie Hwy. Jacobson hopes visitors will use these artifacts as a gateway to learn more about American history and the founding period. "Just anything you could ever imagine with Franklin's face on it, other than a $100 bill, will be here," Jacobson said, highlighting the comprehensiveness of the collection on display.

Florida begins enforcement of new terrorism designation law as DeSantis targets seven groups on day one

5:13 PM ET

Florida moved quickly to implement a new state law that grants officials the power to designate organizations as terrorist groups. On Wednesday, Governor Ron DeSantis announced the first slate of targets at a Tampa press conference with FDLE Commissioner Mark Glass and Statewide Prosecutor Brad McVay, just hours after the statute took effect on July 1. The governor said the state is preparing to designate the Muslim Brotherhood, the Council on American-Islamic Relations (CAIR), Antifa, cartel-linked groups including Tren de Aragua and Sinaloa-related organizations, and Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps. "The law takes effect on July 1, today, and so we are not going to waste any time," DeSantis said.

HB 1471, officially titled "Systems of Law and Terrorist Organizations," authorizes Florida's Chief of Domestic Security to recommend designations for domestic or foreign terrorist groups. Those recommendations must then be approved or rejected by the governor and Florida Cabinet by majority vote before they take effect. Designated organizations can face public funding restrictions, possible corporate dissolution in certain circumstances, and penalties tied to material support. According to DeSantis, the designations would affect state and local funding, colleges and universities, and Florida's K-12 school choice scholarship program.

DeSantis presented Wednesday's announcement as a continuation and strengthening of a December executive order that targeted CAIR and the Muslim Brotherhood. That earlier action was quickly challenged in federal court, and in March a judge temporarily blocked enforcement after CAIR, CAIR Florida, and civil rights groups sued, claiming it violated constitutional protections. DeSantis said the new law provides better legal footing for the state's efforts. "We did need to have more of a legal structure to be able to add teeth to these designations," he said. Glass echoed this sentiment, saying the law gives the state "more tools in the toolbox."

Attorneys representing CAIR signaled they expect another legal battle. Scott McCoy, deputy legal director of the Southern Poverty Law Center, stated that "regardless of what Gov. DeSantis calls it, the U.S. Constitution is supreme across our nation, including in Florida." He added that on CAIR's behalf, the Southern Poverty Law Center, American Civil Liberties Union, and other partners "look forward to responding in court." The designations remain unofficial until the Cabinet approves them. While no regular Cabinet meeting is scheduled for July, DeSantis indicated officials may convene in an emergency session to approve the designations.

6:23 PM ET

Volunteers in Medicine, a free medical clinic serving low-income families in Martin County, is experiencing increased demand for its services following the expiration of enhanced financial assistance through the Affordable Care Act at the beginning of 2026. The end of the enhanced subsidy program has left thousands of Floridians without health insurance coverage and struggling to access medical care. According to Dr. Jordan Bromberg, medical director of Volunteers in Medicine, more than 200,000 people across Florida did not renew their ACA health insurance this year. While only about 20 patients have so far come to the clinic after losing ACA coverage, estimates suggest as many as 500 Martin County residents may have lost access to health insurance.

Rokaya McGarry represents one of the patients who turned to the clinic after the subsidies expired. She explained that she could no longer afford health insurance once the enhanced ACA subsidies ended. McGarry said she had begun viewing medical care as a luxury rather than a necessity until her situation became critical. After losing her mother, she fell severely ill with bronchitis and felt weak. In a moment of desperation, she reached out to Volunteers in Medicine, and the clinic took over her care within a day.

Dr. Bromberg emphasized the importance of early medical screening and intervention. He noted that many health conditions can be identified early through preventive care, potentially preventing serious outcomes. Bromberg expressed concern about the number of uninsured Floridians and stressed that there are far more people in the community who need the clinic's services than have sought them out so far. He highlighted that Volunteers in Medicine has the capacity to serve more patients than currently use its services.

The clinic provides free medical care without any strings attached. To qualify for services, individuals must earn under $39,990 annually, or families of four must earn under $82,500 per year. McGarry expressed deep gratitude for the care she received, saying she could not imagine where she would be without the clinic's assistance. As the clinic continues to support Martin County residents who lost insurance coverage, Dr. Bromberg's message remains clear: the facility has room for more patients and encourages those who qualify to seek the medical care they need.

Florida opens $167 million in funding for manufactured home repairs and storm hardening

Senate Bill 594 took effect July 1, providing state affordable housing funding to help mobile and manufactured home owners pay for emergency repairs and storm retrofits.

6:54 PM ET

Nearly 140 new Florida laws took effect Wednesday, including Senate Bill 594 that opens $167 million in state affordable housing funding for mobile and manufactured home repairs. The law allows homeowners to access money for emergency repairs and storm-hardening retrofits through the State Housing Initiatives Partnership program. The change follows an investigation that found nearly 700,000 manufactured homes in Florida, about two-thirds of the state's more than 1 million mobile and manufactured homes, were built before current federal safety standards. The investigation was prompted by an EF-3 tornado that destroyed 138 homes at the Spanish Lakes Country Club Community in Fort Pierce, killing six residents.

Julie Stellatos with Martin County Human Services said an online portal will open in August, and about 75 percent of the county's annual SHIP funding, typically $1 million to $1.5 million, could help eligible homeowners. St. Lucie County is waiting on final guidance from the state before announcing its application process. Indian River County staff are taking the legislative change to the Board on August 18, with a September 30 deadline to submit their plan to the state. Okeechobee County said it is updating its local housing assistance plan to comply with the new law. The funding is first come, first served once applications open.

Alligator Alcatraz detention facility closed after one year of operation in Everglades

One year after Florida opened the controversial Alligator Alcatraz immigration detention facility in the Everglades, ICE confirmed all detainees have been removed and the facility has closed.

5:55 PM ET

Florida's Alligator Alcatraz detention facility in the Everglades closed after one year of operation on June 16, when ICE confirmed all detainees had been removed in preparation for hurricane season. President Donald Trump and Gov. Ron DeSantis announced the facility's opening on July 1, 2025, marking the first time a state had run a detention facility exclusively for immigration detainees. The state used emergency powers to commandeer an old landing strip belonging to Miami-Dade County and built the facility in eight days using emergency funding and no-bid contracts through the Florida Division of Emergency Management.

Florida's new manufactured home repair funding now available for Martin County residents

Senate Bill 594 opens up $167 million in state affordable housing funding to help mobile and manufactured home owners pay for emergency repairs and storm-hardening retrofits.

6:54 PM ET

Senate Bill 594, which took effect July 1st, opens up $167 million in state affordable housing funding to help mobile and manufactured home owners pay for emergency repairs and storm-hardening retrofits. The law was prompted by an investigation following an EF-3 tornado that destroyed 138 homes at Spanish Lakes Country Club Community in Fort Pierce and killed six residents. The investigation found nearly 700,000 mobile and manufactured homes, about two-thirds of Florida's more than 1 million total, were built before current federal safety standards and cannot withstand modern storms.

Julie Stellatos with Martin County Human Services says an online portal will open in August. About 75 percent of the county's annual SHIP funding, typically $1 million to $1.5 million, could now help eligible mobile and manufactured homeowners. Residents can fill out an online application or call the office to have one mailed. The funding is first come, first served, so Stellatos advises getting paperwork together as quickly as possible. St. Lucie County is still waiting on final state guidance before announcing its application process. Indian River County is taking the matter to the Board on August 18th, with a September 30th deadline to submit their plan to the state.

Florida Chamber poll finds 64% of state voters very proud to be American during 250th anniversary

A statewide poll shows most Floridians are proud to be Americans during the 250th anniversary of the signing of the Declaration of Independence, though responses fall sharply along partisan and age lines.

6:00 PM ET

A statewide poll conducted by the Florida Chamber of Commerce shows most Floridians are proud to be Americans during this 250th Anniversary of the signing of the Declaration of Independence. The poll found 64% of Floridians say they are "very proud" to be American, while another 12% say they are "somewhat proud" and 12% indicate they are not proud at all. Mark Wilson, President and CEO of the Florida Chamber of Commerce, said "Two hundred and fifty years ago the Founding Fathers set America on a course that has shaped the world ever since." The Chamber survey tapped 604 likely voters across the state between May 1 and May 9.

Perceptions of American pride fall along partisan lines. Some 89% of Republicans surveyed say they are very proud Americans, compared to about 34% of Democrats and 58% of no-party voters. About 25% of Democrats say they are not proud at all of being American, while only 2% of Republicans and 12% of no-party voters say the same. Responses also split significantly by age, with 71% of Floridians over 60 saying they are very proud to be American compared to just 42% under 40. On American history overall, 74% of Florida voters say Americans should be proud of the nation's history, while 12% say they feel ashamed. Republicans showed 91% support for pride in the nation's history compared to 51% of Democrats and 71% of no-party voters.

One year after Alligator Alcatraz opened and closed, questions remain about billion-dollar facility

The controversial immigration detention facility in the Everglades opened one year ago, then closed in June after costing Florida taxpayers an estimated $1 billion.

5:55 PM ET

One year ago, Florida opened Alligator Alcatraz, a detention facility in the Everglades that became one of the most controversial immigration detention camps in the country. President Donald Trump announced the opening alongside Gov. Ron DeSantis on July 1, 2025. The state used emergency powers to commandeer an old landing strip belonging to Miami-Dade County and built the facility in eight days using emergency funding and no-bid contracts through the Florida Division of Emergency Management. According to ICE's own data, about 70 percent of detainees had no criminal record, contradicting government claims that only dangerous migrants would be housed there. Immigration attorneys raised alarms about conditions inside. The American Civil Liberties Union filed a federal lawsuit over detainees' access to legal counsel, asserting attorneys could not show up without appointments and that clients were unable to place unmonitored outgoing calls to their lawyers.

The facility closed in June after detaining roughly 1,500 people at a time during its peak. DeSantis said 21,000 detainees were deported through Alligator Alcatraz. A $608 million FEMA grant approved in October was stalled, leaving Florida taxpayers to cover costs estimated at more than $1 billion. ICE confirmed on June 16 that all detainees had been removed in preparation for hurricane season. U.S. Rep. Maxwell Frost visited on May 26 and observed operations winding down. The ACLU and Friends of the Everglades lawsuits challenging the facility remain active.
