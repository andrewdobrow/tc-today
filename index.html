Riviera Beach named only Florida city to receive 2026 All-America City Award

3:42 PM ET

Riviera Beach has earned a major national recognition by becoming one of only 10 communities in the United States selected for the 2026 All-America City Award from the National Civic League. The city stands out as the sole Florida municipality to receive this honor this year. The award was announced at the National Civic League conference in Denver, where Riviera Beach leaders presented their work on strengthening community engagement and civic participation.

The All-America City Award recognizes communities that effectively bring together residents, local government, businesses and organizations to address local challenges and create meaningful, lasting change. The award has been presented annually since 1949 and is considered one of the nation's most respected civic honors. Mayor Douglas Lawson expressed pride in the recognition, saying "Little old Riviera Beach is one of the biggest, brightest, shining stars." Lawson noted that the city earned the award through long-term efforts to revitalize the community and improve residents' quality of life. Councilwoman KaShamba Miller-Anderson credited the community for the achievement, stating "This award is a tribute to the heart and determination of our residents. Every event we host, every project we complete, and every challenge we overcome happens because our community shows up for one another."

Riviera Beach's application highlighted several civic engagement programs that demonstrate the city's commitment to community participation. The Riviera Beach Youth Council involves high school students in civic leadership and advocacy work. The Citizens Leadership Academy, a civic education program, has achieved a 98 percent completion rate. The Neighborhood Reclaim Home Rehabilitation Program provides mini-grants to help longtime homeowners make necessary repairs. City leaders emphasized that these programs represent a broader commitment to treating civic engagement as an ongoing partnership between government, residents, faith groups, schools and community organizations. Miller-Anderson noted that the city is "doing a lot of innovative things to make sure that our community is able to say that they are part of what's happening here in the city."

Officials say that being named an All-America City sends a national message that Riviera Beach is a place where collaboration produces results and residents actively shape their community's future. The designation celebrates the city's progress while positioning the community for continued growth and new opportunities. Miller-Anderson added that winning this award reinforces positive momentum and brings back community pride. Other cities receiving the 2026 All-America City Award include Chelsea, Massachusetts; Franklin, Tennessee; Harlingen, Texas; Montgomery, Alabama; Morrisville, North Carolina; Norfolk, Virginia; North Charleston, South Carolina; Roanoke, Virginia; and Woodburn, Oregon.

Residents at the Colony Condos are pushing the town to reinstall a fence at the JB-7 public beach access point after the town removed it in 2023 for safety reasons. Since the fence came down, only vegetation separates the public walkway from the condo's private property, and residents say beachgoers are regularly cutting through their land. Lori Hill, a Colony resident, described the summer problem as a recurring issue where visitors park in private spots and push through bushes while carrying coolers, fishing rods, and lawn chairs. Hill said residents initially tried confronting trespassers directly but found many responses became hostile, prompting them to call police instead to handle the situation.

Not all beachgoers acknowledge a significant problem. Michael House, who regularly walks to JB-7 from a nearby public parking lot, said he has not witnessed the trespassing issues residents describe. He suggested that if there are actual property damages or littering, those would be separate concerns worth addressing. House noted that free public parking is available in the area, making it unclear why beachgoers would need to use private condo spaces.

The issue reached the Juno Beach Town Council at a recent meeting, where officials acknowledged the town holds no legal obligation to install a fence. However, Vice Mayor Pro Tem DD Halpern expressed openness to finding solutions and suggested exploring more affordable options than a full replacement fence. Halpern proposed considering a combination approach using split rail fencing paired with landscaping shrubs, which might reduce costs compared to traditional fencing alone. Hill said residents remain hopeful the town will take action to restore clear boundaries between public and private property while protecting their landscaping and parking areas.

The council scheduled the fence question for further discussion during the upcoming budget meetings on July 15, giving town officials time to assess costs and feasibility before making a decision. The dispute highlights the ongoing tension between public beach access and private property rights in the oceanside community, with the resolution potentially affecting how the town manages its beaches moving forward.

Misty Dawn White, 46, was discovered dead Monday following a fire that destroyed her and her fiancé's Hobe Sound home. Authorities with the sheriff's office have identified a possible electrical issue as the cause, though the investigation remains ongoing. The loss has left her fiancé Mark and their community searching for ways to support one another through the tragedy.

Mark learned of White's death in the immediate aftermath and has since received an outpouring of support from friends and neighbors. Nina Fenn, who knew White for eight years, received a call from Mark informing her of what had happened. "It's just a terrible shame and awful loss of life," Fenn said. The community has rallied together to launch a fundraiser aimed at helping Mark rebuild and replace belongings lost in the flames. According to Fenn, the situation represents "completely a state of devastation" for everyone affected.

Those closest to White are remembering her for the meaningful impact she had on those around her. Fenn described White as a Christian woman who had a passion for gardening and plants and played the piano. "She was always the first to lend a hand," Fenn said. "She loves kids; she loves her kids. She loves people." White's willingness to help others and her genuine care for those in her life left a lasting impression on her friends and family.

The timing of White's death has compounded the grief experienced by those who loved her. White's birthday fell on the Fourth of July, meaning she died just days before turning 47. The loss is further difficult for Mark, who has already endured significant personal tragedy. Fenn explained that Mark lost his only child, Jacob, in circumstances she described as tragic. Despite these mounting losses, Fenn remains committed to supporting Mark however she can. "She's always been a good soul to me. She'll always be in our hearts," Fenn said, expressing how White's memory will continue to live on in the hearts of those who knew her.

The Saint Andrew's Scots football program is working through the summer with a clear objective: winning a state championship during the 2026 fall season. Coming off a 10-2 regular season record and a district title last year, the team's early playoff exit has fueled their ambitions for greater success. Head football coach Jimmy Robertson noted that the team's senior class provides a strong foundation for achieving that goal.

Robertson highlighted that most of the senior players are four-year veterans who came up through the middle school program. "Most of these seniors are four-year seniors. I mean, if not the majority of them came from the middle school as well. So they know how things are run. They had a lot of success in their career, and hopefully their senior year is the best season that we've had," Robertson said. This experience and continuity gives the program confidence heading into the new season.

The Scots are taking a strategic approach to improvement by scheduling tougher competition this fall. Senior Jack Williams explained the reasoning behind this decision, saying the team wants to reach the next level competitively. "We knew that we wanted to take our program to the next level. So we really have a challenging schedule for this year because we wanna compete. That's what we wanna do and we wanna take that next level, and it's by playing against really good teams that we do that and attract new people," Williams said. The more demanding slate of opponents is designed both to test the team and to put them on the map for recruits.

The players are laser-focused on one specific measure of success this season: playoff advancement. Senior Jack Whelche stressed that regular season accomplishments mean little without postseason success. "A better year is just gonna be going farther in the playoffs. Obviously we wanna go undefeated in the season, but that doesn't matter if we keep losing in the second round of the playoffs. We just gotta go farther than that, that's all that matters," Whelche said. To prepare for this push, the team is using summer workouts as both a training tool and a source of motivation. Senior Edmyer St. Jean emphasized how the daily grind contributes to the team's overall goal. "It just adds up every day as something like to look forward to. Work harder so we can see the results when we come off the season positive and break our record," St. Jean said.

12:34 PM ET

Florida's State Board of Education has voted to move forward with rules that would prevent undocumented students from enrolling in the state's 28 community colleges and adult education programs. The restrictions would also apply to GED preparation courses, which many students use as a pathway to college or workforce training. This action builds on an earlier decision by Florida's university system Board of Governors, which approved plans to ban undocumented students from the state's more competitive public universities starting in the 2027-28 academic year. Students already enrolled would not be affected by the university proposal.

Those supporting the restrictions contend that taxpayer-funded educational institutions should serve only students who are legally present in the United States. However, immigrant advocates and students have objected strongly to the policy. Alexander Vallejos, a student with DACA protection, argued that Florida is closing opportunities for young people who have lived their entire lives in the state and attended Florida schools. Vallejos stated, "We're just asking for an opportunity to earn our future through education. Because when Dreamers succeed, Florida succeeds." These new restrictions follow Florida's previous decision to eliminate in-state tuition rates for many undocumented students, including those protected under DACA.

The same week, Governor Ron DeSantis began implementing a new state law that enables Florida to designate organizations as terrorist groups. The law, which took effect July 1, allows the governor and Florida Cabinet to make designations based on recommendations from state domestic security officials. DeSantis announced that Florida will seek to designate the Council on American-Islamic Relations, the Muslim Brotherhood, and Antifa, in addition to more than 90 organizations already designated as terrorist groups by the federal government. These federal designations include cartel-connected groups and Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps.

If the Cabinet approves these designations, the state could block taxpayer funding, contracts, and other public support for the listed organizations. The law also imposes penalties for providing material support to designated groups and could allow for dissolution of some organizations under state law. CAIR and civil rights groups immediately filed lawsuits in federal court challenging the designations as unconstitutional. According to a statement from the Southern Poverty Law Center's deputy legal director Scott McCoy, "Gov. DeSantis does not have the power to unilaterally brand an organization with this vilifying designation and punish those who support it without due process or judicial review." Supporters of the law argue that Florida is taking necessary steps to combat extremism and protect public institutions.
