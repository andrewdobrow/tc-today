5:18 PM ET

On August 12, 2025, a minivan carrying three workers became wedged underneath a semi-truck on the Florida Turnpike in St. Lucie County. The truck's driver, Harjinder Singh, allegedly made an illegal U-turn in a clearly marked "No U-turn" zone. Faniola Joseph, 37, Herby Dufresne, 30, and Rodrigue Dor, 54, all died in the crash. Joseph had been traveling south from Indiana to purchase goods for her retail business. Now her daughter, 22-year-old Angeline Dowdian, has filed a wrongful death lawsuit against Singh, his employer White Hawk Carriers, and freight broker C.H. Robinson.

The lawsuit alleges that C.H. Robinson knowingly hired White Hawk Carriers despite the company's serious safety violations. Attorney Dax Bello of Stuart represents Angeline and argues this was not a random accident but a foreseeable consequence of unsafe practices. "This is not an accident. It's a predictable end that came at the cost of three lives," Bello said. The case gains strength from a May 14 U.S. Supreme Court decision in Montgomery v. Caribbean Transport, which clarified that federal law does not shield freight brokers from state negligence claims when they place unsafe drivers on the road. Transportation expert James Lewis has described White Hawk's violations as "very egregious." Bello emphasizes that brokers and carriers have a responsibility to check publicly available safety records before putting drivers on the road.

Angeline lived abroad and had not seen her mother in six years, though they spoke daily through video calls. When the crash occurred, she missed a call from her mother. "She sent me a voice message. She said, 'Angeline, where are you?' This is the first message I go to every morning," Angeline said. She described losing her mother as losing everything. Her father had rejected her at birth, making her mother both parents to her. Visa restrictions have prevented her from visiting her mother's grave since the crash.

Singh faces criminal traffic homicide charges in Florida. White Hawk Carriers has since shut down, though Bello warned the company may reopen under a different name, a practice he said is common in the industry. Angeline's co-counsel Mark Brumer added that litigation will address not only who caused the crash but who could have prevented it. While no legal victory will undo her pain, Angeline hopes the lawsuit prevents other families from suffering as hers has. "I don't want nobody suffer like me," she said. "She worked her whole life so I could have a better one, and died in such a terrible way."

The St. Lucie County Aquarium in Fort Pierce is home to a unique marine exhibit that stands out among public aquariums across the country. The Smithsonian Marine Ecosystems Exhibit operates with a distinctive philosophy that sets it apart from typical facilities. According to Manager Bill Hoffman, the aquarium has been displaying fully live, complex models of ecosystems since 1980 under Smithsonian direction. "We feel like we're the best kept secret on the Treasure Coast, and we don't want to be," Hoffman said, noting that the facility houses more species than many larger exhibits. The partnership between the St. Lucie County Aquarium and the Smithsonian Marine Station allows visitors to explore Florida's most important coastal environments in one location.

The centerpiece of the exhibit is the Atlantic coral reef tank, a large display that recreates one of Florida's most significant coastal ecosystems. Beyond the coral reef, visitors can observe live models of seagrass beds, mangroves, and hardbottom communities. Each habitat demonstrates the complex relationships between different organisms that depend on one another for survival. Even smaller creatures play crucial roles in these ecosystems, as evidenced by displays featuring animals like a four-inch barracuda. These exhibits work together to tell the story of Florida's marine biodiversity and the delicate balance required to maintain healthy ocean environments.

One of the most popular attractions is the touch tank, where guests can handle unusual marine species under the supervision of trained staff members. According to exhibit educator Chelsie Corcoran, visitors often encounter animals they have never seen before in this hands-on area. This interactive experience helps connect people directly to marine life and creates memorable educational moments. The facility plans to expand its programming as part of its 25th anniversary celebration happening later this year.

The aquarium's mission extends beyond entertainment and education. By showcasing these live ecosystems, the facility aims to raise public awareness about threats to ocean health while inspiring conservation efforts. Manager Hoffman emphasized that stable ecosystems depend on countless small organisms working together, a message reinforced through the live displays throughout the exhibit. The Smithsonian Marine Ecosystems Exhibit is located at 420 Seaway Drive in Fort Pierce and welcomes visitors year-round. For more information, visit sms.si.edu or check their Smithsonian Marine Station social media channels.

This represents an even larger field than the last time the district held an open race, when 10 Republican candidates qualified. Donalds himself emerged from that previous primary before winning the 2020 General Election.

The Republican field includes two former members of Congress who previously served other states: Madison Cawthorn and Chris Collins. Major self-funders in the race are Jim Oberweis, a dairy farm owner and former Illinois lawmaker, and Jim Schwartzel, president of Sun Broadcasting. Other notable candidates include author and health activist Catalina Lauf and former congressional staffer Ola Hawatmeh. January 6 insurrectionist John Strand rounds out the GOP Primary ballot.

The Democratic Primary features three candidates. Howard Sapp, a former state House candidate and air traffic controller, and Victor Arias, managing attorney of Arias Law Firm, qualified for the ballot. Robert Neeld, who has previously challenged former GOP Representatives Connie Mack IV and Rooney, also made the Democratic Primary ballot shortly before the deadline. Additionally, Seth Haskin qualified to run without party affiliation and will face whichever candidates prevail in both primaries during the general election in November. A write-in candidate, Alexandra Zakhvatayev, also qualified in the race.

The winner of the Republican Primary in August will enter the general election as a heavy favorite in this heavily Republican district. Under the new congressional map enacted by Governor Ron DeSantis, more than 69 percent of voters in the district voted for Republican Donald Trump in the 2024 presidential election, while just 34 percent backed Democrat Kamala Harris. This significant Republican advantage suggests the August primary winner will likely win the seat in November's general election.

County officials and community leaders expressed alarm over the potential financial impact, with projections...

4:40 PM ET

Indian River County leaders gathered at the Heritage Center in Vero Beach on Friday morning to address concerns about a property tax amendment heading to voters this November. The amendment would increase the homestead exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. County officials and community leaders expressed alarm over the potential financial impact, with projections showing the county could lose approximately $50 million in funding by 2028 from its roughly $597 million budget.

Laura Moss, vice chair of the Indian River County Commission, organized the meeting to bring together neighbors and community leaders to discuss the amendment's implications. Moss criticized the state's approach, saying "The state is going to dictate to the local governments how we fund whatever it is we fund. Every county is different. We fund what the community wants." Lance Lunceford, president of the Indian River County Tax Payers Association, called the amendment "an unsound, unplanned piece of public policy" and emphasized that the projected revenue loss represents a substantial portion of the county's annual budget. For a smaller county like Indian River, he noted, such a reduction could prove devastating to essential services.

The potential impact on emergency services is particularly concerning. Christen Brewer, president of Indian River Firefighters, stated that fire rescue could lose between $15 to $20 million from its $70 million budget, since about 79.1 percent of the department's funding comes from ad valorem taxes. The budget shortfall could prevent the development of three projected fire stations. Lunceford emphasized there is no realistic way to absorb such a large loss without cutting into law enforcement and first responder services that the community depends on.

Rather than a genuine tax cut for residents, Lunceford characterized the amendment as a tax shift that would place financial burdens elsewhere. To compensate for lost homestead exemption revenue, the county may need to increase taxes on non-homesteaded and commercial properties or raise impact fees and utility fees. Lunceford warned that increasing taxes on businesses ultimately increases costs for everyone, as higher business taxes translate to higher prices for consumers. The amendment represents what Moss called "a war on local government" by the state, highlighting the tension between Tallahassee's policy decisions and local governments' ability to fund the services their communities require.
