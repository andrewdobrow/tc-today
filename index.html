Florida Supreme Court adopts new AI courtroom rules requiring lawyers to verify citations amid rampant misuse

Starting June 15, Florida attorneys and people representing themselves in court must certify that any citations they submit are accurate and actually exist. The Florida Supreme Court recently established these new rules to combat what legal professionals describe as a serious and growing problem: artificial intelligence generating false legal citations and fabricated evidence i...

Yesterday, 12:04 PM ET

Starting June 15, Florida attorneys and people representing themselves in court must certify that any citations they submit are accurate and actually exist. The Florida Supreme Court recently established these new rules to combat what legal professionals describe as a serious and growing problem: artificial intelligence generating false legal citations and fabricated evidence in courtrooms. Violations could result in sanctions ranging from public reprimand to fines and suspension from practice.

Federal Judge William Matthewman, chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has worked to address AI misuse in his courtroom after witnessing the problem firsthand. He expressed concern that the situation will worsen before it improves, though he believes the Florida Supreme Court's action will help the state. Criminal defense attorney Paul Figueroa in Tampa, who recently began using AI to enhance his practice, emphasized that the technology is unreliable. He explained that AI can generate quotes appearing directly relevant to a case that actually do not appear in the cited cases. When asked if the new rules are sufficient, Figueroa said they go far enough for current circumstances.

One notable case involved a Polk County attorney representing the school district in litigation over school bus cameras. A judge discovered the attorney had included fabricated facts and cited cases that do not exist in a motion to dismiss. The attorney apologized and acknowledged using ChatGPT as a research and drafting tool, admitting there was no valid reason for failing to verify the information before filing. The school district stated it takes accuracy and legal integrity seriously and is reviewing whether to continue using this attorney for future work.

The problems with AI-generated false evidence have become widespread enough to demand the court's action. Earlier this year, a St. Petersburg woman created a fake photo using AI, claiming a man had robbed and assaulted her, though she later confessed to police that she fabricated the allegation. Judge Matthewman warned both lawyers and self-represented litigants to carefully verify everything they file with the court. He cautioned that failing to do so could result in serious consequences.

Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties enforce fertilizer ban starting Monday

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Beginning Monday, June 30, residents across Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop applying fertilizers containing phosphorus or nitrogen to their lawns and landscaping. The seasonal restriction takes effect as the rainy season begins and will remain in place through the end of summer. The ban aims to curb nutrient-rich runoff that flows into local waterways, where excess nutrients trigger harmful algae blooms in areas like the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River.

Environmental Resources staff in St. Lucie County explain the reasoning behind the restriction. According to Jordan Skaggs with the county's Environmental Resources division, these algae blooms pose multiple threats to the aquatic ecosystem. The dense growth can block sunlight from reaching underwater vegetation that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. Beyond ecological concerns, harmful algae blooms become particularly dangerous at high concentrations, as they can produce toxins harmful to human health, animal populations, and the overall health of waterways and surrounding ecosystems.

Rather than leaving lawns and gardens without nutrient support during the ban period, University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences offers a practical alternative. Grantly Ricketts, director of the St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective way to add nutrients to landscaping without violating fertilizer restrictions. Composting requires minimal equipment and can work in residential settings using something as simple as a small trash can, making it accessible to most homeowners. This method allows residents to recycle organic materials while still maintaining healthy gardens and landscaping throughout the restricted months.

The fertilizer ban applies broadly across all three counties, affecting every residential property with a lawn or landscaping. Local officials emphasize that composting provides both an environmentally responsible solution and a practical way to care for yards during the rainy season. By switching to composting methods now, residents can help reduce the nutrient pollution flowing into waterways while maintaining their landscapes naturally.

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen. The restriction applies to lawn and landscaping maintenance throughout the rainy season. According to Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, the ban targets a specific environmental problem: nutrient-rich runoff that flows into local waterways during heavy rains and contributes to algae blooms.

The algae blooms pose serious threats to the region's aquatic ecosystems and public health. When excessive nutrients from fertilizer wash into waterways like the Indian River lagoon and St. Lucie River, they fuel rapid algal growth that can damage natural habitats. Skaggs explained that these blooms "can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife." More concerning are harmful algae blooms, which at high concentrations can produce toxins that threaten human health, wildlife populations, and the broader ecosystem.

The county is promoting composting as a practical alternative for residents who want to maintain their gardens and landscaping during the ban period. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective way to add nutrients to landscapes without relying on chemical fertilizers. According to Ricketts, composting "can be like a small trash can" and represents "the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape."

This fertilizer ban reflects growing concerns about water quality in Florida's coastal areas. By reducing the nutrients entering waterways during the wet season, county officials hope to minimize the conditions that trigger algae blooms. Residents should plan ahead to transition their landscaping practices before June 1, incorporating compost-based approaches to maintain healthy plants and lawns throughout the rainy season without violating the new regulations.

Renters voiced particular skepticism about the proposal's benefits. A renter along Clematis Avenue in downtown West Palm Beach expressed doubt that savings would extend to the rental community. A few minutes ago

Florida residents will have the opportunity to vote in November on a constitutional amendment that would significantly expand homestead property tax exemptions. The proposal has advanced through both chambers of the state legislature with substantial support, though local government officials have raised concerns about the financial consequences. The Senate approved the measure 30-9, following a House vote of 75-26. The amendment is a priority initiative for Governor Ron DeSantis.

The constitutional amendment would increase homestead exemptions to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028 if voters approve it by the required 60% margin. The measure would apply to homeowners who own primary residences in Florida by the end of this year. Future increases would be indexed to the Consumer Price Index. However, school property taxes would remain exempt from these higher limits and would continue to be subject only to the current $25,000 exemption threshold. This distinction means that while homeowners would see relief on county and municipal taxes, their school tax obligations would not increase at the same rate.

County and city officials have warned that the amendment could create significant budget challenges for local governments, though the exact financial impact is still being calculated. The uncertainty about potential revenue losses has left questions about where budget cuts might occur if voters approve the measure. Some local leaders have suggested that reduced funding could affect municipal services. A similar proposal to expand homestead exemptions failed when presented to voters in 2018, making this the latest attempt to provide tax relief to Florida homeowners. The tradeoff between providing tax relief and maintaining funding for local services remains a central point of debate surrounding the amendment.

The measure represents an ongoing effort to reduce property tax burdens on Florida residents who own their primary homes. As the legislature has moved the amendment forward, it has secured backing from both Republican and Democratic lawmakers, indicating broader support for some form of property tax relief. However, the path forward depends on whether Florida voters believe the benefits of expanded homestead exemptions outweigh concerns about reduced local government revenue. When the constitutional amendment appears on the November ballot, voters will make the final determination about whether to implement these significant changes to Florida's property tax system.

Jupiter Island erosion threatens to split barrier island at Peck Lake, state report warns

The 11.5-mile shoreline has deteriorated to a critical point where ocean water could potentially break through at Peck Lake and split the barrier island in half. This threat exten...

Yesterday, 4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report documents severe erosion along nearly the entire coastline of Jupiter Island, threatening infrastructure, ecosystems, and historical sites. The 11.5-mile shoreline has deteriorated to a critical point where ocean water could potentially break through at Peck Lake and split the barrier island in half. This threat extends from Blowing Rocks Preserve, where private development already faces risk, to Peck Lake, where a breach would convert the thin strip of land into an open inlet. A similar breach occurred in the early 1960s during a storm before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers restored the area by adding sand.

According to Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society, another breach would damage fragile ecosystems that depend on a precise balance between fresh and saltwater environments. He warned that increasing storm surge frequency could create additional breaks in coastal protection. The consequences would extend far beyond the immediate shoreline. The report identifies multiple sites at risk, including the Joseph Reed Mound, an archaeological site estimated to be 3,000 to 4,000 years old that has already lost half its structure to erosion. Conservation lands like Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park also face danger. Perry noted that more than 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit these areas. A second state assessment warns that erosion threatens mangrove swamps that provide critical habitat and coastal protection, while rising water levels could displace entire species populations on the island.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field acknowledged the seriousness of the situation while describing the town's ongoing response. The community continues fighting erosion through regular maintenance projects. In April 2025, Jupiter Island completed a major beach renourishment effort that deposited sand equivalent to filling nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Field stressed that oceanfront homes currently face no immediate danger only because of this continuous work. "We are just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over," Field said. "We will continue to do that as long as there's money to do it."

The town invests approximately 3 million dollars annually in beach renourishment from local taxpayer funds. However, this funding stream remains uncertain. If the island were to breach, repairs would likely require substantial state and federal investment. Field cautioned that "if funding ever goes away for beach renourishment in Florida, the coastline of Florida is going to change very quickly, and not in a way that anybody's particularly happy with." The recent state assessment may help Jupiter Island compete more effectively for state and federal funding to address erosion and protect its communities and natural resources.

According to Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, the ban exists to combat a significant environmental problem. When fertilizers wash into rivers and lagoons during heavy rains, they can trigger algae blooms that damage aquatic ecosystems. The Indian River lagoon and St. Lucie River are particularly vulnerable to these blooms, which can disrupt the delicate balance of these waterways and harm the species that depend on them.

The consequences of harmful algae blooms extend beyond environmental damage. Skaggs explained that these blooms can shade out plants that fish and wildlife rely on for survival. In some cases, when algae blooms reach high concentrations, they produce toxins that pose serious threats. These toxins can endanger human health, harm wildlife populations, and degrade the overall health of the ecosystem.

For residents looking to maintain their gardens and landscaping during the ban period, Grantly Ricketts, director of University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective alternative. Ricketts noted that composting can be as simple as using a small container and represents one of the easiest and most practical methods available to add necessary nutrients to landscaping without relying on commercial fertilizers. By turning kitchen scraps and yard waste into compost, homeowners can continue to support their plants while protecting the region's waterways from nutrient pollution during the critical summer months.
