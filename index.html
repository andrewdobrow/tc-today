5:36 PM ET

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties will need to stop using fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen on their lawns and landscaping. This restriction will remain in effect through September 30, covering the region's peak rainy season.

The fertilizer ban is designed to minimize nutrient-rich runoff that flows into local waterways during heavy rainfall. According to Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, this runoff can trigger harmful algae blooms in sensitive ecosystems. The Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River are particularly at risk from these blooms, which can degrade water quality and damage aquatic habitats. Skaggs explained that these algae blooms "can shade out beneficial plants to fish and wildlife." In addition to ecological damage, the blooms can pose direct threats to human health. "At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health," Skaggs said.

For those looking to maintain their gardens and landscaping during the ban, composting offers a practical alternative. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective solution. According to Ricketts, composting "can be like a small trash can" and serves as "the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape." By composting organic waste, residents can create natural fertilizer for their plants without relying on chemical products containing harmful nutrients.

The ban reflects growing concerns about water quality in Florida's coastal areas. By reducing the amount of nitrogen and phosphorus entering waterways, local authorities hope to prevent the conditions that lead to algal blooms during the wet season. Residents are encouraged to plan ahead and switch to alternative methods like composting before June 1 takes effect. For more information about composting techniques or other approved landscaping practices during the ban period, residents can contact their county's environmental resources office or local extension services.

12:04 PM ET

The Florida Supreme Court has implemented new rules to combat the growing misuse of artificial intelligence in legal proceedings. The regulations, which take effect June 15, require attorneys and self-represented litigants to certify that all materials they cite in court are accurate and actually exist. Violations could result in sanctions ranging from public reprimand to fines and suspension from practice.

Federal Judge William Matthewman, chief magistrate for the Southern District of Florida, has witnessed the problem firsthand in his own courtroom. "It's really a problem," Matthewman said, noting that he expects conditions to worsen before improving. "Unfortunately, I see it getting worse before it gets better. I think the Florida Supreme Court's order will help in Florida, but I unfortunately have seen over the last couple of years that it is growing and growing and growing." His concerns stem from documented cases where AI-generated false information has infiltrated court filings. In Polk County, an attorney representing the school district filed a motion to dismiss containing fabricated facts and citations to cases that do not exist. The attorney acknowledged using ChatGPT as a research aid but stated "there was no excuse" for failing to verify the information before filing. The school district said it takes accuracy seriously and is reviewing whether the attorney will remain eligible for future contracted work.

Criminal defense attorney Paul Figueroa from Tampa, who uses AI to improve his workflow, emphasizes that the technology is unreliable. "It can be something as simple as it gives a quote that's directly on point to my case, and you go, all right, this is brilliant, this is what I've been looking for, I could have searched 300 cases and not come up with anything better than this, and then you realize that's not in the case," Figueroa said. When asked if the new rules are sufficient, he responded affirmatively, saying "I do. I think it goes far enough for what we know now."

Beyond courtroom documents, AI-generated evidence has caused serious consequences for individuals. A St. Petersburg woman fabricated an AI-generated photo of a man she claimed had burglarized and raped her. She later admitted the image was fake, telling police "I lied about it." She was not cleared of charges. In another case highlighted by investigative reporter Katie LaGrone, a fake AI-generated text was used to help land a Florida woman in jail, though she was eventually cleared of all charges. Matthewman has issued a clear warning to legal professionals: "I would caution lawyers and parties representing themselves to make sure everything you file in court is accurate, because if not, you could find yourself in hot water."

Florida state officials are closely monitoring drought conditions across the state as they consider whether to implement a statewide fireworks ban for Independence Day. The potential restriction would affect the 250th anniversary celebration of American independence. According to the Florida Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services, this year's drought conditions are even more severe than those experienced in 1998, when Florida last imposed a statewide fireworks ban.

The drought situation has worsened significantly throughout Florida. Every part of the state is experiencing at least abnormally dry conditions, with certain regions facing particularly dire circumstances. Palm Beach County and the Treasure Coast are among the areas dealing with moderate to extreme drought conditions. In May, Florida Agriculture Commissioner Wilton Simpson issued an early warning about the possibility of implementing a ban. Simpson emphasized the potential danger of the situation, explaining that if drought conditions persist, hundreds of thousands of people setting off fireworks in a single evening could ignite fires across the entire state simultaneously. Despite recognizing the historic importance of the 250th anniversary celebration, Simpson encouraged residents to explore alternative ways to mark the occasion.

Fireworks retailers have already noticed increased business activity from customers attempting to purchase supplies before any potential restrictions take effect. At Sky King Fireworks in West Palm Beach, staff reported a surge in customer traffic as residents shop ahead of the holiday. Bill Murphy, who works at the store, explained that they maintain careful monitoring of drought conditions to assess whether the ground has adequate moisture. However, Murphy expressed cautious optimism about this year's celebrations, noting that weather forecasts indicate significant rainfall is expected in the coming weeks.

The Florida Department of Agriculture has indicated that implementing a fireworks ban would represent a last resort measure. State officials will make their final determination about the ban as July Fourth approaches, basing their decision on how drought conditions develop over the coming weeks. The outcome will have widespread implications, potentially affecting both municipal fireworks displays and personal fireworks use throughout the state. As Florida residents anticipate one of the most significant Independence Day anniversaries in American history, they await the department's decision on whether traditional fireworks celebrations will be permitted this year.

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report has documented severe erosion across nearly Jupiter Island's entire 11.5-mile coastline, raising concerns that the ocean could break through at Peck Lake and potentially split the island in half. The threat extends from Blowing Rocks Preserve, where private development faces immediate risk, to Peck Lake, where a breakthrough could convert a narrow strip of land into an open inlet. This area experienced a similar breach during a storm in the early 1960s, which the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers subsequently filled with sand. Mark Perry, with the Florida Oceanographic Society, warned that another breach could damage ecosystems that rely on specific balances between freshwater and saltwater. "We're going to probably have more storm surges that would create more of these breaks," Perry said. "That makes all these coastal marshes and everything vulnerable."

The erosion threatens far more than the physical landscape. Archaeological sites and wildlife refuges across the island face significant danger. The Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old archaeological site, has already lost half its area to erosion. Conservation areas including Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park are all at risk. Perry noted that over 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit these areas. A separate state assessment identified additional concerns about mangrove swamps, which are critical for habitat and coastal protection, and warned that rising water levels could displace entire species from the island. "It's concerning because a lot of the conservation lands, and even historical and prehistorical sites, are at risk," Perry said.

Jupiter Island has been combating erosion through continuous maintenance efforts. In April 2025, the town completed a major beach renourishment project that added enough sand to fill nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. According to Commissioner Marshall Field, these ongoing efforts have prevented oceanfront homes from facing immediate danger. "We are just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over," Field said. "We will continue to do that as long as there's money to do it." The town currently spends approximately $3 million annually in local taxpayer dollars on beach renourishment.

However, this funding remains uncertain. Field warned that a major breach could require substantial state and federal investment for repairs. "We would have cataclysmic erosion if we didn't get federal funding and state funding," he said. Field believes the recent FDEP assessment will help Jupiter Island compete more effectively for state and federal funding to address erosion. The fundamental challenge remains clear: maintaining the island requires consistent financial support that is far from guaranteed.

Starting June 1, residents in Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties will no longer be able to use fertilizers containing phosphorus or nitrogen on their lawns and landscaping. The restriction is designed to address a growing environmental concern during the region's rainy season, when nutrient-rich runoff flows into local waterways and can trigger harmful algae blooms.

According to Jordan Skaggs with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, the ban targets a specific problem. When excess nutrients from fertilizers wash into water systems during heavy rains, they fuel the rapid growth of algae blooms that can threaten the health of ecosystems. Two major waterways in the region are particularly vulnerable to these blooms: the Indian River lagoon and the St. Lucie River. Skaggs explained that these blooms create multiple problems for aquatic environments. They can block out sunlight needed by beneficial plants that fish and wildlife depend on for food and habitat. In some cases, when algae blooms reach high densities, they produce toxins that can harm human health, damage wildlife populations, and degrade overall ecosystem quality.

The ban affects anyone maintaining outdoor spaces in these three counties, but experts point to practical alternatives. Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension, recommends composting as an effective substitute. Composting allows property owners to recycle organic waste while creating nutrient-rich material for their gardens and landscaping. Ricketts describes the process as straightforward and practical. A compost bin functions much like a small trash can where homeowners can deposit yard waste and other organic materials. Over time, these materials break down naturally and create a nutrient-dense amendment that can be worked into soil to support plant growth without the environmental risks associated with synthetic fertilizers.

For residents accustomed to using commercial fertilizer products, the transition may require adjusting their lawn care routines. However, the environmental benefits of the ban are significant. By reducing the flow of excess nutrients into waterways during Florida's wet season, the counties hope to prevent the algae blooms that have increasingly plagued the region's lagoons and rivers. Property owners who want to maintain healthy landscapes during the ban period should explore composting options or consult local extension services for additional guidance on alternative soil amendment methods.
