Yesterday, 6:33 PM ET

The Supreme Court has sided with the Trump administration in eliminating Temporary Protected Status for thousands of people from Haiti and Syria, leaving hundreds of thousands suddenly at risk of deportation. Many of these individuals live in South Florida. Temporary Protected Status allows foreign citizens already in the United States to remain, work, and support their families while their home countries face crises such as natural disasters, political unrest, or violence. However, this protection is now ending for Haitian and Syrian nationals.

In Riviera Beach, Stecko Dulcio has operated a Haitian restaurant for five and a half years. Though he is now a U.S. citizen and therefore secure in his status, Dulcio expressed deep concern for friends who hold TPS. "That's going to be a disaster," he said when asked about the impact on the Haitian community. "Not only are their lives in danger, and even if they go they don't get killed, but they're going to have a miserable life, because there's no work, no job, and they lose everything they have." Dulcio also noted that gangs have taken over Haiti, controlling houses and transportation while fueling widespread violence. He urged prayer and government intervention to help those affected.

Attorney Byrnes Guillaume, who is Haitian and represents many people facing TPS elimination, explained the practical consequences. "It means that they have no status. It means that if you have a job you no longer are able to work. It means that you were once legal and now you have to go in the shadow," Guillaume said. For families with American-born children, the situation becomes even more complex, forcing parents to choose between leaving their children behind or taking them to a country they've never known. "I do think lives will be at risk," Guillaume warned. The ruling affects roughly 350,000 Haitians and 6,000 Syrians nationwide, though no timeline for deportations has been set.

Florida hosts the largest Haitian immigrant population in the United States. Palm Beach County ranks third nationally and statewide, with 68,400 Haitian immigrants, behind Broward County's 86,100 and Miami-Dade County's 78,800. The Trump administration argues that TPS was designed only as temporary relief during crises and that conditions in Haiti and Syria have improved sufficiently to no longer justify protection. Supporters of TPS strongly dispute this claim. Since January of last year, the Department of Homeland Security has ended protections for people from more than 13 countries.

The sentences were handed down consecutively, with Elder receiving 17 years for fleeing the scene and just over 12 years for DUI manslaughter, totaling 29 years and one-and-a-half months. According to Florida Highway Patrol, Drummond was riding his bicycle eastbound on A1A near South Sewall's Point Road at approximately 6:45 a.m. when the collision occurred. Drummond was in the bike lane at the time Elder's 2019 BMW 535I crossed into the lane and struck him from behind. Following the impact, Elder did not remain at the scene but instead left the area. She was later located and arrested at her residence.

Drummond was a prominent figure in the Stuart community who worked as a publisher of a local magazine. He was widely recognized for his contributions to the area and for serving as a mentor and spiritual leader to many residents. His death marked a significant loss for those who knew him and worked with him throughout his career in the community.

The sentencing brings closure to a case that raised concerns about road safety and the consequences of impaired driving. The consecutive sentences reflect the severity of Elder's actions, including both the decision to drive under the influence and her decision to flee the scene rather than provide assistance or remain to face immediate accountability. The lifetime revocation of Elder's driving privileges ensures she will not be able to operate a motor vehicle following her release from prison.

Yesterday, 6:09 PM ET

Josney Peroza, owner of Ukelele Foods Venezuelan food truck in Delray Beach, learned of a devastating disaster when news alerts reported two powerful earthquakes striking his home country. The quakes measured 7.2 and 7.5 magnitudes and struck seconds apart. When images began circulating, Peroza saw a video showing the building where his mother lived in the coastal state of La Guaira had collapsed. Communication in Venezuela became nearly impossible after the disaster, with power cut and connections blocked. Hours of uncertainty followed before Peroza received a call at 3 a.m. confirming his mother was alive but trapped under the rubble. Relatives and neighbors worked with bare hands to free her from the collapsed concrete and metal structure.

Peroza's mother suffered a broken hip and requires surgery after being rescued. She also lost her home in the collapse. Speaking about the broader humanitarian crisis, Peroza explained that survivors in La Guaira face a catastrophic situation with no power, no water, and no shelter. He emphasized that Venezuela lacks the infrastructure and equipment needed to respond to a disaster of this magnitude. The country's buildings are constructed primarily of concrete and metal, making rescue efforts extremely difficult without proper tools. Officials confirmed nearly 200 people have died and at least 1,000 others have been injured. Peroza stressed the urgent need for international assistance, and the United States has pledged to send rescue crews to help.

When asked if his mother's survival was a miracle, Peroza answered without hesitation, saying it absolutely was. He reflected on the scale of destruction, noting the building had 12 to 15 floors and likely housed about 100 families. He emphasized that his mother's rescue proves other survivors may still be found alive under the rubble if rapid international response occurs. Peroza is concerned about what comes next for survivors, as there will be no commercial activity, employment, or clear plans for food and shelter in the coming days.

Despite the crisis, Peroza said he does not plan to travel to Venezuela. Instead, he believes he can help his mother and his country more effectively from Florida, where he can work to secure her a new house and continue operating his food truck. He described his business as more than a place to eat, calling it "a cultural Venezuelan experience that we share with the community" or "Venezuela without a passport." Peroza plans to continue serving customers while telling his country's story and working to draw international attention to Venezuela's urgent humanitarian needs. He urged those waiting for news from loved ones to trust in hope and the possibility that rescue teams will arrive in time.

1:35 PM ET

Governor Ron DeSantis has signed legislation allowing Lourdes Latour and her husband, Edward, to receive the final portion of an $800,000 settlement with Miami-Dade County. The accident that prompted the settlement occurred on January 5, 2017, when a malfunctioning gate arm struck Latour as she exited the Gables by the Sea community on her bicycle, throwing her to the ground and causing permanent nerve damage. The county had accepted liability for the incident, but state law limited what the government entity could initially pay.

The couple filed suit against Miami-Dade in 2018, alleging the county was negligent in owning, operating and maintaining the gate. A jury determined in January 2025 that Miami-Dade bore 100 percent responsibility for the accident and awarded damages totaling more than $4.9 million. The damages covered long-term pain and suffering, physical impairment, disfigurement, mental anguish, loss of enjoyment, loss of companionship and service. However, Florida's sovereign immunity law capped government liability at $200,000 per person and $300,000 per incident at the time of the settlement. This limitation forced both sides to settle for $800,000 instead of the jury award, with the county paying $300,000 directly and the remaining $500,000 contingent on legislative approval through a claims bill.

HB 6515, sponsored by Coral Gables Republican Representative Demi Busatta and carried in the Senate by Sarasota Republican Senator Joe Gruters, is a special type of legislation designed to compensate individuals for losses caused by the negligence or error of public agencies. The bill passed with overwhelming support, with all lawmakers voting in favor except Pensacola Republican Senator Don Gaetz. DeSantis signed it as part of a batch of 15 bills on Thursday, and it took effect immediately upon his signature. It was one of five claims bills the Legislature passed during the 2025 session.

Separately, state lawmakers approved raising the caps under Florida's sovereign immunity law to $500,000 per person and $1 million per incident. That measure, sponsored by Sarasota Republican Representative Fiona McFarland, has been sent to DeSantis but has not yet received his signature. It remains unclear whether the governor will sign or veto that legislation, which would establish new limits for future government liability cases.

3:18 PM ET

A Supreme Court decision allowing President Donald Trump's administration to end temporary protected status for thousands of Syrians and Haitians could disproportionately impact Florida, which is home to more TPS holders than any other state. According to the National Immigration Forum, around 404,000 people with temporary protected status live in Florida, compared to Texas with around 147,000. The 6-3 ruling issued Thursday in Mullin v. addressed roughly 350,000 Haitians and 4,000 Syrians who had been living in the U.S. legally before the Department of Homeland Security revoked their status. About 49% of Haitians living in the U.S. reside in Florida, according to the Migration Policy Institute. Miami-Dade Democrat Frederica Wilson immediately introduced the Haitian Refugee Immigration Fairness Act to restore protections for those from the violence-struck island, saying constituents are already receiving emails telling them to self-deport or face arrest, deportation, and permanent exile. Original co-sponsors include Democratic Reps. Maxwell Frost and Debbie Wasserman Schultz, though the legislation lacks Republican co-sponsors.

The ruling could have broader impacts beyond Haitians. Venezuelans represent the greatest number of TPS holders in the country, with about 605,000 of the 1.3 million people currently in the U.S., and 49% of Venezuelans choosing Florida as home. Miami-Dade County is also one of the top five counties nationally for Central Americans with TPS, including 170,000 El Salvadorans, 51,000 Hondurans, and about 3,000 Nicaraguans. The House voted in April on legislation to extend TPS for Haitians, which passed with support from 10 GOP lawmakers, including South Florida Republicans Mario Diaz-Balart, Carlos Gimenez, and Maria Elvira Salazar. Any Senate path forward faces a 60-vote threshold requirement.

DeSantis approves safeguards against wrongful child removal for rare medical conditions

Parents of children with rare medical conditions that can manifest in easy bruising and broken bones now have safeguards against wrongful child removal under legislation Gov. Ron DeSantis approved this week.

A few minutes ago

Gov. Ron DeSantis signed HB 47, which revises Florida's protective investigation procedures for children diagnosed with rare medical conditions whose symptoms can resemble physical abuse. The legislation applies to Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, rickets, osteogenesis imperfecta and vitamin D deficiency, which cause fragile bones, easy bruising and other injuries that can be mistaken for abuse. Broward County Democratic Sen. Barbara Sharief, Weston Democratic Rep. Robin Bartleman and Shalimar Republican Rep. Patt Maney championed the measure, which cleared both chambers of the Legislature unanimously in March. Under the bill, the Department of Children and Families can delay immediately referring certain criminal allegations to law enforcement if a parent reports one of those diagnoses or requests a qualifying medical exam. The law, effective July 1, requires child protection teams to consult with qualified physicians or advanced practice registered nurses and mandates that DCF request relevant medical records. It also gives parents the right to seek a second medical opinion within a set time frame.

The legislation was inspired by Michael and Tasha Patterson, who lost custody of their 8-week-old twin sons in 2022 due to a previously undiagnosed case of Ehlers-Danlos. During committee hearings, other families shared their experiences. Sarah Mischler recounted fighting for 11 months and spending more than $100,000 to keep her 7-month-old son after a misdiagnosis of shaken baby syndrome. Sharief said 118 Floridians claiming improper child removal due to incorrect abuse suspicions expressed written support for the legislation.

About 4 million of those trips will be by car, with experts recommending tire safety checks and avoiding afternoon and evening travel on major routes including I-95.

6:49 AM ET

AAA expects nearly 4.6 million Floridians to travel at least 50 miles from home this Fourth of July weekend, up about 3 percent or roughly 146,000 more travelers than in 2025. About 4 million of those trips will be by car, with another 330,000 travelers expected to fly and roughly 204,000 traveling by bus, train, cruise, or other means. Florida is one of the top domestic travel destinations, meaning heavy inbound traffic will add to congestion on major routes including I-4, I-95, and roads leading to beaches and theme parks. AAA and INRIX data suggest drivers avoid afternoon and evening travel, particularly around July 2 when heading out and July 5 and 6 on the return, with early mornings generally being the best times to drive.

On a typical July day, Florida sees an average of 7 to 8 traffic fatalities, but the Fourth of July itself historically brings a 44 percent spike, with an average of about 11 fatalities on that day alone based on data from 2017 through 2021. Past holiday weekends have seen 25 to 32 or more deaths statewide, with many linked to alcohol and non-seatbelt use. The Florida Highwaymen Museum, which opened this year in Fort Pierce, is a featured stop on the America 250 Road Trip, as announced by the city on its Facebook page this week. The museum celebrates one of America's most iconic art movements and honors 26 Black artists who began painting in the 1950s. These artists created bold, colorful landscapes of Florida's untouched beauty and sold them along the roadside when segregation kept them out of traditional galleries. Located at 1234 Avenue D, the museum is the nation's only one with exhibitions that include paintings from each of the 26 renowned Highwaymen artists.

The museum operates on a seasonal schedule. Summer hours from June 16 to July 31 are Saturday and Sunday from noon to 4 p.m. The museum will be closed in August and reopen in September with a new exhibit. Fall and spring hours of operation beginning September 1 through June 15 will be expanded.

Upon arriving in Florida, finding a local soccer team became a top priority, leading him to the L'Union Fait La Force Training Center, a premier soccer development program run by former Haitian National Team members.

The training center is led by former Haiti national team captain Jean-Marc Alexandre, an Atlantic High School and Lynn University graduate who was a member of Real Salt Lake's 2009 MLS Cup-winning team. Alexandre explained that the camp's purpose is to provide these young players with a pathway to the youth national team since they no longer live in Haiti. The center hosted a clinic from June 25-28 teaching top-level Haitian soccer players, including Chevry and Luis Etienne. For Chevry, the opportunities extend beyond the clinic itself. "I will take this to a high level, and my goal is to play in the World Cup and probably win it," he said.

Lucky Shuck in Jupiter draws diners arriving by car or boat

WPTV's Walter Allen visited the Lucky Shuck in Jupiter, a waterfront restaurant founded by a professional athlete and known for its oyster selection.

12:25 PM ET

WPTV Shining a Light reporter TA Walker joined the discovery of Lucky Shuck, a Jupiter restaurant founded by a professional athlete that welcomes diners arriving by car or boat. For Walker, the restaurant holds special significance as the location of his first date with his now-husband, Charlie. Chef John, who has been at the restaurant since its inception, guided visitors through the oyster selection, which features about five different types daily. The house specialty is Jemma's Pearls, named in honor of Joe Namath's granddaughter and given to the football legend for his birthday.

The Lucky Shuck processes approximately 40 cases of oysters per week, with each case containing roughly 100 oysters, totaling 4,000 oysters weekly. Diners can enjoy fresh oysters while watching vessels drift by on Jupiter's waterways. The combination of fresh seafood and waterfront views creates the dining experience that draws people to this South Florida establishment, whether they arrive by vehicle or watercraft.
