The seasonal ba...

Yesterday, 5:36 PM ET

Starting June 1, residents across Indian River, Martin and St. Lucie counties must stop applying fertilizers that contain phosphorus or nitrogen to their lawns and landscaping. The restriction runs through the rainy season and aims to prevent nutrient-laden runoff from flowing into local waterways, where it can trigger algae blooms that harm aquatic ecosystems.

The seasonal ban targets a significant environmental problem in the region. Jordan Skaggs, who works with Environmental Resources in St. Lucie County, explained that the measure protects waterways including the Indian River Lagoon and St. Lucie River. When algae blooms form in these bodies of water, they can block sunlight from reaching beneficial aquatic plants that fish and wildlife depend on for survival. In severe cases, harmful algae blooms produce toxins with serious consequences. According to Skaggs, "At certain densities, harmful algae blooms can release toxins that can affect human health, wildlife health and overall ecosystem health."

Residents looking for alternatives during the ban should consider composting, according to Grantly Ricketts, director of the University of Florida's Institute of Food and Agricultural Sciences St. Lucie County Extension. Composting provides an effective way to add necessary nutrients back into garden soil and landscaping without relying on commercial fertilizers. Ricketts described the approach as simple and accessible. "It can be like a small trash can," Ricketts said. "That is the easiest and most practical way or means to add nutrients to your landscape."

The prohibition covers all fertilizer applications containing the restricted nutrients, regardless of where residents plan to use them on their properties. Those who fail to comply with the seasonal restriction face potential penalties enforced by local code enforcement officials. Property owners should review their landscaping maintenance plans now to ensure they have alternatives in place before the June 1 deadline.

The constitutional amendment is a major priority of Governor Ron DeSantis. If approved by at least 60% of voters, the amendment would increase homestead exemptions for primary residences to $...

6:00 AM ET

Floridians will vote in November on whether to dramatically reduce homestead property taxes after the Senate voted 30-9 to place HJR 1-F on the ballot. The House passed the measure 75-26. The constitutional amendment is a major priority of Governor Ron DeSantis.

If approved by at least 60% of voters, the amendment would increase homestead exemptions for primary residences to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028 for those who own homes in the state by the end of this year. Further increases would be tied to the Consumer Price Index. School taxes would not be subject to the increased exemption limit and would remain at $25,000.

The measure could put money in homeowners' pockets across the state. County and city officials have warned the effort could wreak havoc on local budgets as reduced property tax revenue affects municipal services and infrastructure funding.

Floridians who own primary residences as of the end of 2026 would be eligible for the expanded exemption beginning in 2027. The vote will take place on the November ballot, requiring support from at least 60% of voters to amend the state constitution. The measure represents one of the most significant property tax reduction proposals to reach Florida voters in recent years.

Yesterday, 4:42 PM ET

A 2025 Florida Department of Environmental Protection report warns that Jupiter Island faces severe coastal erosion across its entire 11.5-mile shoreline. The most concerning threat is at Peck Lake, where a breach could split the island in half by turning a thin strip of land into an open inlet. This risk extends from Blowing Rocks Preserve to Peck Lake. A storm previously breached Peck Lake in the early 1960s before the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers filled it back in with sand, demonstrating that such events are not merely theoretical concerns.

The erosion threatens far more than just the physical coastline. Roads, buildings, and drainage systems face damage, but the report also documents risks to natural habitats, wildlife, and archaeological sites. Among the most significant is the Joseph Reed Mound, a 3,000- to 4,000-year-old site that has already lost half its area to erosion. Other endangered locations include Blowing Rocks Preserve, Hobe Sound Wildlife Refuge, and St Lucie Inlet Preserve State Park. Mark Perry with the Florida Oceanographic Society noted that more than 33 threatened and endangered species inhabit these areas. Perry explained that future breaches could destabilize ecosystems dependent on a careful balance between fresh and saltwater environments. He emphasized the urgency of the situation, stating "the last 15 years, it's been rising at a faster rate" regarding sea level rise since the 1950s.

Jupiter Island Commissioner Marshall Field said the town actively combats erosion through ongoing maintenance efforts. In April 2025, the town completed a major beach renourishment project that deposited enough sand to fill nearly 400 Olympic-sized swimming pools. Field stressed that oceanfront homes remain safe only because of these continuous interventions. He described the approach as "just reapplying the Band-Aid over and over and over," acknowledging it is a temporary solution. The town invests approximately $3 million in annual taxpayer funding toward beach renourishment.

However, this funding strategy depends on continued financial support. If a breach occurs, Field warned that repairs would require substantial state and federal investment. He cautioned that "if funding ever goes away for beach renourishment in Florida, the coastline of Florida is going to change very quickly, and not in a way that anybody's particularly happy with." Field noted that the recent FDEP assessment should help Jupiter Island compete more effectively for state and federal funding to address erosion. The report positions the island to make a stronger case for future financial support to mitigate these mounting coastal threats.

Okeechobee County woman sues Campbell Soup and Walmart over alleged worms in SpaghettiOs

Mary Hubbard of Okeechobee County filed a lawsuit claiming she and her daughter found live worms in SpaghettiOs purchased at the Walmart Supercenter on South Parrott Avenue in Okeechobee.

Yesterday, 1:19 PM ET

Mary Hubbard of Okeechobee County claims she and her daughter developed parasitic infections and other medical complications after discovering live worms in SpaghettiOs in June 2024. She purchased the product at the Walmart Supercenter on South Parrott Avenue in Okeechobee. According to the complaint, Hubbard found what appeared to be worms or parasites actively moving within the food and documented the contamination in multiple videos that clearly depict worm-like organisms in the product.

The lawsuit names Campbell Soup Co. and Walmart as defendants and seeks $50,000 in damages. The complaint alleges both companies failed to properly inspect the product. Campbell Soup Co. stated the claims lack merit, while Walmart said it is reviewing the court filing.

35 foster youth graduate at ChildNet celebration in Palm Beach County

35 students walked across the stage at ChildNet's annual graduation celebration at Palm Beach State College. The nonprofit supports foster kids in Broward and Palm Beach County through education, adoptions and independent living resources.

9:32 AM ET

Thirty-five foster youth and young adults graduated at ChildNet's annual celebration at Palm Beach State College, marking a milestone for the Broward and Palm Beach County nonprofit. Among the graduates were Sabrina Diaz and Brian Saintfort, both of whom lost a parent and turned to ChildNet for support. The organization equipped them with the resources they needed to move forward. Diaz shared a message for others in similar situations, saying, "Belief that you're going to be big one day and just put effort into it, never give up on school and never give up on dreams. If you like push past with the pain and just keep trying hard." Saintfort echoed her message, stating, "Believe that you'll make it, then you're gonna make it and stuff."

Larry N. Rein, CEO of ChildNet, reflected on the evening's significance. "This evening is the fruition of great work by everyone in the community, including case workers at ChildNet, DCF and especially the kids who have worked so hard. We are to celebrate them," Rein said. Diaz is already enrolled in pre-med classes at Palm Beach State College, where she hopes to become a doctor. Saintfort plans to pursue a vocational program to become a plumber.
