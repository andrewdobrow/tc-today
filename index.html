Governor Ron DeSantis rejected legislation that would have increased damage caps for people harmed by government negligence for the first time in 16 years. The bill passed with strong bipartisan support in March before DeSantis vetoed it, saying that raising the caps would encourage more frivolous lawsuits. "When you raise the cap, you are going to generate more unmeritorious lawsuits," he said. The measure would have increased sovereign immunity caps from $200,000 per person and $300,000 per incident to $350,000 per person and $500,000 per incident, effective October 1.

HB 145, sponsored by Sarasota Republican Rep. Fiona McFarland with a Senate companion bill from Lake Mary Republican Sen. Jason Brodeur, faced fierce opposition from more than 240 lobbyists representing counties, cities and their agencies, including public hospitals. These government entities worried about budget shortfalls under a proposed property tax amendment. The bill would have allowed local governments to voluntarily settle claims exceeding the new caps without requiring legislative approval. The original proposal included higher caps of $500,000 per person and $1 million per incident, which were reduced in a last-minute compromise. Sovereign immunity is a centuries-old legal standard that shields state and local governments from settling lawsuits without their consent. Currently, victims seeking compensation above the caps must obtain approval through special claims bills passed by the Legislature.

McFarland has argued that the current system creates unequal protection under the law. She contends that harmed individuals face an "incredibly arduous and difficult process" with vastly different outcomes depending on whether the responsible party is a public or private entity. "If you or I or one of our loved ones is hit by a truck, we have to pay attention to what's on the side of that truck," she said. "Depending on whether there's a corporate logo or a government seal, we have different access to monetary wholeness." State lawmakers consider roughly a dozen claims bills annually benefiting people injured through government carelessness or wrongly incarcerated exonerees. This year, just over one-third of 14 such measures passed both chambers, including cases involving a man paralyzed in a police van, a motorist injured in a crash with a state tractor, and a young girl hurt due to Department of Children and Families negligence.

DeSantis defended his veto by noting the current system works because some claims bills clear the Legislature yearly. "I signed a number of claims bills this year," he said. "People have gotten millions of dollars with that." However, the Florida Justice Association criticized the veto as a gift to insurance companies and a disservice to Florida families. The group noted that under the current system, victims often wait years or over a decade for compensation. McFarland and Brodeur, both seeking reelection, have not indicated whether they will refile the legislation for the 2027 session.

The School District of Palm Beach County earned an "A" rating from the state for the third consecutive year, with 143 district-operated schools, or 84%, receiving either an "A" or "B" grade this year. That represents an increase from 120 schools with those top ratings in the previous year. The district also achieved a record graduation rate of 93.5%, and 86% of seniors completed at least one college-level course through Advanced Placement, International Baccalaureate, Cambridge AICE, or Dual Enrollment programs. Additionally, 144 recent graduates earned an associate degree alongside their high school diploma.

S.D. Spady Elementary School in Delray Beach experienced a dramatic turnaround, jumping from a "C" rating last year to an "A" this year. Principal Rona Tata, who has served 15 years in that role after five years as assistant principal, credited the improvement to curriculum changes that began three years ago. The school modified its early childhood program, which serves students starting at age 3 through kindergarten. Many of those students are now in third grade and performing at higher levels. Spady was one of four district schools to make the jump from a "C" to an "A" this year. The other three schools include Dwight D. Eisenhower K-8, Glade View Elementary, and West Gate Elementary.

Serena Williams withdrew from her Wimbledon doubles match with sister Venus on Saturday due to a right knee injury sustained during her singles return earlier in the week. The 44-year-old lost to 20-year-old Maya Joint of Australia 6-3, 6-7 (6), 6-3 on Tuesday. In an Instagram post, Serena said she was "heartbroken to have to withdraw from doubles," noting that coming back to compete and playing alongside Venus "meant the world to me." She explained that while she "did everything I could," her knee "just isn't ready to compete." The post included images of four syringes containing fluid drained from her knee after the singles match, along with a video showing her walking with strapping on her right leg and what appeared to be one of her daughters holding a cane for her.

The Williams sisters, with Venus at age 46, were scheduled to face Camila Osorio and Solana Sierra in a first-round doubles match that had been pushed back to allow Serena more recovery time. However, Serena hinted at a potential return, saying "stay tuned to a city near you" as the U.S. Open approaches. Venus competed in mixed doubles on Friday with Kevin Krawietz, losing in straight sets to Tereza Mihalikova and Lloyd Glasspool. The Williams sisters are six-time Wimbledon women's doubles champions.

Jun 30, 11:20 AM ET

Loggerhead Marinelife Center in Juno Beach is offering educational and interactive programs during peak sea turtle nesting season through September. Guests can participate in guided Turtle Walks, the Hatchling Discovery Program, Hatchling Releases, Tidal Tots and Plastic Free July Community Action Day. The programs are designed to give visitors an opportunity to learn more about sea turtle conservation along one of the world's busiest sea turtle nesting beaches.

The center's Turtle Walk program allows participants to observe the nesting process while learning about sea turtle biology, conservation and ongoing research efforts. Educational demonstrations may include the use of red-light headlamps, amber lighting, night scopes and research tag scanning equipment. During nesting season, Loggerhead Marinelife Center's education and research departments work together to collect and analyze data related to nesting activity and hatchling success. Sea turtle nesting season in Florida typically runs from March through October. Conservation groups encourage beachgoers to turn off beachfront lighting at night, properly dispose of trash and avoid disturbing nesting turtles or hatchlings. Registration information and program details are available through Loggerhead Marinelife Center.

Yesterday, 2:48 PM ET

The Peacock House Art Foundation is establishing Fort Pierce as a vibrant artistic hub by transforming the historic Crain Estate into a cultural center that welcomes both local and international artists. The nonprofit organization honors the legacy of Jaxson and Mary Crain, former residents of the property whose personal touches remain visible throughout the facility. Jaxson Crain's trade as a tile worker is evident in his craftsmanship, which covers floors, walls, and ceilings. According to Krystal Masters, the art director, the estate has retained approximately 90 percent of its original character since the Crains lived there. The foundation has deliberately avoided commercializing the space, choosing instead to preserve its authentic residential charm. Visitors can experience the estate's lush gardens and distinctive architecture through guided tours offered every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday.

The Crains were world travelers who visited nine times and filled their Florida home with unique treasures from around the globe. Among their most notable acquisitions was a gazebo shipped from India, which features small peacock details painted into its design. The estate's distinctive smoking and bourbon room reflects the era with wood paneling and a bathroom decorated in masculine tones. A pristine stained glass window connects this room to a bar on the front patio. The foundation's commitment to preservation means these spaces remain largely unchanged from their original condition.

The property takes its name from the peacocks that have inhabited it for decades. The Crains introduced seven peacocks to Fort Pierce during the 1970s, and the population has since grown to over 100 birds. Among them is Bob, a beloved peacock believed to be 60 years old and recognizable by his distinctive limp. Bob receives special treatment at the estate, complete with his own feeding bowls and daily hand-feeding by staff. Tour guides highlight the memorable moments when children feed peacocks directly from their hands. The estate's animal history also includes a colorful anecdote about a baby monkey that Jaxson Crain reportedly brought home from South America, smuggling it in his pocket.

The foundation operates an artist-in-residency program that attracts creators from around the world, providing simple yet elegant accommodations on the grounds. The on-site art lab offers classes for children and adults of all skill levels, with Masters leading one-on-one sessions tailored for complete beginners. Students work with various mediums including sculpting and watercolor painting, often completing finished pieces during their first class. "The majority of my classes are one on one classes you come in with zero experience and you walk out with a piece of art and it makes you feel good," Masters explains. For her, art serves as therapeutic practice that helps people destress and disconnect from digital distractions.
