## St. Lucie County

### Source pool

1. Police search for missing man last seen Monday morning in Port St. Lucie - WPEC

### Variant A

**Hero:** St. Lucie County sheriff restricts license plate reader use to forcible felonies, missing-person cases
**Urgency:** 7

The St. Lucie County Sheriff's Office is imposing new restrictions on the use of automated license plate reader technology, limiting its use to forcible felony investigations and cases involving missing or endangered people.

 Sheriff Richard Del Toro Jr. announced the changes Monday, Sept. 1, as concerns continue to be raised across Florida about privacy, data sharing, government oversight and access to information collected by automated license plate readers, including Flock cameras.

 The Sheriff's Office said it will comply with Florida Department of Transportation regulations and directives governing license plate reader cameras located within state rights-of-way. Any agency-operated Flock cameras located on state roads or within FDOT-controlled rights-of-way will be removed in accordance with those requirements.

 Del Toro said his office will go further by placing additional restrictions on how the technology can be used within St. Lucie County.

 Under the new policy, use of the license plate reader system will require prior approval from the undersheriff. Searches will be limited to investigations involving forcible felonies as defined under Florida law and investigations involving missing or endangered people.

 The Sheriff's Office said the system will not be used for general investigative surveillance, intelligence gathering unrelated to an authorized investigation or routine investigative inquiries.

 Access will also be restricted to personnel holding the rank of lieutenant or above. Use of the system will be subject to documentation, review and accountability requirements, according to the Sheriff's Office.

 Del Toro acknowledged that license plate reader technology has proved useful in serious investigations. The agency cited previous cases in which the technology helped authorities locate and arrest a homicide suspect, arrest a counterfeit currency suspect and safely locate a missing person.

 At the same time, the sheriff said access to powerful investigative technology should not mean unrestricted use.

 "Every search should have a legitimate public safety purpose, a defined approval process, and appropriate accountability," Del Toro said in the statement.

 The Sheriff's Office said it will continue reviewing Florida law, FDOT requirements, data-sharing practices, privacy protections and safeguards surrounding license plate reader technology. Policies will be revised when necessary to comply with the law and strengthen oversight, according to the agency.

 Del Toro also called for a broader statewide framework governing the technology. He said the Sheriff's Office supports working with the Florida Sheriffs Association, county leaders and state policymakers to establish consistent standards covering authorized uses, access, data retention, information sharing, auditing requirements, accountability and consequences for misuse.

 The sheriff said those safeguards should balance individual privacy and civil liberties with law enforcement's ability to use license plate reader technology during serious public safety investigations.
